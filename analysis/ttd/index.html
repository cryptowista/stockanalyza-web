In August The Trade Desk told investors to expect third quarter revenue of at least $650 million, 12% less than a year earlier, and the stock lost 24% in one evening. On November 9 it reported $736.0 million. That is still 0.5% below the same quarter of 2025, which trade press called the first year-on-year decline since the company listed in 2016, and it was enough for reports of a 21% to 23% jump in after-hours trading. A shrinking quarter that beats a forecast for a much worse one is a good night. It is not yet a growth story.

My Base value is $17.38, 16.4% above the $14.93 close. The Bear value is $8.64 and the Bull value is $29.59. I assign no probabilities to the cases. The verdict is Hold: the price is below my Base, but not by the 42.5% margin a business with falling revenue, a heavy stock pay bill and a controlling founder requires. New money waits for $9.99 or less.

Valuation route: an asset-light, cash-rich advertising technology platform whose revenue is a fee on the ad spend it handles. The primary method is a ten-year free cash flow model with stock pay counted as a cost; the cross-check is a forward multiple of enterprise value to 2028 operating profit after stock pay. Both use the same net cash and diluted share count. Values are present fair-value estimates in US dollars, not 12-month price targets.

What The Trade Desk is

The Trade Desk, based in Ventura, California, runs a self-service platform that advertising agencies and brands use to buy digital ads across connected TV, mobile, display, audio and digital out-of-home. It does not own websites or apps and does not sell its own ad space, which is the whole pitch: it is the buyer's side of the market, independent of Google, Meta and Amazon. Customers spent about $13.4 billion through the platform in 2025, and The Trade Desk keeps a fee on that spend, which it reports as revenue. Video, including connected TV, is a little over half of the business; international is about 17% of revenue.

The independence is also the problem. Amazon offers its own buying platform at much lower fees and tracking data cited by trade press has its share of programmatic spend rising from under 10% to just under 20% in about 15 months. Large agency groups have pushed back on fees: Publicis told clients to stop transacting in March 2026 after an audit of fee practices, and the two sides settled in June. Consumer packaged goods and autos, about a quarter of the business together, cut budgets this year under commodity costs and tariffs.

Against the August guide, revenue beat by $86 million and adjusted EBITDA by about $106 million; analysts had expected about $651 million of revenue and $0.25 of adjusted earnings a share. GAAP operating income includes $44.3 million of restructuring charges for cutting about 15% of the workforce in September; without them it was $136.9 million, an 18.6% margin. The fourth quarter guide of at least $755 million is 10.8% below the $846.8 million of a year earlier, so the decline is not over. For the first nine months revenue is $2,139.9 million, up 4%, and I estimate about $2.92 billion for the full year, roughly the same as 2025.

The board added $731 million to the buyback, for $1 billion available, about 14% of the market value at the close. The company bought back $241.3 million of stock in the first half and, judging from the cash flow statement, nothing in the third quarter. Customer retention stayed above 95%, as it has for more than a decade, and Netflix expanded its integration so advertisers can reach Netflix audiences through the Kokai platform from November. I wrote this analysis from the press release; the conference call came afterwards and is not reflected here.

Which numbers to trust

  • Adjusted EBITDA leaves out stock pay. Stock-based compensation was $313.7 million in the first nine months, 14.7% of revenue, and $490.6 million in 2025. I count it as a cost, so my Base starts from an operating margin of about 16% for 2026 instead of the 33% adjusted EBITDA margin.
  • Free cash flow looks better than it is. Free cash flow was $565.4 million in the first nine months, but $313.7 million of the pay behind it was in shares. After stock pay it was $251.7 million, and that still includes interest on the cash and a working capital boost.
  • The GAAP tax rate is distorted. Tax was $68.4 million on $106.4 million of pre-tax profit in the third quarter, about 64%, mostly an effect of stock awards vesting far below their grant value. I use 22% on operating profit.
  • Spending on equipment rose. Capital spending and capitalized software were $238.3 million in nine months against $94.5 million of depreciation. I charge net investment of 4.5% of revenue in 2027, easing to 3% from 2030.

Cash, shares and capital returns

The Trade Desk has no debt, $1,229.3 million of cash and $420.4 million of short-term investments, $1,649.7 million in all or about $3.49 a share. A $750 million credit line is undrawn. Office leases of about $434 million stay in operating costs as rent, so I do not subtract them. I value 473 million shares: the third quarter diluted count of 470.2 million plus about 3 million for options repriced in October to $12.26, which are now in the money. The diluted count was 501.9 million in 2024 and 493.6 million in 2025, so buybacks did shrink it, but $1.38 billion of the 2025 buybacks went in at prices well above today's. The 22.5 million unvested restricted shares are covered by counting stock pay as a cost; a 2021 option for Jeff Green at $68.29 is far out of the money and left out.

Primary valuation: ten years of free cash flow

The Base has revenue up 1% in 2027, to $2.94 billion, as weak first half comparisons offset a recovery later in the year, then 7% in 2028, 8% in 2029 and 2030, fading to 3.5% by 2036. The operating margin after stock pay starts at 17%, helped by the September job cuts, and rises one point a year to 22% from 2032, close to the 20.3% reached in 2025. Tax is 22%, working capital takes 5% of each extra dollar of revenue. I discount at 10.5% a year, half a point above what I use for steadier businesses, with 3% growth after 2036.

History and earnings check

Revenue grew from $2,444.8 million in 2024 to $2,896.3 million in 2025, 18%, and will be roughly flat in 2026. The GAAP operating margin went from 17.5% to 20.3% and falls to about 16% this year before restructuring. Free cash flow was $632.4 million in 2024 and $783.0 million in 2025, but after stock pay only $137.7 million and $292.4 million, $0.59 a share last year. My Base has $0.54 a share for 2027, which is below the roughly $0.70 annual pace of the first nine months; that is a deliberate choice, because those months include interest income and lower investment than I assume.

On the past 12 months the free cash flow yield is 12.0% before stock pay and 6.0% after it, and the stock trades at about 11 times adjusted earnings of roughly $1.30 a share for 2026 (nine months of $0.95 plus my fourth quarter estimate). The adjusted figure excludes stock pay, so it flatters. The stock traded near $140 at the end of 2024; the 52-week range is $11.62 to $45.50.

Valuation triangulation

  • Base value: $17.38, inside the method range of $17.34 to $17.43.

How the buy price is set

Confidence is C, with a margin of safety of 42.5%. The reporting is clean and the cash is real; the grade is lower because the company gives no outlook beyond the next quarter, its fee is under pressure from cheaper competitors, and my cross-check is not independent of the cash flow model. That gives a maximum buy price of $9.99, below the 52-week low of $11.62.

Four points of operating margin move the Base to $20.60 or $14.15, the largest single effect at 18.6% of the value. 15% more or less revenue in every year gives $19.41 or $15.35, a point of discount rate $18.80 or $16.28, three turns of multiple $18.66 or $16.09, and a tax rate of 18% or 26% $17.92 or $16.83. Each extra billion of cash adds $2.11 a share, which is why the buyback matters more here than at most companies: $1 billion is 14% of the market value.

For scale, the expected return under my Base is about 18.0% a year at $9.99, 12.0% at $14.93, 10.5% at $17.38 and 8.7% at $22.

Dividend

The Trade Desk pays no dividend. It returns cash through buybacks: $1.38 billion in 2025, $241.3 million in the first half of 2026, nothing in the third quarter, and $1 billion now authorized. Buybacks below my Base would add value; the 2025 ones, at much higher prices, did not.

Reasons to own The Trade Desk

  • Third quarter revenue of $736.0 million against a guide of at least $650 million, and adjusted EBITDA about $106 million above the guide.
  • $1.65 billion of net cash, no debt, and a $1 billion buyback authorization equal to about 14% of the market value.
  • Customer retention above 95% for more than a decade.
  • Connected TV partners such as Netflix, which expanded its integration from November, and international revenue growing faster than the United States.
  • An enterprise value of about 10.8 times my 2027 operating profit after stock pay, a modest price for a platform with this cash generation.

What could go wrong?

  • Fee pressure. Amazon's buying platform charges much less, and agency groups have challenged fees; a lower take on the same spend cuts revenue and margin together, as in my Bear.
  • Weak advertisers. Consumer goods and autos, about a quarter of the business, are under pressure from commodity costs and tariffs, and the fourth quarter guide is 10.8% below a year ago.
  • Stock pay and dilution. Stock pay was 14.7% of revenue this year, options were repriced to $12.26 in October, and Jeff Green received a new option on 7 million shares at $14.97 in September that vests as the stock reaches $18 to $105.
  • Governance. Green controls about half the votes through Class B shares with ten votes each. The repricing passed with 441.1 million votes for and 232.5 million against; his Class B shares carry about 421 million votes, so most other votes cast were against it.
  • Management turnover. The company has had four chief financial officers in about 14 months, and the long-time engineering lead left in 2025.

The options market expects movement. At-the-money implied volatility averaged about 77% in October, and before the results it was reported near 159%, against a historical 45%. At 77% a typical one-month move is about 22%, or about $3.30, either way. I could not get a reliable read on how much more downside protection costs than upside, so I draw no tail-risk conclusion. None of this changes the value.

Management execution

  • The third quarter beat the company's own revenue floor by 13% and the EBITDA guide by about two thirds.
  • Growth fell from 18% in 2025 to below zero in the third quarter of 2026, and the second quarter missed expectations badly.
  • The September job cuts, about 15% of the workforce, came with $44.3 million of charges and should lower costs from 2027.
  • $1.38 billion of buybacks in 2025 reduced the share count but at prices far above today's.
  • The option repricing and the new 7 million share option were pushed through against most outside shareholders. The company is on its fourth chief financial officer in about 14 months, and co-founder Dave Pickles returns to lead a new technology council.

On my ten-point management check (two points each), The Trade Desk scores 10 of 20.

What I would watch from here

The full-year report, expected in February 2027, has to show fourth quarter revenue of at least $755 million and adjusted EBITDA of at least $280 million, stock pay staying below $100 million a quarter, and some sign of how 2027 starts. The buyback is the other test: how much of the $1 billion is used, and at what price.

Green

  • Revenue growing year on year again by the first quarter of 2027.
  • Stock pay below 12% of revenue.
  • Buybacks of $200 million or more a quarter at prices below my Base.

Yellow

  • Fourth quarter revenue right at the $755 million floor.
  • Another departure in the finance or engineering leadership.
  • A further repricing or a large new grant.

Red

  • Fourth quarter revenue below $755 million.
  • An announced cut in platform fees or a large agency group moving spend away.
  • Revenue falling more than 5% in the first half of 2027.

Option prices are worth a glance before the February report: a sharp rise in the cost of downside protection would tell me the market expects a weak outlook, though it would not change the value on its own.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. A lasting cut in the fee The Trade Desk keeps on ad spend, because of Amazon or agency pressure, which leads toward the Bear.
  2. Stock pay and repricing that keep transferring value to employees and the founder, which makes my margin path too generous.
  3. A return to double-digit growth with the margin above 22%, which leads toward the Bull.

I use the regular session close of $14.93, up 15.47% on the day. Reports after the results spoke of a 21% to 23% jump in after-hours trading; that is thin trading at no official price, so it is not in the valuation. My conservative choices are stock pay as a cost, investment above depreciation and a 10.5% discount rate; my generous one is a margin that recovers to 22%. The weakest point of the model is 2027, for which the company has said nothing. The strongest bull argument is a cheap price for a debt-free business that just beat its own forecast by 13%. The strongest bear argument is that the beat was against a forecast for a 12% decline, and revenue still shrank.

Final verdict: hold at $14.93

The Trade Desk is a good platform having a bad year: no debt, $1.65 billion of cash, customers who stay, and a market that is starting to ask why it should pay a premium fee for an independent buyer. The third quarter answered part of that question. At $14.93 the stock is below my Base value, but not far enough below to pay for the uncertainty, and a 20% jump after hours would take it close to the Base.

Verdict: HOLD at $14.93. Base $17.38, 16.4% above the price, Bear $8.64, Bull $29.59, Confidence C (17.5/25). Required margin of safety 42.5%, maximum buy price $9.99.

New money waits for $9.99 or less, or for revenue growing again with stock pay falling, which would lift the Base and the confidence. For an existing holder the original cost is irrelevant; the price is between my Base and my Bear, which is a level to hold, not to add.
