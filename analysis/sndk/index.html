At $73.61 Sandisk trades at about 2.6 times what it says it will earn this quarter, annualized, and a 27% free cash flow yield for the rest of this fiscal year. That is not a bargain the market missed. It is the price of a business that earns 85% gross margin on flash memory, a commodity whose price fell 39% the last time the cycle turned. The shares are down 75% from the $291.88 record close of June 25 (the $2,335 pre-split close), and 60% from my October 13 reference of $185.92. My Base case assumes revenue peaks this year and falls by half by fiscal 2031, and it is still worth $91.64 a share, 24.5% more than the price. Nothing in my Base is rescued by the long-term contracts. The Bear, which repeats the 2023 collapse, is worth $30.96.

The Bear value is $30.96 and the Bull value is $214.02, so the price sits 138% above Bear and 66% below Bull. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 47.5%, so the maximum buy price is $48.11. The shares are 53% above that and would need to fall 34.6% to reach it.

Valuation route: a cyclical memory manufacturer, treated as an established non-financial business and valued on the free cash flow that belongs to shareholders after stock compensation, a full tax rate and capital spending. The primary method is a ten-year discounted cash flow. The cross-check is five years of cash flow plus a discounted exit at a multiple of EBITDA. Economic classification: Standard. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

Why the October 13 article shows $1,487.33

The October 13 price was correct when it was written: $1,487.33 was the October 12 close, and Sandisk was a $1,000-plus stock. On October 29 the board approved an eight-for-one forward split. Shareholders of record on November 9 received seven extra shares for each share they held after the close on November 19, and the stock traded on the new basis from November 20. Quote providers have since restated the whole price history on the new basis, so the same day now shows $185.92, which is $1,487.33 divided by 8. Every per-share figure in the old article, including its Base of $962, Bear of $464 and Bull of $1,664, is therefore eight times larger than the same figures today (they are $120.25, $58.00 and $208.00 on the new basis). The old article's values, the share count of 154 million and the buyback prices of about $1,595 are all pre-split and internally consistent. In this analysis every price, value and share count is after the split, and I show pre-split figures only where I say so.

What Sandisk is

Sandisk makes NAND flash, the memory that stores data in phones, laptops, memory cards and data center solid-state drives. It was part of Western Digital until the spin-off in February 2025. It does not run its own wafer factories: its flash comes from joint ventures with Kioxia in Japan (Flash Ventures), where it pays half of the fixed costs and guarantees half of the lease obligations. In the fiscal first quarter, which ended on October 2, revenue was $11.0 billion, up 23% from the fourth quarter and 378% from a year earlier. Datacenter revenue was $4.8 billion (up 61% in a quarter), edge products $5.8 billion and consumer products $0.4 billion.

The earnings are extraordinary by any history. The gross margin was 85.0%, against 7.1% in fiscal 2023, when revenue fell 38% in one downturn. In the last full year, fiscal 2026, free cash flow was $11.5 billion after four years that added up to a loss. Management argues that the cycle has changed, because multi-year "new business model" contracts cover about half of fiscal 2027 bits and about two-thirds of fiscal 2028 bits. The July filing put remaining contract obligations at $59.8 billion, or $91.1 billion including agreements signed after the quarter. I did not find updated contract totals in the sources I read this time.

The market has not paid for that argument. The stock lost 22.3% in one session on November 5, fell again on November 12 and 13, and dropped 8% on November 16 with no company news, in a selloff that took Micron, SK hynix and Seagate with it. Reported reasons are worry about how AI spending will be financed, Chinese memory competition and exhaustion after a very large rally. The split on November 20 changed nothing about value.

Why I use a full tax rate, capital spending at 5% of revenue and an added working-capital cost

Reported free cash flow of $6,309 million in the quarter flatters the economics in three ways. Management's own adjusted figure of $5,696 million removes $465 million of customer prepayments and $148 million of payments to the venture. Receivables grew by $1.9 billion, which is cash not yet collected. And capital spending of $54 million is the part Sandisk pays itself, while much of the real capacity spending sits in the venture. I use three adjustments. Capital spending is 5% of revenue every year ($2.6 billion in fiscal 2027, a rate that matches management's new guide and the venture's needs). Tax is 15% in fiscal 2027 and 2028 (the guide is 14.3%) and 17% after that, which is above the 13.1% reported rate in the quarter. Working capital is 15.4% of revenue, so a rising revenue consumes cash and a falling one releases it. Stock compensation of $91 million a quarter is already inside GAAP operating income, so it needs no further deduction.

One effect is worth stating. In my Base, falling revenue releases working capital in fiscal 2028 to 2031, which adds cash in years when profit is falling. I tested the Base without that benefit by raising capital spending and cutting margins, and the result still leaves the price below the Base.

Balance sheet, shares and the split

At October 2 Sandisk held $5,136 million of cash and reported no financial debt (its fiscal 2026 debt of $1.9 billion was repaid). It also held $2,289 million of marketable equity securities, mainly the stake in Nanya Technology, which produced a $513 million gain in the quarter. Against that stand customer prepayments of about $1.7 billion (the July figure of $1,242 million plus the $465 million added in the quarter) and about $3.4 billion of other liabilities that I could not identify and treat as debt-like. My net bridge from enterprise value to equity is therefore +$2.3 billion, or $1.89 a share.

Shares: 144 million were outstanding before the split at October 2 (1,152 million after). The company's own second-quarter guide uses about 151 million diluted shares (1,208 million after the split), and I use that figure. Buybacks have been large: $5.0 billion of shares at an average of about $1,562 each before the split ($195 after), which is 2.1 times my Base value. The shares have since fallen 62% below that average price, so the repurchases destroyed value for remaining holders relative to buying today, and any further buyback at $73.61 would be worth far more. I do not credit future buybacks in the value.

The split itself changes nothing about the company. Each holder got seven additional shares for each held, the share count went from about 150 million to about 1.2 billion, and market value is unaffected.

Primary valuation: ten years of owner free cash flow

The primary method discounts ten years of owner free cash flow at 10%, the framework default, and I test 9% and 11%. The valuation date is the October 2 balance sheet, so fiscal 2027 counts only the last three quarters (the first quarter's $6.3 billion is already in the cash balance), and the cash flows are discounted from the end of each period.

Base fiscal 2027 revenue is $51.1 billion: the first-quarter actual of $11.0 billion, the guided $12.4 billion midpoint for the second quarter, and growth of 8% and 7% in the next two quarters. Gross margin is 84.6%. After that the cycle turns, using the same shape as my earlier article: revenue falls 15%, 17%, 16% and 23% in fiscal 2028 to 2031, to $23.3 billion, gross margin falls from 76% to 47%, operating expenses rise from $2.9 billion to $3.2 billion, and EBITDA falls from $40.7 billion to $8.0 billion. Owner free cash flow is $24.1 billion for the last three quarters of fiscal 2027, then $24.8, $17.4, $12.6 and $6.5 billion, and about $5.5 billion to $5.9 billion a year from fiscal 2032 to 2036 as revenue settles and grows 2% a year. Terminal growth is 1.5%.

Fiscal 2027 earnings in this path are about $28.50 a share after the split, close to the second-quarter guide annualized, so the first year is not the debate. The debate is what comes after. The present value of the ten years is $69.19 a share, the terminal value adds $23.18 and the net bridge adds $1.89. The primary value is $94.27.

Terminal value share: 25% of the primary value, at a ten-year horizon, well below the 75% level where I would check that the terminal value is supported by sustainable flows. The reason is that most of the value is the next three years of cash flow.

Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 7.0 times EBITDA

The cross-check takes the same first five periods of cash flow and values the business at the end of fiscal 2031 at 7.0 times that year's EBITDA of $8.0 billion, which is $56 billion. Discounted at 10% that adds $29.30 a share, the five years of cash flow add $57.82 and the bridge adds $1.89. The secondary value is $89.02.

Why 7.0 times. By fiscal 2031 my Base has Sandisk as a settled, much smaller memory business with a 47% gross margin, falling profit for several years behind it and flat to slightly growing cash flow ahead. After tax and capital spending, 7.0 times that year's EBITDA implies cash flow shrinking about 1.6% a year forever at a 10% return. That is deliberately conservative for a business that has just shown it can make 85% margins, and it is consistent with a market that treated the 2023 trough as the normal state. Bear uses 5.0 times, a business with almost no profit, and Bull uses 8.5 times, a business that still earns very high returns in fiscal 2031.

Exit value share: 33% of the secondary value comes from the exit, at a horizon of five years, below the 85% level where I check that the exit is supported by sustainable flows.

What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $73.61 needs one of three things: owner free cash flow about 24% lower than my Base in every year, a discount rate of about 15.8% instead of 10% on the Base cash flows, or an exit multiple of about 3.3 times with the other inputs fixed (cross-check method only). No terminal growth rate gets the primary method down to the price, since even a decline of 10% a year after fiscal 2036 leaves the value above it. The market is pricing something between my Bear and Base: a steeper fall than my Base from the second half of fiscal 2027, or a lasting return to the margins of a normal memory cycle.

As a return, buying at $73.61 and holding the Base case for about five years, with the cash flows added up and an exit at 7.0 times fiscal 2031 EBITDA, gives about 10.4% a year. The same math gives -13.0% a year in Bear and 32.8% in Bull. The Base return comes almost entirely from the cash earned in the next two years.

On earnings the shares trade at 2.6 times the second-quarter guide annualized, and the growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) is not meaningful, because EPS is rising from near zero and a low PEG during a price spike supports nothing. Growth durability: cyclical rebound, with a high cyclical distortion. The free cash flow growth check says the same: free cash flow was -$120 million in fiscal 2025 and $11.5 billion in fiscal 2026, so I use no multi-year growth rate from it.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C starts at a 42.5% margin of safety. I added five points for concentration: Kioxia is the single manufacturing partner, and the ten largest customers were 44% of fiscal 2026 revenue (July filing). Cyclicality and operating leverage get no extra points, because they are charged once, in the Bear scenario. The two methods differ by 5.7%, so no divergence modifier applies. The required margin of safety is therefore 47.5%, and a 47.5% discount to the $91.64 Base gives a maximum buy price of $48.11. The framework's valuation gap alone (Base 24.5% above the price) would classify the stock as undervalued, but a Hold is right at a price above the maximum buy. A single input moving by 15% of its own size, such as the 10% discount rate moving to 11.5%, changes the Base by 6.5%, so the buy price is not hostage to one assumption, although a revenue path that falls five points faster every year lowers it by 15.6%.

What the options market implies

I did not obtain a current options chain for Sandisk, so I draw no conclusion about implied volatility or tail risk, and the options check is not supported. The price history shows how much the stock moves: one-day falls of 22.3% on November 5 and 13.5% on October 30, and a 60% drop in six weeks.

Dividend and buybacks

Sandisk pays no dividend and says buybacks are its preferred way to return cash. It repurchased $5.2 billion of shares in fiscal 2026 and $5.0 billion in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, at prices well above my value. A buyback creates value for remaining holders only when the price is below intrinsic value, and at $73.61 it does in my Base and Bull cases, though not in the Bear. I do not count future repurchases in the value per share.

Reasons to own SNDK

  • Cash that is already earned. Free cash flow was $6.3 billion in one quarter, and the guide for the next quarter is higher. My Base has $24.1 billion more arriving in the next three quarters, 27% of today's market value.
  • Contracted volume. Long-term agreements cover about half of fiscal 2027 bits and about two-thirds of fiscal 2028 bits, with remaining obligations of $91.1 billion as of the July filing.
  • A clean balance sheet. No financial debt, $5.1 billion of cash and $2.3 billion of equity investments.
  • Data center mix. Datacenter revenue was $4.8 billion, up 61% in a quarter, and management says demand from AI inference is structural.
  • A price that already assumes a hard landing. The shares trade at 2.1 times my fiscal 2027 EBITDA, and the Base case has revenue halving.

What could go wrong?

  • The price cycle. NAND is a commodity. In fiscal 2023 revenue fell 38%, gross margin fell to 7% and the company lost $2.1 billion. The Bear repeats that pattern.
  • Contract strength. The July filing showed financial guarantees of $16.5 billion, 17.6% of floor revenue, and I did not find take-or-pay terms or the price-floor mechanism. The floors were set at today's prices, so if prices fall far below them, renegotiation is more likely than payment.
  • Partner and structure. Sandisk pays half the Flash Ventures fixed costs whatever it takes, guarantees half the lease obligations, owes Kioxia $1.2 billion through 2029 and buys flash in yen.
  • Competition and demand. Chinese producers are gaining share (a CXMT announcement on October 12 started a rout in the stock), and AI data center spending is itself cyclical.
  • Cash quality. Operating cash flow was 79% of net income, receivables rose 40% in a quarter and a large part of reported cash flow comes from customer prepayments.
  • Capital allocation. $9.5 billion of buybacks in two quarters at prices around $195 a share after the split, 2.1 times my Base.

Management execution

Management has delivered what it guided since the spin-off. The first-quarter results beat the guide, the second-quarter guide is above expectations, the debt was repaid, adjusted free cash flow is disclosed beside the reported figure, and the contracts changed how the business sells. Against that, the record is only about seven quarters long, so the framework's three-year minimum is not met, the buybacks were made at prices far above my value, datacenter revenue missed some analysts' hopes, and the stock fell after every one of the last three reports despite beats. Management credibility is 11/20: good but unproven.

Debt and funding

Sandisk reports no financial debt, so I do not build a refinancing table. The funding risks are off the balance sheet: the venture guarantees, the $1.2 billion owed to Kioxia through 2029, and the cash it must put into new venture capacity if the venture's own cash flow is short. In the Bear case, with revenue of $12.4 billion in fiscal 2031 and EBITDA of $1.0 billion, free cash flow is close to zero, and the $5.1 billion of cash and $2.3 billion of securities would fund the obligations for years.

What I would watch from here

Green

  • Second-quarter revenue of $12.6 billion or more with gross margin at or above 85%, and a third-quarter guide that grows
  • Receivables days at or below 54, and adjusted free cash flow above $6 billion a quarter
  • Updated contract obligations above $91 billion, and evidence of take-or-pay terms or enforced floors
  • Monthly NAND contract prices still rising, with no sign of the Chinese supply that triggered the October rout

Yellow

  • Gross margin of 80% to 85%, or a flat third-quarter revenue guide
  • Receivables days above 54 for a second quarter, or inventory growing faster than revenue
  • Buybacks continuing at more than $5 billion a quarter while the price is above my Base
  • Capital spending above 5% of revenue without a matching rise in bits

Red

  • Revenue declining sequentially, or gross margin below 80%
  • Customers asking to renegotiate floors, or guarantees being called
  • A Kioxia or venture funding call that needs more than the Base capital spending
  • Contract prices for NAND falling two months in a row

The next review point is the fiscal second-quarter report, expected in late January 2027, with the third-quarter guide. Better results raise the Base only if they lift the cash flow beyond fiscal 2028, since the exit multiple stays at 7.0 times.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. NAND prices fall faster than my Base, which is the Bear path and a repeat of fiscal 2023.
  2. The long-term contracts are renegotiated when prices fall below the floors, which would remove the one structural reason this cycle could differ.
  3. Cash conversion keeps slipping, with receivables and inventory absorbing cash while buybacks continue at much higher prices.

A 15% move in the discount rate itself, to 11.5%, lowers it by 6.5% and to 8.5% lifts it by 8.5%, inside the 15% trigger at which I would reopen the model.
