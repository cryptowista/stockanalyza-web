Cognizant's third-quarter adjusted earnings per share rose 7.2% to $1.49. Adjusted net income, by my arithmetic, fell about 1% to $670 million from roughly $677 million a year earlier, and the whole gain came from a diluted share count that shrank 7.6%. That is the company in one line: a steady operating machine that grows profit per share by retiring shares, in an industry the market spent 2026 deciding that AI would hollow out. The stock fell 57% from $87.03 on January 14 to $37.08 on June 30, and has since rebounded 70%. At $63.19 it trades 1.5% above my Base value of $62.23.

The Bear value is $38.03 and the Bull value is $81.90, so the price sits 66% above Bear and 23% below Bull. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 42.5%, so the maximum buy price is $35.78. What Cognizant is

Cognizant sells technology services: it builds, modernizes and runs software and data systems for large companies, and it is ranked by the industry tracker SIA as the sixth-largest IT services firm in the world. It employed 359,900 people at September 30, about 32,000 of them in the U.S. Revenue for the first nine months of 2026 was $16.56 billion, and by segment the third quarter split into Financial Services (31%), Health Sciences (28%), Products and Resources (24%) and Communications, Media and Technology (17%).

The market's worry is that AI lowers the number of billable hours needed for the same project, and that AI model makers sell directly to the clients Cognizant serves. Management's answer is that more than 50% of its software development is now AI-assisted, that it has more than 8,500 AI engagements, and that it plans outcome-based pricing and AI-infused rate cards. Nearly 58% of its work is fixed-price or transaction-based, which lets a vendor keep productivity gains until the client renegotiates. Bank of America reinstated coverage at Neutral on November 19 and argued that AI is a structural, not cyclical, headwind. Deutsche Bank cut its rating to Hold on November 13, citing limited organic growth drivers for 2027.

The share price tells the story. It reached $87.03 on January 14, fell about 10% on June 18 after Accenture cut its outlook, a day after Berenberg downgraded the stock, left the Nasdaq-100 on June 22, and bottomed at $37.08 on June 30. It jumped about 10% on October 1 after Accenture's results and has traded between roughly $56 and $74 since early October.

Why I deduct stock compensation and restructuring from free cash flow

Cognizant's reported free cash flow is operating cash flow less capital spending. It adds back stock compensation, which was $193 million over the last twelve months according to StockAnalysis (a third-party figure I could not tie to the 10-Q), so I subtract it: a company that pays staff in shares and then buys the shares back is spending cash either way. Adjusted profit also leaves out restructuring, but Cognizant has booked it in three of the last four years ($229 million in 2023, $134 million in 2024, $135 million in the first nine months of 2026 under a program that totals $180 to $250 million). I treat about $100 million a year before tax as a recurring cost.

My normalized net income is $2,570 million: the adjusted guidance midpoint of $5.755 on 460 million shares is $2,647 million, less $75 million of restructuring after tax. Cash conversion has not been steady. Free cash flow was 82% of net income in 2024, 99% of net income excluding the one-time tax charge in 2025 ($2,595 million on $2,620 million), and 82% in the first nine months of 2026. Management guides 90% to 100%. I use 90%, the bottom of that range, which gives free cash flow of $2,313 million. After $193 million of stock compensation, owner free cash flow is $2,120 million, or $4.71 a share, a yield of 7.5% on today's market value.

I could not find cash taxes paid in the sources I reached, so I could not test whether the tax line flatters cash flow. I use a full 25% tax rate in the earnings figures instead. The twelve-month free cash flow of $2,363 million is 2% above my $2,313 million before stock compensation, so no transition model is needed.

Primary valuation: ten years of owner free cash flow

The primary method discounts ten years of owner free cash flow at 10%, the framework default, and I test 9% and 11%. As a sense check, with a 10-year Treasury yield near 5.15% on November 18 and a beta below 1, a CAPM-style cost of equity lands near 9.4%, so 10% is not generous. Free cash flow starts at $2,120 million, which I treat as the 2026 level, so the first discounted cash flow is a full year away. That is a slight conservatism of about a month.

Base grows owner free cash flow 3% a year for five years and 2% for the next five, which reaches $2,458 million in 2031 and $2,713 million in 2036. Terminal growth is 1.5% a year. That is roughly the 2% organic growth Deutsche Bank sees, plus small gains from price and mix, less the pressure from AI. The terminal value is 11.9 times 2036 owner free cash flow, which is a yield of 8.4% at that point. The present value of the ten years of cash flow is $33.02 a share and the terminal value adds $27.76. Net debt is zero. The primary value is $60.78.

Terminal value share: 46% of the primary value, at a ten-year horizon, below the 75% level where I would check that the terminal value is supported by sustainable flows. Buybacks are not added to the cash flows because repurchases at fair value do not change value per share, and I divide by today's diluted count.

Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 8 times operating profit

The cross-check takes the same first five years of cash flow and values the business at the end of 2031 at a multiple of normalized operating profit. Normalized adjusted operating profit for 2026 is about $3,458 million: 16.1% of the $22.10 billion guidance midpoint is $3,557 million, less $100 million of recurring restructuring. It grows 3% a year to $4,009 million in 2031. At the Base exit of 8.0 times, the business is worth $32,070 million then, and the secondary value is $63.67.

Why 8 times. Today's enterprise value is about 8.2 times normalized operating profit, and the enterprise value equals the market value because net debt is zero. After 25% tax and with owner free cash flow equal to about 82% of that after-tax profit, 8.0 times operating profit implies growth of about 2.2% a year forever at a 10% required return. That fits a firm with flat to low-single-digit organic growth, high returns on small incremental capital and a threat from AI to part of its work. Bear uses 6.0 times, which implies about zero growth forever, and Bull uses 9.5 times, which implies about 3.3%.

Exit value share: 69% of the secondary value comes from the exit, at a horizon of five years, below the 85% level where I check that the exit is supported by sustainable flows.

What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $63.19 needs one of four things: owner free cash flow and operating profit growing 3.4% a year for five years instead of 3% (the other inputs unchanged), an exit multiple of 7.9 times instead of 8, a discount rate of 9.7% instead of 10% on the primary method, or cash conversion of 93% instead of 90%. All four are small changes. The market is pricing my Base almost exactly, and that is why the verdict is Hold: the price is fair, not cheap.

As a return, buying at $63.19 and holding the Base case for five years, with the cash flows and an exit at 8.0 times operating profit, gives about 10.2% a year. That is near the 10% discount rate, as it should be for a fairly priced stock. The same math gives -2% a year in Bear and 17% in Bull.

On earnings the shares trade at 11.0 times the 2026 adjusted EPS guidance midpoint of $5.755. The growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) is about 1.2 on 9% guided EPS growth, a diagnostic only. It overstates quality, because about 7 points of the growth come from a falling share count, not from profit, and the tail of that growth is acquisition-driven. The free cash flow growth check points the same way: free cash flow was $1,827 million in 2024, $2,595 million in 2025 and $2,363 million over the last twelve months, so the jump in 2025 was a rebound from a low base and working capital, not a trend, and I use no multi-year growth rate from it.

How the buy price is set

Dividend and buybacks

Cognizant pays $0.33 a share a quarter, $1.32 a year, a yield of 2.1% at $63.19. At 450 million shares that is about $594 million, or 28% of my owner free cash flow, which is safe. The pressure is on buybacks: $1,877 million in nine months, with repurchases of 4.9 million shares at about $53 in the third quarter. At today's price those shares would cost about 19% more. The 2026 total return of capital of about $2.6 billion exceeds the free cash flow, and management has $2,080 million of authorization left.

Reasons to own CTSH

  • Margins keep expanding. Seven straight quarters of year-over-year adjusted operating margin gains, to 16.3% in the third quarter, with a Project Leap cost program targeting savings of about $200 million in 2026.
  • Financial Services and Communications are growing double digits. They are 48% of revenue, and trailing twelve-month bookings of $28.7 billion are 1.3 times revenue.
  • Cash and payouts. Owner free cash flow of about $2.1 billion after stock compensation, a 2.1% dividend, and a share count down 9% in two years.
  • A clean balance sheet. Net debt is zero after a year of buybacks and acquisitions, with a $2,080 million buyback authorization remaining.
  • The price is fair. At 11 times adjusted earnings and a 7.5% owner free cash flow yield, the market is not paying for a rebound.

What could go wrong?

  • AI deflation. If clients buy fewer hours and renegotiate rates, revenue and margin fall together. Berenberg and Bank of America make this case, and Accenture's June outlook cut showed how fast it can hit the sector.
  • Little organic growth. About 1 point of the third-quarter growth came from services Cognizant built itself. The headline depends on acquisitions and low-margin resale.
  • Debt-funded returns. Capital returns of about $2.6 billion and $1.3 billion of acquisitions exceeded free cash flow, and debt went from $576 million to $1,552 million.
  • Cash conversion. Free cash flow fell 20% in the third quarter and was 82% of net income in nine months, with days sales outstanding up 5 days to 89.
  • Health Sciences. Revenue is shrinking (-0.9%) on Medicaid and Medicare funding uncertainty, and it is 28% of revenue.
  • Restructuring that recurs. Adjusted profit leaves out charges that have appeared in three of the last four years.

Management execution

Cognizant beat its 2025 guidance across revenue, adjusted operating margin and adjusted EPS, and has expanded adjusted operating margin for seven straight quarters. The 2026 revenue guidance, however, has been trimmed from the top: 4.0% to 6.5% in February, 4.0% to 5.5% by July, and 4.0% to 4.4% in October, while the EPS guidance range was raised and then narrowed. The company retired about 9% of its diluted shares in two years. Against that, the 2026 buybacks and acquisitions were funded partly with debt, the adjusted metrics leave out recurring restructuring, and the call did not explain the drop in third-quarter free cash flow. Insiders made nine sales and no purchases in six months, per Quiver Quantitative, which is normal for a company that pays in stock but gives no signal in either direction. Management credibility is 13/20: reliable on margin and share count, weaker on organic growth and on explaining cash flow.

The three most important thesis breakers

  3. A large acquisition at a poor return, such as a deal that pays a premium for services revenue that AI is already compressing.
