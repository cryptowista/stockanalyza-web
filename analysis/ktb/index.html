What Kontoor is

Kontoor Brands, based in Greensboro, North Carolina, was split off from VF in 2019. It owns Wrangler, Lee and, since May 31, 2025, Helly Hansen, the Norwegian sport and workwear brand it bought for C$1.3 billion, or $957.5 million. In 2025 the company sold 64% of its products through U.S. wholesale, 21% through international wholesale and 15% through its own stores and websites. Walmart alone was 30% of net revenue, and the ten largest customers were 53%.

About 77% of units come from independent factories, roughly 330 of them in 28 countries with Bangladesh the largest, and 23% from Kontoor's own plants in Mexico. That matters for tariffs, which the 10-K says increased gross margin pressure in 2025 and are expected to keep doing so.

Lee is the piece going away. In May 2026 Kontoor agreed to sell it to Authentic Brands Group for $750 million in cash plus an earnout of up to $250 million over five years. Lee had 2025 revenue of $750.4 million (24% of the company) but segment profit of only $68.9 million, down 23%, so a margin of 9.2%. Wrangler earned 28.8% in the first half of 2026. The sale leaves a smaller, more profitable company, which is the whole point of it.

Look at the gap between revenue and profit. Wrangler's sales grew 2% and its segment profit grew 29%. The difference is a gross margin that rose from cost savings in the Project Jeanius program, better channel mix (direct-to-consumer up 9% in the U.S., women's up 20% in the first half) and pricing. U.S. wholesale, still the biggest channel, was flat. Management says Wrangler has gained share in core bottoms for 17 quarters in a row, citing Circana data.

Helly Hansen is the other half of the story. Its second quarter is its smallest and it still made a profit. Management put the first-half operating margin at about 7%, up roughly 600 basis points, against a long-term target in the mid-teens. Two cautions on earnings quality. The reported gross margin of 56.2% is 2.4 points above the adjusted 53.8%, and the 10-Q shows a $15.4 million gain from selling a manufacturing facility inside cost of goods sold, plus about $49 million of tariff refunds booked in the first quarter. Adjusted guidance leaves the refunds out. And for the second half, management guides only to mid-single digit growth at both brands, adding that retail partners remain cautious about inventory commitments.

History and growth diagnostics

I do not have a clean multiple history on my basis. A market data aggregator shows the shares at 13.9 times trailing GAAP earnings, within a five year range of 9.0 to 19.1 times (period-end readings) and a median of 15.0. That says the shares are neither at a trough nor at a peak, and no more.

The growth-adjusted check (PEG) is a diagnostic only. The price is 13.8 times the middle of 2026 adjusted EPS of $5.30. Reported EPS growth of 27% to 29% is not a clean growth rate, because it compares a full year of Helly Hansen with seven months, a recast base without Lee and an overhead cost that management plans to cut. Growth durability: acquisition-affected. PEG is not meaningful. Cyclical distortion: low, because the 2026 profit is not a rebound from a trough.

Free cash flow growth quality is the weak spot, as the table above shows. Reported free cash flow per share grew 12.9% a year from 2021 to 2025 (from $4.85 to $7.87 on 56.4 and 55.2 million shares outstanding), while the pre-working-capital figure grew 3.2% a year (from $4.84 to $5.48). The first is flattered by working capital, so I class the growth as working capital distorted. Forward yield on my normalized $298 million is 7.4% of the $4,048 million market value, and trailing yield on reported cash flow is 10.4%. What is the market already pricing in?

At $72.94 and 55.5 million diluted shares the market value is $4,048.2 million. Subtract my estimate of $600 million of Lee cash and add net debt of $1,060.3 million and the enterprise value of the business that remains is $4,508.5 million, which is 9.7 times my Base EBITDA. At 10 times, that price needs EBITDA of $451 million with no savings at all, or $433 million if I count half of the stranded costs. Guidance implies $465 million.

So the price does not assume a recovery. It assumes Kontoor hits its guidance and keeps a multiple a little below the middle of the band. Measured as earnings, the shares trade at 13.8 times the $5.30 midpoint, 12.5 times management's $5.80 to $5.90 bridge (about $5.85) after the stranded costs go, and 10.8 times its $6.70 to $6.80 pro forma bridge (about $6.75). My own arithmetic for that last bridge, with $400 million of buyback at $72.94 retiring 5.5 million shares, the savings and $200 million of debt repaid, gives $6.65.

How the buy price is set

A Confidence C score carries a base margin of safety of 35%. I add 5 points for customer concentration (Walmart at 30% of revenue, ten customers at 53%). No other modifier applies. Leverage would earn a reduction only after the Lee sale closes, and I do not count it before. The required MOS is 40% and the maximum buy price is $80.99 times 0.60, or $48.60. That is 33.4% below the current price and below the Bear value of $53.70. At $48.60 the equity would be worth about $2,697 million, 9.1 times my normalized free cash flow, a yield of about 11%.

I checked that no risk is counted twice. Customer concentration sits only in the margin of safety and the multiple build. Tariffs sit in the multiple build and the Bear margin. The Lee deal sits in the scenario range of $550 million to $715 million, the stranded costs in the 0, 20 and 40 million cases, and the working capital distortion in the free cash flow normalization and the Confidence score.

Dividend

Kontoor pays $0.53 a quarter, $2.12 a year, a 2.9% yield at $72.94. Cash dividends rose every year, from $95.1 million in 2021 to $116.1 million in 2025, about 5.1% a year. The payout is 40% of the adjusted EPS midpoint and 38% of 2025 free cash flow before working capital. Buybacks add to that: $25 million in 2025, $75.4 million in the first half of 2026, and $400 million coming through the accelerated repurchase when Lee closes, with $700 million of authorization left. The dividend is well covered. Dividends paid to non-U.S. holders face U.S. withholding tax, and the rate depends on your tax treaty and your broker.

Reasons to own Kontoor

  • Wrangler is a profit machine. Segment profit was $138.9 million in the second quarter, a 29.6% margin, up 29% on 2% sales growth, and management reports 17 straight quarters of share gains in core bottoms.
  • The cash is steady. Free cash flow before working capital has stayed between $259 million and $303 million for five years, on capital spending of about $30 million a year (guided), 1.1% of sales.
  • Lee sold above expectations. $750 million in cash compares with $600 million to $625 million that Wells Fargo analysts had expected, according to Retail Dive, and the cash puts net leverage near 1.0 times EBITDA at closing before the buyback (my arithmetic).
  • Helly Hansen has room. The 2030 targets are revenue above $1.1 billion, about 10% a year from $675 million, a gross margin in the mid to high 50s and an operating margin in the mid-teens. On roughly $700 million of sales (my estimate), moving the first-half margin of about 7% to 15% is $56 million of extra profit, or about $10 a share at 10 times. My Base does not count on it.
  • The share count is falling. Diluted shares were 56.1 million in 2025 and 55.5 million in the second quarter, and the $400 million buyback would retire about 5.5 million more, 9.9% of the total.

What could go wrong?

  • Thesis breaker: the Lee sale fails or pays less. The deal needs regulatory approval and can lapse after February 1, 2027. If it fails, Kontoor keeps a brand that earned $68.9 million of segment profit (8 times that is $551 million, close to my cash estimate), but the $400 million buyback and the debt reduction do not happen and the stranded cost story goes away.
  • Customer concentration. Walmart is 30% of revenue and has been 30% in 2023, 2024 and 2025. U.S. wholesale was flat in the second quarter and partners are cautious on inventory.
  • Tariffs. Guidance assumes a 15% reciprocal rate in the second half. Section 122 tariffs expired on July 24, Section 301 rates of 10% to 12.5% began in July, and imports from Mexico stay exempt under the trade agreement. About $54 million of refunds is owed to Kontoor, of which $23 million has been received. My Base excludes the rest, as management does.
  • Earnings quality. Add-backs were 28% of adjusted operating income in 2025, working capital provided $132 million of cash, and 2026 includes a facility sale gain and refunds outside the adjusted numbers. The cash flow statement end-of-period cash ($80.8 million) does not match the balance sheet ($58.5 million), and the release does not explain the gap.
  • Acquisition accounting. Goodwill ($461.1 million) and intangibles ($447.7 million) add up to $908.8 million against equity of $618.5 million. The 10-K lists a possible write-off as a risk of the Helly Hansen integration.
  • Stranded costs. $40 million of overhead only disappears if management cuts it. I credit half in the Base, and the savings are worth $3.2 a share in my method.

Management execution

Scott Baxter is both chief executive and chairman. Since the Helly Hansen purchase in 2025 the execution has been good in the ways that can be measured: adjusted gross margin up 710 basis points in the second quarter, Helly Hansen ahead of plan, EPS guidance raised, and a Lee price above the expectations of at least one broker. Against that, the 2025 add-backs were large, the reported and adjusted numbers differ by more than I like, 2026 guidance had to be rebuilt around the Lee sale, and the same person holds both top jobs. Assessment: 14/20, executing well, reporting with a lot of adjustments.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. The Lee sale is cancelled or delivers well under $520 million after tax, so the buyback, the debt reduction and my $10.81 a share of Lee cash shrink or disappear.
  2. Gross margin falls back below about 48.5% because tariffs outrun pricing or the Jeanius savings fade, taking EBITDA toward my Bear case of $423 million.
  3. Wrangler sales turn negative because Walmart or other wholesale partners cut orders, which would break the pricing and share story that supports a 10 times multiple.
