Carpenter Technology melts the metal that goes into jet engines and aircraft structures, and for two years it has been one of the best profit stories in industrials: operating income of $702 million in fiscal 2026, and a record first quarter in fiscal 2027. The shares went from $175 at the start of 2025 to $625.99 in August 2026. Then, on October 22, the company reported the quarter and the shares fell 20.6% in one session, the worst day of the year, because margins stopped rising, guidance for the next quarter was flat and cash flow was negative. They have kept falling, and closed at $258.78 on November 25 after a new 52-week low of $248.68 that day. My Base case has operating income reaching $1.2 billion by fiscal 2031, a little below the company's own target for fiscal 2029, and it is worth $228.02 a share.

The Bear value is $119.77 and the Bull value is $300.30, so the price sits 116% above Bear and 14% below Bull. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 30%, so the maximum buy price is **$159.62**. Valuation route: a specialty metals producer valued on the free cash flow that belongs to shareholders after tax, capital spending and the cash tied up in inventory and receivables. ## What Carpenter is

Carpenter makes premium specialty alloys: nickel-based superalloys, titanium, stainless and other metals that have to survive heat, stress and corrosion. Its customers are the makers of aircraft engines, airframes and fasteners, and, to a lesser degree, medical implants, energy equipment and industrial machinery. Aerospace and defense were 67% of sales excluding surcharge in the latest quarter.

It reports two segments. Specialty Alloys Operations (SAO) is the core: it earned $216.3 million of operating income in the quarter at an adjusted margin of 37.7% excluding surcharge. Performance Engineered Products (PEP), which includes titanium and engineered parts, is small and weaker: $7.3 million of operating income at a margin of 8.8%. A surcharge, which passes raw material costs such as nickel through to the customer, adds about a fifth to reported sales ($163.4 million of $798.2 million in the quarter), so I use sales excluding surcharge, $634.8 million, to judge margins. Making these alloys takes qualification by the customer and years of lead time, which is why capacity is scarce and why pricing has risen.

The company is building new capacity at an existing site (the "brownfield" project), which it says is on schedule and on budget and will start up in early fiscal 2028. Management said on the fourth-quarter call that the project should add about $150 million of operating income by fiscal 2030.

## Why I use operating income less tax, capital spending and working capital

Carpenter's cash flow is lumpy, because it builds inventory when orders rise and spends heavily on the new capacity. Operating cash flow was $605.0 million in fiscal 2026 and $52.8 million in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, and the reported free cash flow of $362.3 million for fiscal 2026 came after $242.7 million of capacity spending, so it understates what the business earns once the project is finished. I build owner free cash flow from the operating income instead: operating income after tax at 20% (the fourth-quarter rate was 20.7% and the first-quarter rate of 15.5% is too low to rely on), plus depreciation of about $147 million a year, less capital spending, less the cash that goes into inventory and receivables.

My Base assumptions are my own estimates, calibrated to the company's guide: capital spending of $330 million in fiscal 2027 ($110 million of upkeep and $220 million for the final phase of the brownfield project), $150 million in fiscal 2028 and then about 5% above depreciation, and working capital equal to 25% of any increase in sales excluding surcharge, plus an extra $33 million in fiscal 2027 after the $157.5 million outflow in the first quarter. With those inputs my fiscal 2027 owner free cash flow is $437 million before interest, or about $415 million after it, in the middle of the company's $400 million to $430 million guide. Stock compensation is not added back, because I start from GAAP operating income.

## Balance sheet, shares and buybacks

At September 30 Carpenter had $159.5 million of cash and $691.0 million of long-term debt, so net debt was $531.5 million, or 0.6 times EBITDA, which the company calls a historic low. It also has $499.1 million of undrawn borrowing capacity and says it has no debt maturities until 2034 (the credit facility was extended to November 2030 and has nothing drawn, and $700 million of senior notes were issued in November 2025). Accrued pension liabilities are $82.4 million, and I deduct them as a debt-like claim, so the net bridge from enterprise value to equity is $613.9 million, or $12.33 a share.

The share count is falling. Weighted diluted shares were 50.0 million in the quarter, against 50.4 million a year earlier, and I use 49.8 million. The company repurchased 241,924 shares for $133.9 million in the quarter, an average of about $553 a share, which is more than twice today's price. It completed its $400 million program and announced a new $1.0 billion one in August, of which $985.0 million remains, which is 7.6% of today's market value. It paid dividends of $9.9 million, $0.20 a share a quarter. I did not model the buyback, because buying at a fair price adds no value, but at $259 it would, and I note that the company's best use of the cash has changed with the price.

## Primary valuation: ten years of owner free cash flow

The primary method discounts ten years of owner free cash flow at 10%, the framework default, and I test 9% and 11%. The valuation date is the September 30 balance sheet, so the rest of fiscal 2027 counts as a stub of nine months, and fiscal 2028 to fiscal 2036 follow.

Base operating income is $850 million in fiscal 2027 (the bottom of the guide), $975 million in 2028, $1,100 million in 2029, $1,160 million in 2030 as the brownfield project ramps up, and $1,200 million in 2031. That is 8% below the low end of the $1.2 billion to $1.3 billion target for 2029, because the second half of 2027 already has to do a lot, aerospace is cyclical and the target depends on aircraft build rates I cannot see. After 2031 it grows 3% a year, reaching $1,391 million in 2036. The margin on sales excluding surcharge rises from 32.6% to 35%. Owner free cash flow is $437 million in fiscal 2027, then $708, $806, $888 and $923 million, and $1,073 million in 2036. Terminal growth is 2.5%.

The stub and the ten years are worth $111.19 a share, the terminal value $116.27 and the net bridge takes off $12.33. The primary value is **$215.13**.

Terminal value share: 51% of enterprise value, at a ten-year horizon, below the 75% level where I would check that the terminal value is supported by sustainable flows.

## Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 11 times EBITDA

The cross-check takes the stub and the next four fiscal years of owner free cash flow, to June 2031, and values the company at the end of fiscal 2031 at 11 times that year's EBITDA of $1,375 million, or $15.1 billion. Discounted at 10%, the cash flow adds $60.07 a share, the exit adds $193.17 and the bridge takes off $12.33. The secondary value is **$240.91**.

**Why 11 times.** The sector guide puts quality industrial and aerospace names at 12 to 18 times, and I use less because the exit year is near the top of my profit path, when a cyclical business deserves a lower multiple. By fiscal 2031 my Base turns 67% of EBITDA into owner free cash flow, so 11 times EBITDA is 16.4 times free cash flow. At a 10% return that implies growth of about 3.7% a year afterward, above my 2.5% terminal growth and about what inflation plus modest volume growth would give. The market pays 13.5 times today, so the cross-check does not assume the market's multiple holds.

Exit value share: 76% of enterprise value comes from the exit, at a horizon of five years, below the 85% level where I check that the exit is supported by sustainable flows.

## What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $258.78 needs one of these: operating income 14% higher in every year (about $968 million in fiscal 2027, $1,253 million in 2029 and $1,367 million in 2031), a discount rate of 8.7% instead of 10%, or an exit multiple of about 14.5 times in the cross-check. When I replace my Base path with the company's own target, $1.25 billion in fiscal 2029 and $1.4 billion in 2030, the value is $270.39. In other words, the price is what the shares are worth if management hits the middle of its fiscal 2029 target and the brownfield project delivers on time, and it is a few dollars below that.

That is why the verdict is Hold and not Reduce. The market is not pricing a fantasy. It is pricing the company's target, which is a credible plan with real demand behind it, but which has to be met with a second half of fiscal 2027 that is 17% to 25% above the first.

As a return, buying at $258.78 and holding the Base case to the end of fiscal 2031 (June 2031), with the cash that builds up added to an exit at 11 times EBITDA, gives about **8.2% a year**, below the 10% I require. The same math gives -6.9% a year in Bear and 15.5% in Bull. If the market still pays today's 13.5 times at the end of fiscal 2031, the Base earns 12.4% a year, and at 10 times it earns 6.4%. The dividend of $0.80 a share is a 0.3% yield, so the return comes from earnings growth and the exit.

On earnings, the shares trade at about 19 times my fiscal 2027 earnings, which is not demanding for a business with a 37% margin in its main segment, but earnings of this size are new. A growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) would reward that rebound, so I do not rely on it. Growth durability: pricing and mix are structural for the next two to three years, because capacity is short, while volume growth depends on aircraft build rates and is cyclical. Free cash flow growth is distorted by the brownfield spending and the inventory build, which is why I use the operating income path.

## How the buy price is set

Confidence B starts at a 30% margin of safety. ## Dividend and buybacks

Carpenter pays $0.20 a share a quarter, $0.80 a year, a 0.3% yield, which cost $9.9 million in the first quarter against $52.8 million of operating cash flow, so the dividend is safe and small. Buybacks are the larger tool: $133.9 million in the first quarter alone and $985.0 million of authorization left. ## Reasons to own CRS

- **Scarce capacity and pricing.** Management says pricing keeps strengthening, and several long-term agreements are being negotiated at higher prices.
- **Demand step-up.** Aerospace structural and distribution bookings rose 49% from a year ago and engine bookings 22%.
- **Margins in the core segment.** SAO earned a 37.7% adjusted margin, up 5.7 points in a year.
- **A clean balance sheet.** Net debt is 0.6 times EBITDA, there are no maturities until 2034 and $499.1 million is undrawn.
- **Capacity growth.** The brownfield project is on schedule for early fiscal 2028 and is expected to add about $150 million of operating income by fiscal 2030.
- **A low price for the buyback.** The shares are 59% below the August high, and $985.0 million of authorization is available.

## What could go wrong?

- **The aerospace cycle.** Two thirds of sales go to aerospace and defense, and the profit growth depends on aircraft and engine build rates that the airframers can miss. This is the Bear case.
- **Back-end loading.** The fiscal 2027 guide needs about $230 million a quarter in the second half, 17% above the first quarter. A miss would confirm the October sell-off.
- **Margin plateau.** The SAO margin stopped rising at 37.7%, and my Base has the margin on all sales excluding surcharge rising only 2.4 points more.
- **Weak end markets.** Medical fell 16%, energy 23%, and defense and fastener bookings softened.
- **Cash conversion.** First-quarter free cash flow was negative, inventory rose $61.6 million, and the company stopped reporting volumes, which makes it harder to separate price from volume.
- **Execution on the new capacity.** A delay or cost overrun on the brownfield project would push back the $150 million of extra operating income.
- **Capital allocation.** The company bought back stock at about $553 a share in the quarter, and the shares are now less than half of that.

## Management execution

Management, led by Chairman, President and CEO Tony Thene, has lifted operating income to $702 million in fiscal 2026, delivered a record first quarter, kept both the fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2029 targets and said it is working on actions to exceed them. The brownfield project is on schedule and on budget. Against that, the first quarter landed just under the midpoint of its own guide, SAO missed its own range, the company stopped giving volume, the buyback in the quarter was at more than twice the current price, and the guide for the second quarter did not move. I did not review pay or governance in detail.
