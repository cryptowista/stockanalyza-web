Pershing Square USA raised $5 billion at $50 a share in April 2026, its semi-annual report puts day-one net asset value at $48.79 after the cost of the offering, and the shares closed at $34.58 on November 17.

Valuation route: closed-end investment company, valued by the look-through net asset value of its listed holdings, with the cost of the structure and an exit discount modeled explicitly. ## What Pershing Square USA is

PSUS is a closed-end fund, a Delaware statutory trust registered under the Investment Company Act and listed on the New York Stock Exchange since April 29, 2026. It has 100,000,000 shares, no redemptions and no performance fee. Bill Ackman and Ryan Israel run it through Pershing Square Capital Management, the same manager as the London-listed Pershing Square Holdings (PSH), and the target is 12 to 15 large positions.

- **Fee.** 2.0% of net asset value a year, charged as 0.50% each quarter on the prior quarter-end value and paid in advance. It earned $16.5 million in its first two months.
- **Preferred shares.** $50 million of Series A preferred stock at 7.50%, all held by PSUS Holdings, a subsidiary of Pershing Square Inc. Holders can require redemption from year 10 and elect two of the trustees.
- **Bonus shares.** Buyers in the offering got one share of Pershing Square Inc. (PS) for every five PSUS shares. A buyer today gets nothing extra.
- **Tax.** The fund intends to qualify as a regulated investment company, so it pays no federal income tax if it meets the diversification and distribution rules.
- **Leverage.** None was shown at June 30, but the fund says it plans unsecured bonds of roughly 15% to 20% of total assets.

The fund is not an operating business, so a cash flow valuation does not apply. What a shareholder owns is the portfolio, minus what the structure costs, at a price set by supply and demand for the shares.

## What the fund costs

Everything a shareholder earns is net asset value growth after these costs, because dividend income is far too small to cover them. Net investment income was $2.0 million in the first two months, against $17.5 million of expenses.

- **Management fee.** 2.0% of net asset value. At $51.27 a share that is about $1.03 a share, or $102.5 million a year (my arithmetic).
- **Other expenses.** Professional fees, trustee pay and other costs were $0.9 million in the first two months, about 0.1% a year (my annualization).
- **Preferred dividend.** $3.75 million a year, about 0.07% of net asset value.
- **Total.** About 2.2% a year. I use this figure throughout.

A cost of 2.2% a year does not look like much. Compounded, it removes about 20% of terminal wealth over 10 years and 28% over 15. The current discount of 32.6% equals about 18 years of it, so today's price already allows for roughly 18 years of the fee with no skill added. Anything the manager adds beyond the fee comes on top. The same twelve shares can be bought directly for nothing, so the question is whether selection, position size and the manager's involvement with the companies are worth more than 2.2% a year.

## Secondary valuation: where the sister fund trades

The cross-check applies a discount to today's net asset value instead of modeling the future. The closest comparison is PSH, the London fund run by the same manager. On October 27 it traded at $50.50 against a net asset value of $77.95, a 35.2% discount. On November 12 the fund repurchased shares at $52.80 when the latest net asset value, dated November 10, was $83.35, a 36.7% discount. Third-party reports put its discount near 25% in August, at 30% to 35% in 2023 and 2024, around 40% in 2022 and near 9% when it listed in 2014. I use a central discount of 30%, with 25% and 37% as the range.

| Discount applied to $51.27 | Value a share |
| --- | --- |
| 25% (narrower end) | $38.45 |
| 30% (central) | **$35.89** |
| 37% (about where the sister fund trades now) | $32.30 |

PSH charges 1.5% plus a performance fee, and PSUS charges 2.0% with none. In a strong year PSUS is the cheaper wrapper and in a weak year it is the dearer one. So PSUS arguably deserves a discount no wider than PSH's, and at 32.6% against 36.7% it already trades that way. This is a market reference and not an intrinsic value, which is why it carries a lower weight than the primary method.

## Valuation triangulation

- Primary (net asset value path, 30% exit discount, 10% required return): $40.16, weight 60%.
- Secondary (30% discount to today's net asset value): $35.89, weight 40%.
- Divergence between the two: 11.2% of their midpoint, inside the 20% limit.
- Weighted Base: $38.45. The model's own spread is $35.89 to $40.16, and the real uncertainty is wider, as the Bear and Bull show.

I weight the primary method higher because it shows each assumption, so a reader can change it. The two methods are not independent: both depend on how wide the discount will be, one at exit and one today. Their agreement therefore adds nothing to Confidence.

## Dividend and buybacks

The fund has paid nothing yet and expects to declare any dividend once a year. Income is far below costs, so a distribution would come mainly from realized gains, and as a regulated investment company the fund has to pass on most of them or pay tax itself. I found no repurchase program for PSUS. Its London sister repurchased 22,932 shares on November 12 at a 37% discount to net asset value, so the manager uses the tool where it is available, and PSUS has not announced one.

## Reasons to own PSUS

- The shares sell 32.6% below the reported value of the assets. If the discount closed completely, the price would rise 48%, which is arithmetic and not a forecast.
- There is no performance fee, so a strong year is not shared with the manager.
- The portfolio is 12 large, profitable franchises at about 19 times earnings, with growth that the manager puts at 19.6% a year against 13% for the S&P 500.
- The manager and its affiliates own 11.2% of the shares, so they carry the same discount as everyone else.
- Pershing Square reports a 15.9% yearly net asset value return over 21 years to 2024 against 10.0% for the S&P 500, in its letter to shareholders of the London fund.

## What could go wrong?

- The discount widens further. It has gone from about 16% in the first week to 32.6% while the assets recovered, and the London fund shows it can sit at 37% or more for years.
- Concentration. The top five holdings are 58.0% of net assets and three large technology platforms are 39.1%. The manager's letter names Amazon's capital spending of about $220 billion in 2026 as the main investor worry.
- Costs of 2.2% a year are paid in good and bad years. Since the offering the fund has trailed the S&P 500 by about 11 points.
- Leverage is planned. Bonds of 15% to 20% of total assets would add risk to a concentrated portfolio, and equity forwards with two banks already carried a $122.7 million unrealized loss at June 30.
- The preferred stock is held by an affiliate and can be redeemed from year 10. The Board has noted that preferred issuance can raise the manager's overall fee.
- If the fund failed the tests for a regulated investment company in a year, its income would be taxed at corporate rates.
- Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, 1.8% of the fund, depend on a government decision that has no date.

## Management execution

- The manager put about 85% of the capital to work in 12 companies within six weeks of listing and the fund was 97.8% invested at September 30. Buying into a falling market cost the first quarter, and the third quarter recovered part of it.
- In June the manager called the discount a short-term technical effect of the offering, when it was about 20%. Five months later it is 32.6%. The semi-annual report names it as the fund's biggest challenge and announces a marketing program, not a repurchase program.
- The manager replaced a planned first-year fee waiver with bonus shares of Pershing Square Inc. for the original buyers. Anyone buying on the exchange since then does not get them.
- The Board disclosed that the manager offsets 20% of the fees from PSUS against the performance fee of the London fund. So fees paid by PSUS partly reduce what holders of the London fund pay.

## What I would watch from here

### Green

- A funded repurchase program that is used, or a tender offer for shares.
- The discount back under 25% for a month without a jump in net asset value.
- The fourth quarter review shows PSUS ahead of the S&P 500 since the offering.

### Yellow

- A discount between 30% and 38% with net asset value flat.
- The first issue of bonds, with the amount and cost disclosed.
- The first annual distribution, and whether it is paid from income or from gains.

### Red

- A discount wider than 40%, or no response to it from the manager.
- The top five holdings above 65% of net assets, or bond leverage added during a drawdown.
- Margin calls on the equity forwards, or any problem with the regulated investment company status.

The next hard data points are the monthly fact sheets and the fourth quarter review on the manager's quarterly schedule.

## The three most important thesis breakers

1. The discount settles at 40% or wider with no repurchases. The Base earnings path then gives $34.42, which is today's price, with no return from the discount.
2. Portfolio earnings grow at 8% a year and not 14%, which gives $30.53 at a 30% exit discount and $26.17 at 40%.
3. The fund adds 15% to 20% bond leverage into a falling market, which turns a 12-stock portfolio into a leveraged bet while the discount is wide.

## Final verdict: hold at $34.58

The discount is a real asset only if someone closes it. Since the first week of trading it has roughly doubled, and the fund's own filings offer a marketing program as the answer. At $34.58 you pay 0.67 times the assets and about 2.2% a year to hold them.

**Verdict: HOLD at $34.58. Weighted Base $38.45, 11.2% above the price, cross-check $35.89, Confidence C (15/25). Required margin of safety 42.5%, maximum buy price $22.11 (range $18.57 to $27.62).**

An existing holder has no valuation reason to sell, because the price is below the Base value and the Base case returns about 13% a year. A new buyer gets about 13.3% in the Base and 0.4% in the Bear, and owning the same shares directly earns more than PSUS in every case except the Bull. New money should wait for a price at or below $22.11 ($27.62 under my most generous assumptions), or for evidence that the discount is being addressed. The next review is the fourth quarter report.
