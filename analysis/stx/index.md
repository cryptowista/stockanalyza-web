## What Seagate is

Seagate, based in Singapore and Ireland, makes hard disk drives, mostly large-capacity drives sold to cloud data centers. In the September quarter it shipped about 220 exabytes, of which 198 exabytes were nearline drives, and data centers took 90% of the capacity (Blocks and Files). Data center customers were more than four fifths of revenue in the June quarter (Digitimes). Seagate and Western Digital share most of the market, and Toshiba is a distant third.

Two things changed in a year. Demand from AI data centers outran supply, and Seagate has raised capacity per drive through a new recording technology called HAMR (heat-assisted magnetic recording), sold under the name Mozaic. HAMR should be about half of nearline exabytes shipped by the end of calendar 2026, and the next generation, up to 55 terabytes a drive, enters customer testing near the end of 2027. Most nearline supply is committed under long-term agreements through calendar 2028, and some cloud agreements run to 2030; management says demand visibility extends beyond 2030. Seagate plans mid-20% annual growth in exabytes, from density and not from more drives.

The risk is on the other side of the same coin. Toshiba said on October 2 that it plans to double hard drive capacity, with a medium-term target of 30% of the market by capacity, and the shares fell about 10% that day. How quickly that capacity reaches cloud buyers is unknown (Yahoo Finance). Since then the whole AI hardware group has been sold: after the midterm elections on November 3 the shares fell 8% and then 16% on consecutive days, on no news of their own (TIKR), and on November 20 they fell again as memory and storage stocks sold off together.

## Owner free cash flow

I add depreciation (1.8% of sales, from $76 million a quarter), subtract capital spending of 5.5% of sales (the first-quarter figure was 5.0%) and subtract 10% of every extra dollar of sales for receivables and inventory, net of payables; at October 2 receivables and inventory exceeded payables by $1.64 billion.

## Reasons to own Seagate

- Profit is at a record: a 48.8% GAAP operating margin, $7.68 of earnings a share in one quarter and $1.5 billion of free cash flow.
- Demand visibility is unusual for hardware: nearline supply is mostly committed under long-term agreements to 2028, some to 2030.
- The technology is ahead: HAMR drives are about to be half of nearline exabytes, and 55 terabyte drives are planned for testing late in 2027.
- The balance sheet has been repaired quickly: gross debt fell from $3.6 billion to about $1.6 billion in a few months, and net cash is expected by year end.
- The Bull value of $556.82 is 22% above the price, and the shares are down more than half from September.

## What could go wrong?

- Cycle. A year ago the GAAP operating margin was 26%, not 49%, and my Base falls by about $9 a share for each point of margin in every year.
- Supply. Toshiba plans to double capacity and wants 30% of the market; if Western Digital or Seagate also add output, the pricing power of the last year can reverse quickly.
- Contracts. The long-term agreements fix volumes through 2028, but I did not see their pricing terms; the 10-Q and the call transcript were not read in full.
- Customer concentration. Data center customers took 90% of capacity and I did not retrieve the share of the largest customers; a pause in cloud spending would hit first.
- AI sentiment. The shares fell 8%, 16% and 12% on days without Seagate news; the whole group trades as one position.
- Technology. A slow ramp of Mozaic 4 or the 55 terabyte generation would delay the gains in density that protect the margin.
- Data gaps. The terms of the remaining debt and exchangeable notes, the $1.4 billion of other non-current liabilities, capital spending guidance, segment revenue and customer shares were not available to me.

## Management execution

Dave Mosley, chair and chief executive, has taken the margin from 40% to 57% gross in a year, delivered a record $3.1 billion of free cash flow in fiscal 2026, and retired about $2.6 billion of debt in four quarters while raising the dividend. The offsets: the share count is not shrinking despite the buybacks, and the strong results came from a market that favored sellers, which is not management's doing alone. **Assessment: 15/20, strong on execution and deleveraging, unproven in a downturn. The next test is the December-quarter report in late January, when the guide of $9.20 a share meets the first signs of new supply.**

### Yellow

- Gross margin flat or down by one point from 57.8%.
- Mozaic ramp slower than planned, or inventory growing faster than sales.
- Capital spending rising above 7% of sales.

### Red

- Revenue guidance below $4.7 billion or a quarter of falling revenue.
- A cloud customer pausing orders or Western Digital cutting prices.
- Gross margin below 52%, the level of the June quarter.

Today demand, contracts and profitability are green; supply and sentiment are yellow, and the price is red. That is why the verdict is reduce: the business has done nothing wrong, but the price already pays for a margin that I can only show for two years.

## The three things that would make this verdict wrong

1. A margin that stays near 45% to 50% for ten years. At 10 points above my Base path the value is $398.24, and the price needs 16 points. Long-term agreements to 2028 and 2030 make this possible and not likely.
2. Demand growing faster than my sales path. At 3 points more growth in every year the Base is $359.69.
3. A lower discount rate. At 6.9% my Base equals the price; at 9% it is $340.14. With the ten-year Treasury yield above 5% (StockStory, November 16) I do not think a rate below 10% is justified for a stock with a beta of 2.2.

Two of the three together could close the gap, which is why I show a Bull value above the price. My rules ask for a margin of safety, not a bet on the best case.
