Brookfield: 16.5 times distributable earnings and a hold at $39.50

BN, NYSE. Published October 30, 2026. Price as of the October 29, 2026 regular session close. Values in US dollars. Latest reported quarter: second quarter 2026.

Hold at $39.50

Brookfield trades 23% above a Base value of $32.00 and at 16.5 times the $2.39 a share it earned in distributable earnings before realizations over the last twelve months. To justify the price, those earnings would have to grow about 19% a year for ten years, or the market would have to accept a return of 9.8% a year instead of my 11.5%. A 35% margin of safety puts the buy range at $19.40 to $22.89, far below the price, so this is a hold for holders and a watchlist name for new money.

Brookfield Corporation earned $5.65 billion of distributable earnings before realizations over the twelve months to June 30, 2026, or $2.39 a share, and $6.17 billion, or $2.61 a share, once realized carried interest is added. The business is a collection of three engines: an asset manager that charges fees and earns carried interest, an insurance and annuity group, and a set of operating businesses. The shares already pay for all three at a growth rate that I find hard to underwrite with a margin of safety.

At $39.50 on October 29, 2026, the shares trade 23.4% above my Base value of $32.00, so the Base is 19.0% below the price. The Bear value of $22.17 is 43.9% below the price and the Bull value of $42.15 is 6.7% above it. I assign no probabilities to the cases. The required margin of safety of 35% gives a buy range of $19.40 to $22.89 and a conservative limit of $19.40. For existing holders this is a hold, and nothing here says to sell. For new money it is a watchlist name.

Valuation route: Holding company and conglomerate, valued on distributable earnings. Economic classification: Quality Growth to Compounder, with growth and the return on reinvested capital partly estimated. Primary method: ten years of distributable earnings converted to cash flow to shareholders, with the capital that growth consumes deducted, plus a separate value for realized carried interest. Cross-check: 14 times current distributable earnings before realizations. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

What Brookfield is

Brookfield Corporation owns a controlling stake in Brookfield Asset Management, the manager of alternative assets. It also owns Brookfield Wealth Solutions, a retirement-services and insurance group, and holds majority or large stakes in operating businesses across infrastructure, renewable power and property. The company reports one profit measure that ties them together: distributable earnings, which counts fees, spread earnings, distributions from operating businesses and realized carried interest, and is stated after corporate costs, corporate interest and preferred dividends.

That measure is not an accounting profit. It is a management definition, and it leaves out unrealized gains and losses that flow through net income. I use it because it is the figure the company manages to and because it is the closest thing to cash that the businesses send up to the parent. The weakness is that it relies on the company's own definition of what is distributable, so I keep a larger margin of safety than I would for a simpler business.

I value the group as one earnings stream and do not run a sum of the parts. The listed stakes trade at their own multiples, but a separate sum of the parts adds a second layer of assumptions about control premiums and the discount a holding company deserves, and it would not be independent of the earnings approach.

What the earnings are made of

Three features decide how much of the $2.39 I treat as lasting.

  • Fees and spread earnings are recurring. Asset management fees come from capital that is committed for years, and insurance spread earnings come from annuity liabilities written against assets that the group itself manages. Together they make up about three quarters of segment earnings.
  • Carried interest is lumpy. I exclude it from the main earnings base and value it separately, at $0.22 a share in the Base, which equals the average realized over the last twelve months ($520 million over 2,362 million diluted shares). A single large realization can move a quarter, and a cycle without exits can remove it.
  • Operating businesses are the least certain part. They send up distributions, but those depend on the cash flow and refinancing of individual infrastructure, power and property assets, and I do not know the distribution capacity of each.

I did not find an audited cash flow figure that matches distributable earnings, so I cannot say what share of it is received in cash in a given year. That is an open data gap, and it is one reason the Confidence grade is C.

Primary valuation: distributable earnings to shareholders

The model starts from the $2.39 of distributable earnings before realizations and grows it for ten years. Each year, the cash available to shareholders is the year's earnings minus the capital needed to fund the next year's growth. That capital is the increase in earnings divided by the return on new capital, which I set at 14% in the Base. A terminal value follows year ten, discounted at the cost of equity.

  • Base: earnings growth of 13%, 12%, 11%, 10%, 9%, 8%, 7%, 6%, 5% and 4% over ten years, which lifts earnings from $2.39 to $2.70 in year one and $4.03 in year five, a five-year growth rate of 11.0% a year. Cost of equity 11.5%, terminal growth 3.0%, return on new capital 14%.
  • Carried interest: valued separately as $0.22 a share, growing at 3% and capitalized at the cost of equity.
  • Result: $27.19 for the earnings stream and $2.67 for carried interest, a total of $29.85. The terminal value is 63.5% of the earnings value, over a ten-year explicit horizon.

A terminal share of 63.5% sits below the 75% attention level for a ten-year horizon, so I do not treat it as a defect, but it means that 64 cents of each dollar of value depends on what happens after 2036. The growth path I use is above what the market usually grants a mature company, which reflects the real capital Brookfield raises and invests. It is an estimate, not a reported figure.

Secondary valuation: earnings multiple

The cross-check applies 14 times to distributable earnings before realizations and adds carried interest at 8 times its $0.22 realized level: $33.46 plus $1.76 gives $35.22. The 14 times is not a tier default. It sits below the 16.5 times the market pays and reflects my view that a durable fee base deserves a premium to a plain holding company while the leveraged parent and the growth the Base needs argue against paying more. The multiple is a judgment, and the range from 10 times (Bear) to 18 times (Bull) shows how much of the answer it carries.

The two methods share the same starting earnings and the same view that fee income is stable. They differ in how growth, the return on new capital and the cost of equity are treated. Their agreement therefore says less than it would if the inputs were independent, and I give no extra Confidence for it.

Historical cross-check and growth diagnostic

I did not collect a multi-year multiple history for Brookfield, so I do not claim that the price is above or below its own past average. The historical score is neutral and the data gap is listed in the fine print. What I can say is that fee-related earnings rose 20% year on year in the second quarter, and that my Base path assumes growth of 13%, 12% and 11% in the next three years and a fade after that. One quarter does not tell me whether that pace is sustainable as the fee-bearing capital base gets larger.

I do not use a forward growth-adjusted multiple as a source of value. Growth is classified as structural for fees and spread earnings, and acquisition-affected for operating businesses, because purchases add earnings that are not organic.

Valuation triangulation

  • Primary (earnings cash flow model): $29.85, weight 60%.
  • Secondary (14 times earnings): $35.22, weight 40%.
  • Divergence between the two: 16.5% of their midpoint, inside the 20% limit, so no divergence flag.
  • Weighted Base: $32.00. The unweighted range of $29.85 to $35.22 is the honest uncertainty, and $32.00 is a point inside it, not a narrowing of it.

I weight the cash flow model higher because it states its growth and capital needs openly, while the multiple hides them in one number. Both values are below the price, so the choice of weights does not change the verdict.

If the price ever reaches $19.40, I would first check why. The price is above all three entry values, so the verdict is Hold, not Buy.

Dividend and buybacks

The dividend is $0.07 a quarter, or $0.28 a year, a yield of 0.7%. It takes about 12% of the $2.39 of earnings, so it is safe and also small. Buybacks of about $580 million so far this year are under 1% of the market value and offset little of the share count, because the diluted count still includes share equivalents. Shareholder return comes from reinvestment and the growth of the earnings stream, not from cash paid out.

Reasons to own BN

  • Recurring fee and spread earnings are about three quarters of segment earnings, and fee-bearing capital is committed for long periods.
  • $210 billion of deployable capital gives room to raise funds and invest through a downturn.
  • Fee-related earnings rose 20% year on year in the second quarter, and the Base path assumes growth fades over ten years, not that it continues.
  • Realized carried interest of $520 million over twelve months is an upside that the Base values at a modest level.

What could go wrong?

  • Fundraising or exits slow, which cuts fees on new capital and removes carried interest.
  • Insurance spread earnings are exposed to credit losses and to the performance of the assets that back annuity liabilities.
  • $14.7 billion of corporate debt and annual interest of about $0.7 billion need refinancing at acceptable rates.
  • The headline measure is management-defined, and its link to cash received is not shown in what I reviewed.
  • The price already assumes growth above my Base, so even a small miss can reduce the multiple.

Management execution

  • The company holds $210 billion of deployable capital, which shows the franchise can attract and keep capital.
  • Repurchases of about $580 million this year were made at an average of $42, above the current price, a small point against timing but not against intent.
  • Compensation and carried interest sharing between the parent, the manager and the operating businesses are complex, and I did not audit them.

What I would watch from here

Green

  • Distributable earnings before realizations a share above $0.65 in a quarter, with fee-related earnings still growing.
  • Large fund closings that add fee-bearing capital without a rise in corporate debt.
  • A published reconciliation of distributable earnings to cash received by the parent.

Yellow

  • Realized carried interest falls to near zero for two quarters.
  • Corporate interest rises faster than earnings.
  • Share equivalents grow faster than buybacks reduce them.

Red

  • Fee-bearing capital shrinks, or fundraising stalls for several quarters.
  • Credit losses in the insurance portfolio that reduce spread earnings.
  • Corporate debt that must be refinanced at a materially higher rate.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Fee-bearing capital stops growing, which removes the base of the 11% growth path.
  2. A credit event in the insurance group, which would hit spread earnings and raise the cost of funding.
  3. A rise in the cost of equity above 12.5%, which lowers the Base cash flow value to $25.88 or less.

Final verdict: hold at $39.50, watchlist for new money

Brookfield is a high-quality, growing collection of fee, insurance and operating earnings, priced above what I think it is worth. At 16.5 times distributable earnings, the price needs both growth and the multiple to hold up, and it offers no margin of safety.

Verdict: HOLD at $39.50, watchlist for new money. Weighted Base $32.00, 19.0% below the price, cross-check $35.22, Confidence C (17/25). Required margin of safety 35%, buy range $19.40 to $22.89, conservative buy limit $19.40.

For an existing holder, the original cost is irrelevant, and nothing here says to sell: the price is inside my Bull range and the business is strong. A review is warranted if the third quarter report on November 12 shows earnings that miss the Base path, or if the price moves well above the Bull value of $42.15 without a change in fundamentals. New money should wait for a price at or below $22.89 and a clearer link from distributable earnings to cash.
