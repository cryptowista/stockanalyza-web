Century Aluminum reported $550.7 million of consolidated adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, after the company's own adjustments) for the first half of 2026: $227.9 million in the first quarter and $322.8 million in the second. Over the same six months its operations produced $236.0 million of cash, and $134.4 million of that went into capital spending. The gap is not a mystery. The company adds back $151.6 million of costs from the failure of equipment at its Iceland smelter, plus $31.7 million of Mt. Holly restart and emergency power charges, and those were cash costs. Adjusted EBITDA is a ceiling here, not a cash number.

At the October 9, 2026 close of $36.76, the shares trade 12.2% above my triangulated Base value of $32.77. The Bear value is $4.49 and the Bull value is $71.44. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 55%, so the maximum buy price is $14.75, about 60% below today's price. The business is earning very large profits at today's prices. The question is how much of that is the metal and how much is a premium that policy can change.

In the second quarter, Century's realized US Midwest premium, the extra amount a US buyer pays for delivered metal, was $2,480 per tonne. The aluminum price itself on the London Metal Exchange (LME) was $3,250. The premium was 76% of the metal price (both figures from management's second quarter call). That premium exists because of a 50% US tariff on most imported aluminum, the rate management cited on the call. It is the single most valuable input in this model and the one nobody at Century sets.

Valuation route: industrial and manufacturing. Subtype: primary aluminum smelter with a consolidated alumina refinery stake, commodity cyclical tier (5x to 8x mid-cycle EV/EBITDA). The mining, net asset value of reserves method does not fit because the business transforms purchased inputs and owns no reserves, but I borrow its rule to run three commodity-price scenarios. Economic classification: Standard (2/12). Primary method: mid-cycle EBITDA after stock compensation at 6.0x, built from an aluminum price deck. Secondary method: a scenario cash flow model (DCF) that starts from today's prices and fades to mid-cycle. Cross-check: price to free cash flow (P/FCF). Values are present fair value estimates, not 12 month price targets.

What Century Aluminum is

Century makes primary aluminum, metal smelted from alumina with electricity. It runs the Sebree (Kentucky) and Mt. Holly (South Carolina) smelters in the US and the Grundartangi smelter in Iceland, and it consolidates the Jamalco alumina refinery in Jamaica, where a partner holds a minority share. It sold the Hawesville site in February 2026. In the second quarter it shipped 130,632 tonnes: 95,057 from the US and 35,575 from Iceland.

Three features set the economics:

  • Price taker. Revenue is the LME price plus a regional premium. Costs, mainly electricity and alumina, do not move with either.
  • One buyer. Glencore, which also owns 30.0% of the common stock, accounted for about 44.0% of second quarter net sales and 54.0% of 2025 sales.
  • Power. Sebree buys electricity under a market-based contract, Mt. Holly has a power agreement with Santee Cooper through 2031, and Grundartangi uses Icelandic hydroelectric and geothermal power. Management guided a $10 million to $15 million energy headwind in the third quarter from warmer weather.

A fourth item is a tax credit. The Section 45X production credit, 10% of certain eligible production costs, reduced cost of goods sold by $26.3 million in the second quarter and $51.3 million in the first half. It sits inside the cost line, so it is inside my cost per tonne below. It is also a law that can change.

Why a price deck, and what the cost per tonne is

A smelter's profit is a spread: what the metal sells for minus what it costs to make. The framework asks for mid-cycle earnings, not peak earnings, so I do not capitalize $1.34 billion. I build EBITDA from three prices (the LME price, the Midwest premium and the European premium), a cost per tonne and a volume.

I calibrated the cost on the two quarters where I have prices. For each, I took the weighted selling price (LME plus premium, weighted by the US and Iceland share of tonnes) and subtracted consolidated adjusted EBITDA per tonne shipped. The remainder is the all-in cost per tonne, net of alumina and other revenue.

The larger capital question is Oklahoma. Century has a 40% stake in a joint smelter with Emirates Global Aluminium, 60%, for 750,000 tonnes a year at a reported cost of about $4 billion. Management targets a final investment decision by year-end 2026 and first metal by the end of 2029, and says the final power contract is still under negotiation and a $500 million Department of Energy grant is secured and pays out dollar for dollar against project spending. Century's dollar commitment is not disclosed in what I read. A pro rata share would be about $1.6 billion, or $1.1 billion after the grant (my estimate), which is 3.2 times June 30 cash. I value the existing mills only and assume the project earns its cost of capital, so it adds no value, and I count it as a funding risk, not a cash flow.

Primary valuation: mid-cycle EBITDA after stock compensation at 6.0x

The formula: common equity equals (mid-cycle EBITDA minus $35 million of stock compensation) times 6.0, minus net debt of $213.8 million, plus the credited share of the Hawesville put, divided by 104.79 million shares. The multiple sits in the framework's commodity cyclical tier of 5x to 8x, below its 6.5x midpoint, because Century is a single-commodity price taker with one dominant customer. I held the multiple at 6.0x in all three scenarios, so the price deck alone separates them and the downside is not counted twice. I had no verified peer table, so 6.0x is an assumption, tested from 5.0x to 7.0x below.

The price decks are my judgments, not forecasts and not forward curve prices (I did not have the curve). The Midwest premium is the one I weigh hardest. How the buy price is set

A Confidence score of 11/25 is grade D, which starts the MOS at 50%. I add 5 points for cyclicality, because the earnings are a spread between two prices that Century does not control, and 5 points for concentration, because one counterparty buys about half of the output and owns 30% of the company. I subtract 5 points for the balance sheet, with $784.9 million of liquidity and cash above debt in July. The method gap of 21.9% is under the 30% threshold, so it adds nothing. The result is 55%, under the 60% ceiling, so a price is issued.

Maximum buy price: $32.77 times (1 - 0.55) = $14.75. That is about 60% below today's price. At $14.75 the price already covers a Midwest premium near $0.30 per pound on the primary method ($13.94), so a buyer there is paid for the policy reversal in advance. The 52-week low is $25.40, and $14.75 is a price the shares have not traded at in the last year. Hold is the correct answer at $36.76, and it does not mean the business is poor. It means the price pays for it in full.

Dividend and capital return

Century pays no dividend and I assume none. Management ranked its priorities as liquidity, sustaining capital, then organic growth such as Oklahoma, and said capital returns are under consideration but announced nothing. No buyback is credited. The 2025 refund of the Section 45X credit, $94.3 million received in July, and the insurance receipts of $19 million in July are cash that will show up in the third quarter balance sheet. I do not credit them in net debt, which is as of June 30. Cash built in the third quarter (guided adjusted EBITDA of $325 million to $345 million, less capital spending, hedge settlements and interest) is also not credited until the November report; I count it as upside not yet in the Base.

Reasons to own CENX

  • Earnings at today's prices are enormous. Third quarter guidance annualizes to $1.34 billion of adjusted EBITDA, 35% of the diluted market value, and the Bull case is not far from it.
  • A scarce position. Century has US smelting capacity in a market where imports face a tariff, and it is one of two partners in the first new US smelter in decades, with up to 300 thousand tonnes a year of imports at a 25% tariff rate from 2027 under the July 20 executive order, according to management.
  • The operating restarts worked. Mt. Holly's last 90 pots restarted on time and on budget, and Grundartangi Line 2 was back about six months early.
  • A light balance sheet. Net debt is $213.8 million after pension and hedge liabilities, with no maturity before 2028.
  • Optionality outside the Base. The Hawesville put at $300.0 million is credited at 50%, and the 10-Q says a release of the US tax valuation allowance is reasonably possible within 12 months, which would cut book tax expense but not cash.

The Bull case of $71.44 is above the 52-week high of $70.43. It does not need heroic assumptions; it needs today's prices to last.

What could go wrong?

  • Thesis breaker: the Midwest premium falls. A $0.30 premium at an LME price of $2,650 leaves $13.94 on the primary method. The premium is a function of a tariff that policy can change.
  • Aluminum price. Each $100 per tonne is $72 million of EBITDA. At $2,300 and a $0.60 premium the primary method gives $15.4.
  • Oklahoma funding. A pro rata share of about $1.6 billion before the grant is 3.2 times the cash on hand. The final power contract is not signed. A funding plan that dilutes shareholders or loads the balance sheet would change the character of the company.
  • Customer concentration. Glencore bought 54.0% of 2025 sales and owns 30.0% of the shares.
  • Operations. Iceland had an equipment failure that cost $151.6 million in the first half, Mt. Holly shows "some instability" after the restart, and Jamalco still has lower quality bauxite for a couple more quarters.
  • Energy and tax credits. The Section 45X credit is $51.3 million of cost reduction in the first half and depends on law. Power for Sebree is priced to the market.
  • Hedges. The derivative liability is $65.1 million net, and realized settlements cost $30 million in the second quarter.
