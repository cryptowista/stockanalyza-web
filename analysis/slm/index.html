Sallie Mae: priced for a 20% return on equity

SLM, Nasdaq. Published October 14, 2026. Price as of the October 13, 2026 regular session close.

Hold at $22.70

Sallie Mae has earned more than 30% on common equity in each of the last three years. At $22.70 the stock prices in about 20%, forever, so my Base value of $25.40 sits 11.9% above the price. That is not a big enough gap for a single-product lender whose next test, the peak lending season, reports on October 22. Hold, watchlist for new money. The buy zone starts at $13.97.

The borrowers behind Sallie Mae's latest charge-off problem are not the weak ones. At a June investor conference, the finance chief said a small group of borrowers with strong credit profiles was defaulting out of proportion, steered by debt-resolution firms that market themselves as refinancing and then negotiate discounted settlements after the loan goes bad. The stock fell 7.6% on the news. Four months later it trades at the lowest multiple of tangible book value in at least five years.

At the October 13, 2026 close of $22.70, the shares trade 10.6% below my triangulated Base value of $25.40, an upside of 11.9%. The Bear value of $13.66 is 39.8% below the price and the Bull value of $35.05 is 54.4% above it. Confidence is grade C (16/25), the required margin of safety (MOS) is 45%, and the maximum buy price is $13.97. My broker showed $22.68 before the US open on October 14, in line with the close. Third-quarter results are due on October 22, 2026, eight days after this analysis.

Valuation route: Bank holding company and specialist consumer lender, valued on the equity that belongs to common shareholders, after $251.1 million of preferred stock. Classification: Quality Growth (8/12). Primary method: residual income, which values a bank as its book value plus the present value of profits above the cost of equity. Secondary method: price to next year's earnings. Cross-check: the stock's own price to tangible book history. Values are present fair value estimates, not 12 month price targets.

What Sallie Mae is

SLM Corporation is the part of the old Sallie Mae that kept the brand and the bank when the federal loan servicing business was split off as Navient in 2014. What is left is a pure private student lender. Sallie Mae Bank, a Utah industrial bank, made $7.4 billion of private education loans in 2025, held $19.5 billion of them net of reserves at June 30, 2026, and funds them with $19.9 billion of deposits gathered online, without branches.

The loans are underwritten like consumer credit but sold through colleges. In the second quarter of 2026, 84% of new loans had a cosigner, usually a parent, and the average origination FICO score was 755. Because students do not pay while in school, most of the risk shows up years later, when a loan enters repayment. That timing matters for everything below.

Since November 2025 the model has a second leg. KKR agreed to buy at least $2 billion of newly made Sallie Mae loans a year for an initial three years, while Sallie Mae keeps the customer and the servicing and collects fees. Selling loans at a premium frees capital, which the company then hands back through buybacks. In 2025 it sold $4.95 billion of loans and booked $368.9 million of gains.

The chief executive called it "a strong second quarter." The return on common equity was 9.9%, and earnings per share were lower than a year earlier. Management's own explanation is more useful than the adjective: the net interest margin hit its low point for the year because cash from the March loan sale sat idle before the autumn lending season, and the company expects it to recover toward its long-term target of about 5%, though not far above it this year.

How much of the profit is repeatable

A quarterly return on equity that swings from 56.4% to 9.9% is mostly accounting, not luck. Under the current expected credit loss rules (CECL), a lender books the lifetime expected loss on a loan the day it makes it. Sallie Mae makes most of its loans in the third quarter, before the academic year, so that quarter carries the reserve build. When it sells loans, as it did with $3.33 billion in the first quarter, the reserve on those loans is released and the sale gain lands at the same time. First-quarter provision was a credit of $11.5 million.

Gains on loan sales were 37.2% of 2025 pre-tax income. Take them out entirely and 2025 would still have earned about 22% on average common equity (my estimate, at the 2025 tax rate). That is the floor I keep in mind: the lending spread alone clears any reasonable cost of equity by a wide margin. The gains themselves are thinner than they were. The 2025 sales brought about 7.5% over par. The first-quarter 2026 sale, which seeded the KKR program, brought about 4.4%.

The guidance arithmetic is simple. First-half earnings per share were $1.85, so the second half needs $1.25 to $1.35. On my numbers that is achievable without heroics, but it assumes the margin recovers, the peak-season reserve build stays normal and some further loans are sold. My Base uses $600 million of profit to common shareholders over the next 12 months, a 27% return on today's common equity, or $3.15 per diluted share before counting future buybacks. Analyst consensus is $3.12 for 2026 and $3.37 for 2027, and per share figures there include the falling share count.

Capital, funding and shares

Total equity at June 30, 2026 was $2,474.7 million. After $251.1 million of Series B preferred stock, common equity was $2,223.6 million, and after $58.5 million of goodwill and intangibles, tangible common equity was $2,165.1 million. On 188.0 million shares outstanding that is $11.83 of book value and $11.52 of tangible book value per share. On the 190.4 million diluted shares used for second-quarter earnings, it is $11.68 and $11.37. I value the shares on the diluted count.

The bank reported a CET1 ratio of 11.8% and total risk-based capital of 13.1%, with liquidity at 18.6% of total assets. Those ratios are adequate rather than generous. The real cushion is the reserve: the allowance for loan losses was $1,354.0 million, a reserve rate of 5.89% by the company's measure, which is already about 61% of common equity sitting on the balance sheet against future losses. Deposits are online savings and certificates, and they cost money: the cost of funds was 4.13% in both of the first two quarters.

The share count is where the story is told. Management says shares outstanding are down about 59% since 2020, bought back at an average of $17.19. This year it has bought about 13 million shares, 6.5% of the end-2025 count, at an average of $21.95, including a $200 million accelerated repurchase that retired 9.3 million shares and finished in June. Another $242 million of authorization was left at June 30, and the company expects to use most of it in 2026.

Primary valuation: residual income

For a bank, capital is the raw material, so I value the equity directly. Residual income says the shares are worth today's common book value plus the present value of every dollar earned above the cost of equity. A lender that earns exactly its cost of equity is worth book value and nothing more. Sallie Mae earns far more, which is why the result is roughly twice book.

Base case inputs, all my estimates:

  • Return on beginning common equity of 27% in year 1, fading one point a year to 23% in year 5, then 20% in the terminal period. That fade assumes thinner loan sale gains and some competition for PLUS-related borrowers, and the terminal 20% is close to the 22% the lending spread earned in 2025 without any sale gains.
  • Cost of equity of 12%. That is above a typical US bank on purpose: one product, credit risk that peaks years after the loan is made, and a business whose size depends on federal student aid policy.
  • Common equity grows 4% a year. Almost everything else is paid out, mostly through buybacks.
  • Terminal growth of 3%.
  • Starting common equity of $2,223.6 million at June 30, 2026, after preferred stock, on 190.4 million diluted shares.

Secondary valuation: forward earnings

The cross-check is a plain earnings multiple on the same next-12-month profit: $3.15 per diluted share. At 8 times that is $25.23. The 8 times is my estimate, anchored on the stock's own history: the price was between 5.5 and 10.3 times earnings at the last five year ends, with a median of 7.9 times. I could not verify a peer table for monoline consumer lenders on the same period, so the multiple is tested at 6 and 10 times below.

For the scenarios the same method uses $2.10 at 6 times in the Bear (an 18% return on equity) and $3.39 at 10 times in the Bull (29%). That gives $12.61 and $33.87.

Price to book history and growth diagnostics

On tangible common book value, my arithmetic from year-end prices and balance sheets gives 2.89 times at the end of 2021, 2.94 times in 2022, 2.70 times in 2023, 3.14 times in 2024 and 2.52 times in 2025. Today's 1.97 times on outstanding shares is the lowest in that set by a wide margin. Applied to today's diluted tangible book value of $11.37, the range from 2.52 to 2.89 times gives $28.65 to $32.86. I do not weight it, because the returns that justified those multiples, 31% to 36% on common equity in 2023 to 2025, included large loan sale gains that are now smaller.

The growth-adjusted check (PEG) is a diagnostic only. The price is 7.3 times 2026 consensus earnings per share of $3.12. Consensus has 2028 at $3.81, a growth rate of 10.5% a year, which gives a PEG of about 0.7. Most of that growth is a shrinking share count rather than more profit, which is why I do not treat the low PEG as evidence of cheapness. Growth durability: normalizing, with flat profit and per share growth from buybacks, plus a possible lift from federal loan changes. Cyclical distortion: medium, for a real reason. Student loan losses follow graduate employment with a lag, and delinquencies are higher than a year ago. A free cash flow growth check does not apply to a bank.

What is the market already pricing in?

At $22.70 and 190.4 million diluted shares, the market value is $4,322 million, or 1.94 times common book value of $2,223.6 million. In a bank, price to book equals (return on equity minus growth) divided by (cost of equity minus growth). With 3% growth and a 12% cost of equity, 1.94 times means a permanent return on equity of 20.5%. At an 11% cost of equity it is 18.5%, at 13% it is 22.4%.

So the market is not pricing a collapse. It is pricing roughly what Sallie Mae would earn if loan sale gains disappeared and nothing else changed. That is a reasonable, slightly cautious place to be with a lender whose delinquencies rose this year and whose next quarter carries the heaviest reserve build. It is also the main reason my upside is modest: my Base fades the return toward that level rather than holding it at 27%.

How the buy price is set

A Confidence C score carries a base margin of safety of 35% to 50%, and I start at 35%. I add 5 points for credit cyclicality (losses on student loans show up years after origination and rise with graduate unemployment) and 5 points for concentration: one product, one funding model, and a market whose size is set in Washington. No positive modifier applies. The required MOS is 45%, and the maximum buy price is $25.40 times 0.55, or $13.97.

That is just above the Bear value of $13.66, and it looks severe for a company that earned 34.4% on common equity last year. At $13.97 the stock would trade at 1.23 times tangible book value and about 4.4 times my next-year earnings. The margin is meant to cover the case where my Base return on equity is wrong, and for a lender, that case tends to arrive all at once.

Dividend

Sallie Mae pays $0.13 a quarter, $0.52 a year, a 2.3% yield at $22.70 and about 17% of the midpoint of 2026 earnings guidance. The most recent dividend went ex on September 4, 2026. The preferred stock gets paid first, about $14 million a year at the current run rate.

The dividend is not the point of owning this stock. In 2026 so far, buybacks have returned about $285 million against about $50 million of common dividends. If you are buying for income, you are buying the wrong part of the capital return.

Reasons to own SLM

  • The market prices a much lower return than the business earns. 1.94 times book implies about 20.5% on equity. The company reported a 34.4% return on common equity in 2025, and about 22% even without loan sale gains.
  • Federal loan limits changed in its favor. From July 1, 2026, new graduate students cannot take Grad PLUS loans, and Parent PLUS is capped at $20,000 a year and $65,000 per student. Management estimates $4.5 billion to $5 billion of extra yearly originations over several years, against $7.4 billion made in 2025.
  • The share count keeps falling. About 59% of shares retired since 2020 at an average of $17.19, and 6.5% more this year at $21.95.
  • A capital-light outlet for growth. KKR's commitment to buy at least $2 billion a year lets Sallie Mae grow originations without growing its balance sheet at the same pace, and keeps servicing fees.
  • The reserve is already large. An allowance of $1,354.0 million, 5.89% by the company's measure, is booked for lifetime losses before they happen.

What could go wrong?

  • Thesis breaker: credit losses rise with graduate unemployment. Delinquent loans were 3.72% of loans in repayment at June 30, up from 3.51% a year earlier. Charge-offs are guided at $365 million to $385 million for 2026, against $345.7 million in 2025. Each extra $100 million of charge-offs is roughly $75 million after tax, about 12% of my Base profit.
  • Debt-resolution firms. The segment tied to them was 6.4% of gross charge-offs in April. The company has cut back on outside recovery sales and estimated about $25 million of extra 2026 charge-offs if normal practices do not resume. Management calls it timing. If these firms keep growing, it becomes a cost of doing business.
  • Margin. The net interest margin fell to 4.75% in the second quarter. The company expects it to recover toward about 5%, not far above. A further 25 basis points lower on roughly $27 billion of interest-earning assets is close to $70 million a year before tax.
  • Loan sale gains. They were 37.2% of 2025 pre-tax income, and the premium fell from about 7.5% in 2025 to about 4.4% in the first-quarter 2026 sale. A second partnership was expected in the third or early fourth quarter. If buyers of student loan pools demand higher yields, the gains shrink first.
  • Policy runs both ways. The 2025 federal law helped Sallie Mae, but student lending is politically exposed. Rate caps, bankruptcy changes for private student loans or tougher consumer finance oversight could all reverse part of that gain.
  • Funding. Online deposits are rate-sensitive and cost 4.13%. If competition for deposits rises, the margin pays for it.

Management execution

Jonathan Witter has been chief executive since 2020. The capital record is the strongest part of his tenure: buying back more than half the company at an average of $17.19 has been worth far more to shareholders than any dividend, and the KKR deal turned loan sales from a one-off into a program. The communication record is weaker. The 2026 earnings guidance moved from $2.70 to $2.80 in January to $3.10 to $3.20, helped by a large first-quarter sale, and the debt-resolution problem, flagged at an investor conference in June, cost the stock 7.6% in one session. Assessment: 14/20, excellent with capital, less predictable on credit.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Delinquent loans rise above 4.5% of loans in repayment and 2027 charge-off guidance moves above $450 million, taking the return on equity toward 18%.
  2. The federal loan changes fail to lift originations: full-year 2026 growth below 5% and no acceleration in 2027.
  3. The loan sale market closes or reprices: gains below 3% of loans sold, or KKR buys less than its $2 billion minimum, which would leave capital trapped on the balance sheet and slow the buybacks.

Final verdict: hold at $22.70

Sallie Mae is a highly profitable lender with a disciplined capital return and a new tailwind from federal loan limits, and the market prices it as if its return on equity drops to about 20% for good. That discount is not foolish. It reflects credit losses that are rising, a margin at its low point for the year and profits that lean on selling loans to private credit buyers.

Verdict: HOLD at $22.70, watchlist for new money. Triangulated Base value $25.40, upside 11.9%, Confidence C (16/25), required margin of safety 45%, maximum buy price $13.97.

The main risk to this verdict is a third quarter that brings the margin back near 5%, originations up double digits and charge-offs inside guidance, which would put the consensus-like $27.79 within reach. The best borrowers going bad is a strange problem to have. Until it shrinks, expect the market to keep pricing Sallie Mae like an ordinary lender.
