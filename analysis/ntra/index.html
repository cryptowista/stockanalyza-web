On November 5 Natera reported revenue of $794 million, about $86 million above what analysts expected, and raised its 2026 revenue guidance for the third time. The shares fell 7% that day and 14.6% the next, and have lost 37% since the October 29 close of $436. At $275.94 they still value the company at $40 billion, which is 13 times this year's revenue for a business that still loses money after stock pay.

Natera is a real business growing at a pace few companies reach at $3 billion of revenue: oncology test volume rose 61% and the clinical volume of its cancer recurrence test grew 56% in a year. My Base case lets that continue. Revenue grows from $3.03 billion in 2026 to $6.9 billion in 2031, the operating margin after stock pay goes from -9% today to 20% in 2031 and 27% at maturity, and the weighted value is $102.51, which is 63% below the price. The Bear value of $21.66 assumes slower growth and a 14% mature margin. The Bull value of $220.63 assumes management reaches its $6 billion 2029 revenue target and a 35% margin, and it is still 20% below the price. I assign no probabilities. Confidence is grade C, so the required margin of safety is 42.5% and the maximum buy price is $58.94. The framework calls a stock highly overvalued at $133.27, 30% above Base; the price is 2.1 times that line.

Valuation route: a high-growth diagnostics company that is not yet profitable after stock pay, valued on owner free cash flow: operating profit after stock pay, less cash tax, plus depreciation, less capital spending and the working capital that growth ties up. Because the cash flow is negative today, both methods rest on my path to profitability. Primary method: a ten-year path with a terminal value, discounted at 10%. Cross-check: a multiple of 2031 owner free cash flow. Both are tested against a reverse valuation. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

What Natera is

Natera, based in Austin, Texas, sells blood tests built on cell-free DNA, the fragments of DNA that cells shed into the bloodstream. Its tests serve three areas: oncology, women's health (prenatal screening) and organ health (transplant rejection). Oncology is the growth engine. Signatera, its test for molecular residual disease (MRD), looks for tiny traces of a patient's own tumor DNA after treatment to flag a recurrence early.

In the third quarter Natera processed about 1.09 million tests, up 22%, of which 339,500 were oncology tests. Revenue grew faster than volume, 34%, because the average price per Signatera test rose to about $1,290 from $1,271 in the previous quarter. The company does not report revenue by area, so I cannot show how much of the $794 million came from oncology. Traditional Medicare paid for 16.9% of third-quarter revenue, up from 13.6% a year earlier, and no single customer exceeded 10%.

The company reports a gross margin of 64.7%, but $32.3 million of that quarter's revenue came from revisions to estimates of how much it will collect on earlier tests (the "true-ups"). Without them the gross margin was 63.2%, up about 1.4 points from the second quarter. That is the number I use as the base margin. On the call the new chief executive said the $6 billion 2029 target needs only limited help from early cancer screening, and that Signatera pricing would not need to rise much beyond about $1,450. My Base case includes no value for screening products.

Owner free cash flow

Natera reported $14.7 million of free cash flow in the first nine months of 2026, the difference between $138.9 million of operating cash flow and $124.2 million of capital spending. That figure excludes stock pay, which is a real cost paid in shares: $304 million in the same nine months. After it, free cash flow was about -$290 million. The company guides to positive cash flow for 2026 without an amount; that is a cash measure, before stock pay.

I start from GAAP operating profit, which already deducts stock pay. Cash tax follows a path: none in 2027 and 2028, 5% in 2029, 10% in 2030 and 15% in 2031, then 21%, because the company has large past losses; I did not retrieve the size of its loss carryforwards, so this path is my estimate and I test a version with full tax from the first profit. I add depreciation (2.5% of revenue; $46 million in nine months), subtract capital spending of 5.0% of revenue (5.5% in the nine months) and subtract 12% of every extra dollar of revenue for receivables and inventory, net of payables. Receivables were $471 million at September 30 against $297 million at the end of 2025.

Dividend and share count

Natera pays no dividend and I found no buyback program. The company grows its share count instead: 3.3% in nine months, with stock pay at 13.5% of revenue. I count the stock pay as a cost, so the dilution is already in the cash flow and I do not subtract it twice. If stock pay falls toward 7% of revenue as revenue grows, which a path to a 27% margin implicitly requires, the dilution rate should fall with it; if it stays near 13%, the margin I need is harder to reach.

Volatility and options

The 30-day implied volatility was 56.8% on November 6 (Market Chameleon), which implies a typical move of about 3.6% a day; I do not have a current figure. The history is more useful: a rise of 21.4% on August 7, a fall of 14.6% on November 6, and falls of 8.5%, 7.0% and 5.0% on other days in the last three weeks (Trefis). Expect moves of 5% to 10% in a day on news about reimbursement, competitors or the result of a trial, not only on Natera's own results.

Reasons to own Natera

  • Demand is strong: oncology test volume up 61%, clinical MRD volume up 56%, and more than 30,000 clinical MRD units added in each of the last two quarters.
  • Pricing is moving up: the Signatera price per test rose to about $1,290, and Medicare added coverage for liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma) on November 3.
  • Guidance has risen three times in 2026, and the company beat the third-quarter revenue estimate by 12%.
  • The balance sheet has $1.1 billion of cash and almost no debt, so the company does not need to raise money to reach profit on its plan.
  • Early cancer screening and drug-company partnerships are options that I value at zero.

What could go wrong?

  • Valuation. The price needs a mature margin of about 56% in my model; reaching my 27% leaves the shares far below today's price.
  • Profit. The company lost money after stock pay in every period I looked at (2025 and 2026), and operating costs are growing 10% to 12% next year on top of 19.5% this quarter.
  • Revenue quality. $32.3 million of third-quarter revenue came from changes in collection estimates; that benefit was $55.1 million a year ago, and the company says its estimates now track collections more closely, so it should keep shrinking.
  • Payers. Medicare paid 16.9% of third-quarter revenue and coverage and prices can change; I did not see the pricing terms for commercial insurers.
  • Competition. Guardant reported revenue up 36% to $361 million and raised guidance the same evening; MRD testing is an area where large laboratories are investing.
  • Legal. Natera is in a false advertising dispute with Guardant in California and a long-running one with CareDx, where the appeals court left Natera with no damages to pay but kept liability for false advertising in place (Bloomberg Law). I did not read the full legal note.
  • Leadership. A chief executive change at the moment spending is rising; the successor has been at the company, which lowers the risk, but he has not run it through a downturn.
  • Data gaps. Revenue by test area, unvested stock awards, the size of loss carryforwards and the detail of the cash flow guidance were not available to me.

Management execution

Steve Chapman led Natera for almost eight years, through the move from prenatal testing to a company with a leading cancer recurrence test, and the company has raised its revenue guidance three times this year. The offsets: costs have grown faster than revenue in the latest quarter, the share count is rising, and the departure comes the same night as a larger spending plan. Solomon Moshkevich has been president of clinical diagnostics, so the plan continues, and he has set a target of $6 billion of revenue in 2029 and profit after it. Assessment: 14/20, strong on growth and execution, not yet proven on profit and share count. The next test is the fourth-quarter report in late February, which should include the first 2027 guidance.

What I would watch from here

The next results are for the fourth quarter, expected in late February 2027 (the date is not confirmed by the company). They need to show revenue near the top of the guided range, a gross margin without true-ups above 63% and a first guide for 2027 operating costs.

Green: moves me toward the buy zone

  • Operating margin after stock pay turning positive before 2028, with cost growth below 10%.
  • Gross margin without true-ups above 65%.
  • A price at or below $58.94, or evidence of cash flow after stock pay above $500 million a year.

Yellow

  • Costs growing faster than my 10% to 12% for 2027.
  • Share count rising by more than 3% a year.
  • Signatera price per test flat or down from about $1,290.

Red

  • Clinical MRD volume growing less than 40%, or a sequential decline.
  • A cut in Medicare or commercial payment rates for MRD tests.
  • A material adverse ruling in the patent or advertising cases.

Today demand and the balance sheet are green; margins, dilution and leadership are yellow, and the price is red. That is why the verdict is reduce: the company has answered the growth question, and the shares ask it to answer a profit question that has not been asked yet.

The three things that would make this verdict wrong

  1. A margin far above my path. At 10 points above my Base in every year the value is $163.03, and the price needs 29 points. That is a Bull case with a higher margin than I can support.
  2. Faster growth, or a new product. At 3 points more growth every year the Base is $121.34. Early cancer screening, which I value at zero, is the most likely source, and I cannot size it from the filings.
  3. A lower discount rate. At 5.1% my Base equals the price; at 9% it is $116.17. A stock that has fallen 37% in three weeks does not look like a 5% asset.

Together the three could bridge the gap. My rules ask for a margin of safety, not for a combination of the best cases.
