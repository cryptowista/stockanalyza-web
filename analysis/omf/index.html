OneMain's credit is improving, but at $57.23 the price already pays for the recovery

OneMain lends to Americans who cannot borrow from a bank, charges them high rates and returns roughly 11% of its market value a year in dividends and buybacks. Its third quarter was better than the second: charge-offs fell from 7.77% to 6.89% and management expects lower losses again in 2027. The stock reacted by rising, and it now sits 10.3% above my Base value. The business is not the problem. The problem is that a lender earning 22% on equity is priced for 20% to continue, and the Bear value of $31.78 shows what a normal credit downturn does to a company that borrows $24.4 billion to make its loans.

The Bear value is $31.78 and the Bull value is $67.47, so the price sits 80% above Bear and 15% below Bull. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 30%, so the maximum buy price is $36.33. The shares are 58% above that and would need to fall 36.5% to reach it.

Valuation route: a nonprime consumer lender funded by debt, not deposits, so the cash that matters is what can be paid to shareholders after the loan book is grown. The primary method is a ten-year discounted stream of distributable earnings (net income minus the equity retained to grow receivables), plus a terminal value. The cross-check is five years of the same stream plus a discounted exit at a multiple of earnings. The discount rate is 12%. Economic classification: Standard. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

What OneMain is

OneMain makes personal loans to nonprime customers through branches and a digital channel, and it has added auto loans and a credit card around that core. At September 30 managed receivables were $28.1 billion, up 9% from a year earlier. Originations were $4.5 billion in the quarter, up 17%, and about 60% of personal loan originations were secured, which carries lower losses. Auto receivables grew 16% to $3.2 billion, and the card book has 1.5 million accounts and $1.4 billion of receivables.

It is not a bank. It funds itself with $24.4 billion of principal debt, 52% of it secured, and its corporate ratings are below investment grade. In August it raised $2.3 billion in three issues on one day at what management called attractive pricing. Bank measures such as deposit ratios do not apply, and free cash flow is not a useful anchor, because loans are the raw material of the business. The right anchor is return on equity after real charge-offs and the capital the company has to keep against its loans.

The stock has been range-bound. The record close was $71.37 on January 6, 2026, the 52-week low is $45.78, and the shares have traded between about $54 and $58 since the third-quarter report on October 30. At $57.23 they are 20% below the January peak.

The headline is credit. The charge-off ratio fell 88 basis points in a quarter, and management said that 2027 losses on consumer loans could approach about 7%, the top of what it calls a normalized range, if the economy holds. Delinquency is lower than a year ago, though it rose from the second quarter, which is a normal seasonal pattern I did not test. Earnings per share were lower than a year ago because last year's adjusted figure was unusually high, and the underlying earnings power, measured by capital generation, grew 4%.

Liquidity is strong: $936 million of cash (of which $268 million sits in insurance subsidiaries or is otherwise unavailable), a $1.0 billion undrawn corporate revolver and $6.5 billion of undrawn committed funding. Net leverage is 5.5 times. Management said the $4.20 dividend will be maintained and that extra capital returns will lean toward buybacks. The company cited a roughly 20% return on equity target across its products.

Why I value distributable earnings, not reported earnings alone

A lender that grows its loan book has to keep capital against the new loans. At 5.5 times leverage, each extra dollar of receivables needs about 12 to 15 cents of equity. If receivables grow 8% a year, a company earning 22% on equity can distribute only about 14% of its equity each year. That is why I take net income minus growth in equity, which I call distributable earnings, as the cash that belongs to shareholders. It is also why a high dividend and a high growth rate cannot both be assumed.

The model uses ending September 30 equity of $3,391 million and 114.46 million shares (a third-party count; the weighted diluted count in the quarter was 115.5 million, and using it would lower the Base by 0.9%). Book value per share is $29.63 on my share count. Buybacks are not credited separately: they are part of the distributable stream, and at a fair price they do not change the value per share.

Primary valuation: ten years of distributable earnings

The primary method discounts the stub quarter of 2026 and the ten years from 2027 to 2036 at 12%. That is two points above the framework default of 10%, because the company is leveraged 5.5 times and funded below investment grade. I charge that risk once, in the discount rate, and I test 11% and 13%.

Base return on equity starts at 22%, close to the recent run rate (the nine-month figure annualizes to about 22%), and fades to 18% by 2032: 22%, 21.5%, 20.5%, 19.5%, 18.5%, then 18%. Fading matters because card yields are near their likely peak, charge-offs are at a good point in the cycle and competition for nonprime borrowers is real. Receivables growth slows from 8% in 2027 to 3% by 2033. On that path net income is $761 million in 2027, or $6.65 a share, about $7.20 a share in 2030 to 2031 and $8.23 in 2036. Distributable earnings are $4.23 a share in 2027, rising to $6.86 in 2036. The 2027 distributable amount covers the $4.20 dividend but not the dividend and the buybacks together, so continued buybacks would need either lower growth or more leverage.

The terminal value assumes that in 2036 the company earns 17% on equity and grows 3% a year. That is a price to book multiple of 1.56 times (17% minus 3%, divided by 12% minus 3%) on a book value of $47.09 a share. The ten years of distributable earnings are worth about $31 a share and the terminal value about $23, so the primary value is $53.83.

Terminal value share: 43% of the primary value, at a ten-year horizon, well below the 75% level where I would check that the terminal value is supported by sustainable flows.

Secondary valuation: five years of cash and an exit at 8.0 times earnings

The cross-check takes the stub quarter and 2027 to 2031 distributable earnings and values the company at the end of 2031 at 8.0 times that year's net income of $823 million ($7.19 a share). Discounted at 12%, the five years add $18.24 and the exit adds $31.73. The secondary value is $49.97.

Why 8.0 times. In 2031 my Base has the company earning 18.5% on equity with receivables growth of 4% and credit past its best. For that profile a P/E of 8.0 corresponds to 1.44 times book. It is close to where the stock trades today, at 8.8 times annualized earnings. Worked backward, 8.0 times and an 18% return on equity at a 12% cost of equity imply earnings that shrink about 1.6% a year forever, which is deliberately cautious for a growing franchise and consistent with a market that discounts lenders for credit risk. Bear uses 6.0 times, a lender in a recovering credit cycle, and Bull uses 10.0 times, a lender earning more than 20% on equity.

Exit value share: 64% of the secondary value comes from the exit, at a horizon of five years, below the 85% level where I check that the exit is supported by sustainable flows.

What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $57.23 needs one of these: a return on equity 2.1 points higher than my Base in every year (a flat 20.4% for ever), a discount rate of 10.8% instead of 12%, distributable earnings 22% higher in every year, or, on the cross-check method alone, an exit multiple of 9.8 times. On the primary method alone, a terminal return on equity of 19.1% instead of 17% is needed. The market is therefore pricing in a 20% return on equity that does not fade, which is what management targets and what the last twelve months delivered (about 22%).

As a return, buying at $57.23 and holding the Base case to the end of 2031, with distributable earnings added up and an exit at 8.0 times 2031 earnings, gives about 7.9% a year, below the 12% cost of equity. The same math gives -4.5% a year in Bear and 16.0% in Bull. The dividend alone is a 7.3% yield, so the Base return is mostly the dividend and little else.

On earnings the shares trade at 8.8 times the annualized first nine months and 8.6 times my 2027 estimate. The growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) is not meaningful here, because 2027 growth in my Base is 3% to 4% from a low-credit-loss base and a PEG would reward a recovery that is already in the price. Growth durability: cyclical normalization, with moderate distortion from low charge-offs. The free cash flow growth check does not apply to a lender.

How the buy price is set

Confidence B starts at a 30% margin of safety. I added nothing, because each risk is charged once: leverage and below-investment-grade funding are in the 12% cost of equity, the credit cycle is in the Bear scenario and regulatory and forward-evidence risk is in the Confidence score. The two methods differ by 7.4%, so no divergence modifier applies. A 30% discount to the $51.90 Base gives a maximum buy price of $36.33. The framework's valuation gap puts the stock in the fairly valued range (price within 15% of Base), which maps to a Hold. A single input moving by 15% of its own size, such as the 12% cost of equity moving to 13.8%, changes the Base by about 12%, so the Base is not hostage to one assumption, though a return on equity 4 points lower every year lowers it by 19.7%.

What the options market implies

I did not obtain a current options chain for OneMain, so I draw no conclusion about implied volatility or tail risk, and the options check is not supported. The share price has moved between $54 and $58 for a month after the quarter, which says the market has not changed its mind either way.

Dividend and buybacks

The quarterly dividend is $1.05, or $4.20 a year, a 7.3% yield at $57.23. The November 13 payment has been made. On my 2027 earnings of $6.65 the payout is 63%. Management says the dividend will be maintained and extra returns will lean toward buybacks, which were $171 million in the first nine months at an average price in the low $60s, above today's price. Dividends and buybacks together are about 11% of market value a year, but a lender returns capital only while credit and funding allow it. In my Bear case distributable earnings fall to $2.83 a share in 2028, below the dividend, and a cut would follow.

Reasons to own OMF

  • Credit is improving. The consumer charge-off ratio fell from 7.77% to 6.89% in a quarter, and management expects lower losses in 2027.
  • High returns on equity. The run rate is about 22%, against a 12% cost of equity.
  • Cash returned. A 7.3% dividend plus buybacks of about 3.5% of market value a year.
  • Funding and liquidity. $24.4 billion of debt, 52% of it secured, $6.5 billion of undrawn committed funding and a $2.3 billion August financing at tight spreads.
  • Growth options. Receivables grew 9%, auto grew 16% and cards reached $1.4 billion.

What could go wrong?

  • Credit. Nonprime borrowers default more in a recession. Charge-offs at 10% or more would cut return on equity to single digits, as in my Bear case.
  • Funding. The company depends on capital markets and is rated below investment grade. A funding shock hits earnings and the ability to pay out capital together.
  • Regulation. Consumer lending rates, fees and collection practices are regulated. I did not review current regulatory actions for this analysis.
  • Card economics. Card revenue yield is 34.9%, which management says is near its peak, and the target loss rate of 15% to 17% is not yet tested in a downturn.
  • Pro-cyclical capital returns. Dividends and buybacks are largest when credit is good and the stock is high, and the average buyback price this year is above today's price.

Management execution

Management, led by Chairman and CEO Doug Shulman, has delivered improving credit through the second half, funded the business at tight spreads, held the dividend and reduced the share count (the diluted count fell from 119.4 million to 115.8 million in the year to the second quarter and was 115.5 million in the third). Against that, adjusted earnings per share fell 21% from last year's quarter, buybacks have been made at prices above today's, and the real test of the credit tightening is still ahead, in a downturn. Management credibility is 14/20: good.

Debt and funding

Principal debt is $24.4 billion, 52% secured, and the company reports $936 million of cash, a $1.0 billion undrawn revolver and $6.5 billion of undrawn committed funding. I did not find a maturity schedule in the sources I read, so I do not build a refinancing table. Maturities within 36 months are therefore an open data gap, and I treated funding risk through the cost of equity and the Bear case. The August financing of $2.3 billion shows that the market was open at tight spreads as recently as three months ago.

What I would watch from here

Green

  • Consumer loan net charge-offs at or below 7% for a second quarter, as management projects for 2027
  • Consumer loan 30+ delinquency below 5.3% and falling year on year
  • Capital generation above $284 million a quarter and book value per share rising
  • Unchanged or better ratings, and funding issued at similar spreads

Yellow

  • Charge-offs of 7% to 8.5%, or delinquency of 5.5% to 6.5%
  • Return on equity of 15% to 20%
  • Card loss rates running above the 15% to 17% target
  • A negative ratings outlook, or buybacks above distributable earnings

Red

  • Charge-offs above 9.5%, or delinquency above 6.5%
  • Return on equity below 15%, or book value per share falling for two quarters
  • A downgrade together with wider funding spreads
  • A dividend cut driven by credit or capital

The next review point is the fourth-quarter report in early 2027, with management's first 2027 guidance. Better results raise the Base only if they lift the return on equity that lasts past 2028.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Charge-offs return above 9.5% as the economy weakens, which is the Bear path.
  2. A funding shock that raises borrowing costs while the book is still growing.
  3. Return on equity settling below 15%, which would make the 1.9 times book multiple hard to support.

Stacking audit. Combining every generous choice (11% cost of equity, return on equity 2 points higher, a 9.0x exit and 4% terminal growth) gives $64.81. Combining every conservative one (13%, return 2 points lower, a 7.0x exit, 2% terminal growth and distributable earnings 20% lower) gives $37.29. The price of $57.23 is inside that range, and the Base of $51.90 sits 53% of the way up it. The choices that lean conservative are the fading return on equity, no credit for buybacks beyond the distributable stream and a 12% cost of equity. The ones that lean generous are the assumption that charge-offs do not repeat the 2023 peak in the Base and a terminal return of 17%, well above the cost of equity.

The strongest counter-argument is that my Base is too low: the nine-month return is about 22%, management targets about 20%, and delinquency is falling. If return on equity stays near 22% and the market pays 1.9 to 2.2 times book, the shares are worth about $57 to $65. That case is credible and sits inside the Bull range, but it needs benign credit and funding to continue, and the price already pays for most of it.

Final verdict: hold at $57.23, watchlist for new money

OneMain is a well-run lender with improving credit, a 7.3% dividend and a falling share count, and at $57.23 it trades 10.3% above my Base value of $51.90. That is a reason to keep a position you already hold, not to add: Confidence is B, the Bear case loses 44.5% and the framework asks for a 30% margin of safety, which sets the buy price at $36.33.

VERDICT: HOLD (WATCHLIST FOR NEW MONEY) at $57.23; Triangulated Base $51.90; upside -9.3%; required MOS 30%; maximum buy price $36.33. The main thesis breaker is a recession that sends charge-offs back above 9.5% while the loan book is still growing.

New money waits for $36.33 or lower, or for evidence that a 20% return on equity survives a weaker economy. The next test is the fourth-quarter report: if charge-offs hold near 7% and management guides to lower losses in 2027, the Base gets safer, and if delinquency turns up, the Bear becomes the case to price.
