What Concentrix is

Concentrix is a global provider of customer experience services: it runs support, sales and back-office work for more than 2,000 clients, mostly through large teams of agents, plus technology and specialist services such as risk and compliance. It was separated from SYNNEX in December 2020 and combined with Webhelp in September 2023. Revenue was $9.8 billion in fiscal 2025, and the five largest clients were about 19% of it. The company has said roughly half of expected 2026 revenue comes from a broad basket of new and AI-influenced services. That basket includes work won since 2023 and existing revenue influenced by its tools, so it is not standalone software revenue, and I do not value Concentrix like a software company.

The business has real advantages in scale, languages, compliance and relationships with large buyers, and I score its moat 13 of 20. The weakness is that clients can rebid, split programs, automate or move work offshore, and some contracts can be ended for convenience. The risk is not that the company disappears. The risk is that automation lowers the price of the work faster than new services replace it.

Which cash to value

A low price to free cash flow screen can mislead here. I value owner cash: operating cash flow after interest, tax, restructuring and operating rent, less all capital spending, less the noncash stock pay that the company would otherwise have to replace. Goodwill write-downs are not deducted again. Pension deficit repair and a cross-currency hedge settlement are subtracted separately once, at the end of the model.

FY2023 includes only part of the year after the Webhelp deal. Three items could flatter the headline cash: receivables sold to banks (the sold balance was about $98 million in August, against $119 million in November), a securitization facility that is debt and not cash flow ($620.5 million outstanding), and about $199 million of restricted cash held for clients, which I exclude. The share count matters. Restricted stock and units carry dividend rights and share in positive earnings, so the loss-period count of 61.0 million understates the claims. I use 64.3 million economic shares: 61.105 million ordinary shares at September 30 divided by 95.1%, plus a small buffer. This counts the existing awards once. I then charge $100 million a year in the cash flow as the cost of replacing future stock pay, and I test $60 million and $140 million.

The dividend

The $1.48 dividend costs about $95.2 million a year on 64.3 million economic shares. Last twelve months owner cash covers it 5.09 times, and Base fiscal 2028 owner cash covers it 5.65 times, an economic payout of 17.7%. Unlike a company paying more than it earns, Concentrix can afford the dividend on these numbers. The quarterly rate rose from $0.25 in 2021 to $0.37, a short history and not a long record. The risk is that lenders, refinancing or earnings pressure take priority over it, not that today's cash falls short. October 23 is the record date, and buying before it is not free return, because the price adjusts.

Reasons to own

  • Scale and relationships. Long client relationships and global delivery give real, if not exclusive, switching costs.
  • Cash is real despite the loss. Operating cash of $442.9 million in nine months and $484.5 million of owner cash over twelve months, after stock pay and all capital spending.
  • Debt repayment feeds the equity. Net debt is falling, and every dollar repaid lowers interest. Repurchases were paused in the third quarter.
  • The price may assume too much decline. A 21% permanent EBITDA cut already explains the quote.

Main risks

  • Automation and offshore moves. The company sees about 10% of 2027 revenue as potentially movable offshore, and technology and consumer electronics revenue fell about 10% in the quarter. Management expects a 3% to 5% constant-currency decline in the fourth quarter.
  • Leverage and maturities. The 2028 maturities are large, and a smaller earnings base can tighten terms even with positive cash flow. The Bear needs fresh equity.
  • Quality of adjusted numbers. Restructuring recurs, adjusted earnings repeatedly missed early targets, and factoring and seasonality can distort cash.
  • Capital allocation. Past acquisitions have been written down, and new deals or buybacks compete with debt repayment.

How I checked the model

I rebuilt the model in a separate script, solving the debt and interest loop each year. The Base reproduced: owner cash of $140.8 million, $511.6 million and $537.9 million, closing 2028 debt of $3,304.7 million, the primary value of $60.23, the secondary of $43.71 and the triangulated $53.62, with 31.8% divergence and a ceiling of $26.81. The Bear reproduced at $8.84 and $5.94, the discount rate and multiple grid matched, and so did the reverse solutions ($496 million, 2.48 times, $277 million, 16.6%), the 15% and 20% required-return entries of $50.75 and $46.46, and the Base return of 53.7%. In the Bull, the primary value of $115.42 matches and the combined value is $108.44 when I assume depreciation of $230 million, which is not stated in the source. Small differences remain in the stock pay test and the optimistic reasonableness case, where I get about $57.8 against $57.73. I checked the third quarter results, guidance, dividend, share count, cash, debt, nine-month cash flow, goodwill write-down and share repurchases against the company's release and its quarterly filing.

Main data limits: the October debt and cash are my estimates and no balance sheet exists for that date. The December maturity, covenant limits, liquidity, pension gap, lease cash and the economic share count come from the filing and my calculations, and I did not recheck them all against the full 10-Q. The CastleHill price is undisclosed. The $27.86 quote could not be matched with a second source at that minute: the previous close was $27.87, and a later quote seven minutes after showed $27.81. That difference does not change the verdict. The vertical revenue splits, the 10% offshore figure, January guidance, past year-end share prices, the one peer comparison (TTEC, a weaker company, given no weight) and rounded earnings estimates come from third-party pages. Aggregated consensus revenue sits above the company's guidance range, and its cash estimate cannot be reconciled with it, so I do not use either.

Final verdict: hold at $27.86

Concentrix looks inexpensive on rebuilt owner cash, and an accounting loss caused by a noncash write-down is no reason to reject it. But the discount of 48.0% is smaller than the 50% I require at Confidence D, the two methods disagree by 31.8%, and the Bear case has little equity protection.

Verdict: HOLD at $27.86. Triangulated Base value $53.62, upside 92.5%, Confidence D (12/25), required margin of safety 50%, maximum buy price $26.81, one-input range $19.87 to $29.51.

For current holders this means keeping the position and watching the cash. For new money it means waiting for a price at or below $26.81 together with evidence of stable owner cash and funded maturities.
