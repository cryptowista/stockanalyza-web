In the quarter to August 31, 2026 Applied Digital booked $77.4 million of interest expense and $65.8 million of base rent. The company has $36 billion of lease contracts, and 250 MW of the 1.41 GW under lease is live. Debt is $6.4 billion, the company spent $2.1 billion on construction in that one quarter, and its chief executive says about 800 MW still has to be financed over the next 12 months. At $11.45 the shares are down 77% from their May 28 close of $49.65.

Contracted revenue is not income. The $36 billion is base-term rent over 15 years, about $2.4 billion a year once every building is delivered. Until then the company pays interest on money it has already borrowed. The stock fell on no news of its own in November: a leadership change at Nvidia, higher borrowing costs for AI data centers and a soft outlook from neocloud peer Nebius pulled it down with the rest of the group.

My Base value is $13.66, which is 19.3% above the price. The Bear value of $0.06 assumes that only the 800 MW already financed gets built, and equity is worth almost nothing after debt and preferred claims. The Bull value of $27.42 is 139% above the price and needs 1.7 GW at a higher multiple. The probability-weighted value, with 25% on Bear, 50% on Base and 25% on Bull, is $13.70. At Confidence C and with leverage, financing and tenant concentration all adding to the required margin, my rules ask for 57.5%, which gives a maximum buy price of $5.81. The verdict is Hold for existing holders, and new money should wait for either a lower price or the financing to close.

Valuation route: a developer and owner of long-lease AI data centers that is not yet profitable, so I value it on EBITDA once the contracted capacity is built, discounted back, with a ten-year cash flow model that includes all remaining construction spending as the second method. Economic classification: Standard (3/12). Values are present fair value estimates, not 12 month price targets.

What Applied Digital is

Applied Digital designs, builds and operates large data centers for AI computing and leases them to tenants on long contracts. It earns rent, not computing fees. Its first campus, Polaris Forge 1 in North Dakota, is leased to CoreWeave. Four newer campuses are leased to what it calls tier-one, investment-grade hyperscalers: Polaris Forge 2 and 3 in North Dakota, Delta Forge 1 in Louisiana and Delta Forge 2 in Alabama, which carries a 210 MW, 15-year lease signed in June 2026 worth about $5.2 billion.

Two tenants hold most of the backlog. A third-party summary of the leases puts one hyperscaler at about $20.2 billion and CoreWeave at about $11 billion of the $36.2 billion, which is roughly 86% together. The company also operates two older hosting sites in North Dakota with 286 MW, which earn about $150 million of revenue a year, and owns about 96% of ChronoScale, a cloud services business that is consolidated but excluded from the company's adjusted figures.

The revenue is real but not all of it is rent. Of the $262.6 million of HPC hosting revenue, $183.5 million was tenant fit-out services, which the company passes through at a thin margin, and $65.8 million was base rent. The 89% margin on rent is the business that matters, and at the quarter's run rate it earned about $235 million of net operating income a year. The $59.4 million of stock-based compensation, which the company adds back, is a real cost to shareholders, and I deduct it in my view of earnings power.

The company is loss-making, so its tax line says little about future profit. I use a 12% cash tax rate on stabilized profit, on my assumption that depreciation shelters most of it.

Capital structure and share count

At August 31 the company held $2,949.9 million of cash and $313.4 million of restricted cash against $6,376.6 million of debt and $45.9 million of finance leases, so net debt is about $3.16 billion. Redeemable noncontrolling interests of $2,010.9 million sit on top, together with $181.3 million of convertible preferred shares, giving $2.2 billion of claims that rank ahead of common shareholders. The release states $3.7 billion of cash including restricted cash, while the balance sheet adds to $3.26 billion, a gap I could not reconcile and which I do not rely on.

There were 297.1 million shares outstanding. I use 315.1 million diluted shares, which adds an estimated 18 million for the convertible preferred and unvested awards. Future financing needs new shares as well, and I model them separately. The preferred equity from the project financing partner on the newer campuses reportedly carries a 12.75% rate, which is expensive money that does not appear in the share count.

Debt matures late: over 80% of principal falls due in fiscal 2031 or later. The most recent issue, $1.59 billion of 7.000% senior secured notes due 2031, priced well below the 9.25% on an earlier tranche. That is the company's best evidence that lenders will fund the contracted campuses.

Primary valuation: stabilized EBITDA

Once the 1.41 GW under lease is built, rent is about $1.70 million per MW a year at the average base-term rate. At an 89% margin, net of $150 million of corporate cost and with $100 million from the older hosting sites, stabilized EBITDA is $2,086 million. I give it 11.0x, a multiple that implies about a 9% EBITDA yield for assets with 15-year leases, no proven escalators and a tenant mix that includes one lower-rated tenant. The multiple implies that EBITDA stays flat after the base term, which is a conservative reading of the renewal options the company cites.

The remaining construction cost is $9,510 million at $11.0 million per MW, which is my estimate because the company does not disclose it, minus $6.0 billion already spent. After using the $3.3 billion of cash on hand and adding $0.9 billion of carry cost, the funding need is $7,147 million. I assume 65% project debt, 20% preferred equity at 12% and 15% new common equity at $13.00 a share, which means 82 million new shares. Stabilized equity value is $20.34 per share at mid-2029. Discounting at 12% for 2.5 years gives $15.32. Terminal value is not a separate step here, because the whole value sits in the stabilized year.

Secondary valuation: ten-year cash flow model

The second method forecasts EBITDA as buildings go live, subtracts all remaining construction spending, maintenance spending of 0.8% of cost and a 12% tax on profit, and discounts the unlevered cash flows at 9.5%. After year ten I apply 8.5x to the last EBITDA. Net debt and the $2.2 billion of preferred claims at today's balance sheet are then subtracted. This gives $11.17 per share, with 81% of the value coming from the terminal value 10 years out. That is above my 75% attention level, and it is the price of a business whose leases run 15 years and whose building costs come first. I disclose the share rather than penalize it twice, because the risk is already in the Bear case and the margin of safety.

The EBITDA path in $ million is 632, 1,389, 1,919, 2,086 for FY27 to FY30, then flat. The multiple method assumes new equity is issued at $13.00, while the cash flow model funds construction at a fair cost of capital and issues no discount shares. This is the main reason the two methods differ.

Valuation triangulation

The two methods give $15.32 and $11.17, a divergence of 31%, which is above my 30% flag. Both start from the same lease revenue and the same cost per MW, so agreement between them would not be independent evidence, and the gap is a guide to the financing assumptions. I weight the stabilized method 60% and the cash flow model 40%, because the first captures the financing mix that the company actually announces and the second ignores it. The weighted value is $13.66, a point inside the range, not a narrowing of it. Confidence loses one point for the divergence, and the margin of safety does not rise a second time for the same reason.

What is the market already pricing in?

At $11.45 the market value of the 315.1 million diluted shares is $3,608 million. Adding net debt and preferred claims gives an enterprise value of $8,959 million, and adding the $6,247 million of construction spending still to be funded gives $15,206 million. That is 7.3 times stabilized EBITDA of $2,086 million. Holding my other Base inputs, the primary method gives the price at a multiple of 10.1x. So the market is not paying for growth beyond the leases: it is doubting that the leases get built and financed on my terms. If everything goes my Base way, the model return from $11.45 to the stabilized value is 26% a year over 2.5 years. In the Bear case it is a total loss, and in the Bull case it is 71% a year.

How the buy price is set

The base for the margin of safety is 42.5% at Confidence C. I added five points for leverage, five for tenant concentration and five for the binary risk that the 800 MW is not financed, which gives 57.5%. The leverage and financing risk are charged here and not again in the scenarios beyond the Bear case. Divergence between the methods is charged in Confidence, not here. The maximum buy price is $13.66 times (1 - 57.5%), which is $5.81, or $4.75 to $6.51 by method. The price is 97% above the maximum buy. The stock would need to fall by 49% to reach it, or the financing for the remaining 800 MW would need to close on terms close to the 7% notes, which would raise my Base value and lower the required margin.

Reasons to own

  • $36 billion of non-cancellable base-term leases, with roughly $86 billion if all renewal options were exercised, against a market value of $3.4 billion.
  • Delivery is on schedule so far: Polaris Forge 1 reached 250 MW live on October 1, and the company expects more than 600 MW in service over the next 12 months, against 250 MW in the past 12 months.
  • The newer leases are with tenants the company describes as investment grade, and the latest bond priced at 7%.
  • Cash of $2.9 billion plus $0.3 billion restricted funds much of the near-term construction.

What could go wrong?

  • Financing: about 800 MW needs funding over 12 months, and the company has not published terms. A failed or expensive raise means dilution or a slower build.
  • Interest: Q1 interest expense of $77.4 million was higher than base rent of $65.8 million, and preferred equity costs 12.75%.
  • Concentration: two tenants hold about 86% of the backlog, and CoreWeave's bonds were reported to yield over 17%.
  • Policy: the incoming Texas governor has pledged to pause new data center approvals, and the company's own sites are in other states, but sentiment does not make that distinction.
  • Sector: the stock has had 92 daily moves above 5% in a year, and it traded as a proxy for AI sentiment rather than for its leases.

Management execution

Execution on buildings has been good: Polaris Forge 1 reached ready for service in phases and on the dates the company gave. The financing record is also good, with $1.59 billion at 7%. The weak point is disclosure. Cost per MW, the equity share of the next financings and the plan for a stock-based compensation expense that reached $59.4 million in one quarter are not in the release. The chief executive and the president bought about $6.7 million of stock on October 16 at roughly $18 to $19, which is a signal but not a hedge: the price is now well below that.

Confidence score

Confidence is C at 13.0 out of 25. Data quality scores 3 out of 5 because capital cost per MW and the equity share of future financing are not disclosed, and the cash figure in the release does not reconcile to the balance sheet. Valuation robustness scores 2 because the two methods diverge by 31%. Business durability scores 3, with 15-year contracts offset by tenant concentration. Management and governance score 3, and the sensitivity of the result to the cost of capital scores 2.

What I would watch from here

Green: financing for the remaining campuses closes at a blended cost near the 7% notes, with common equity issued above $15 a share, and cost per MW is disclosed at or below $11 million.

Yellow: the January report shows delivered capacity on plan but financing still open, or a preferred raise at a rate above 12.75%.

Red: a campus delivery slips by more than a quarter, a tenant renegotiates, or the company sells common equity below $10 to fund construction.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. The 800 MW is not financed within 12 months, or only at preferred rates above 13%.
  2. CoreWeave, about 30% of the backlog, defaults or renegotiates its lease.
  3. Construction cost per MW comes in above $12.5 million, which moves my Base value toward the Bear case.

Final verdict: hold at $11.45

Applied Digital owns long-term leases that are real and a balance sheet that has to carry them for two more years. At $11.45 the market asks 7.3 times stabilized EBITDA for the whole project, and my Base case supports about 11 times. The gap is the cost of the unfinanced 800 MW. My maximum buy price is $5.81.

Verdict: HOLD at $11.45, watchlist for new money. Triangulated Base value $13.66, upside +19.3%, Confidence C (13.0/25). Required margin of safety 57.5%, entry range by method $4.75 to $6.51, maximum buy price $5.81.

The date I would mark is the fiscal second quarter report in early January 2027. By then the company should have said how it will fund Polaris Forge 3 and the Delta Forge campuses, and the first MW at Polaris Forge 2 should be live.
