Fidelity National Financial reported third-quarter net earnings of $633 million, up from $358 million a year earlier. Adjusted net earnings, the figure that strips out market-value swings and one-time items, were $352 million, down 19% from $439 million. The difference is almost entirely F&G Annuities & Life, the annuity insurer FNF owns about 72% of, whose accounting gains swamped a weaker adjusted result. The more useful number is in the title business: adjusted pre-tax earnings of $422 million, up 3%, at a 17.1% margin, in a quarter when October orders were down 17% from a year ago. At $45.13 the market pays about 10.1 times my normalized earnings for the title and corporate businesses, if the F&G stake is worth what its stock trades for, which is a low price for a business whose management calls a 15% to 20% margin normal.

My Base value is $58.68, the Bear value is $38.52 and the Bull value is $79.24, so the price sits 17% above Bear and 43% below Bull. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 42.5%, so the maximum buy price is $33.74. The shares would need to fall 25% to reach it. For context, the stock closed at $39.26 on October 14, $42.89 on November 5, the day of the results, and $45.04 on November 9, and its 52-week range is $37.88 to $58.41.

Valuation route: a title insurer that owns a majority of a life and annuity insurer, valued as two parts. The title and corporate businesses are valued on normalized after-tax earnings (a ten-year discounted cash flow, and a five-year cash flow plus a discounted exit at a multiple of earnings). The F&G stake is valued on book value excluding unrealized bond losses, at a price-to-book multiple that its sustainable return on equity justifies. Economic classification: Quality Growth (5.3/12, two criteria excluded for missing data). Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

What Fidelity National Financial is

FNF has two businesses. The first is title insurance and settlement services: it insures real estate buyers and lenders against defects in property title, and earns fees for escrow and closing work. Title revenue was $2,444 million in the third quarter, made up of $755 million of direct premiums (sold through its own offices), $915 million of agency premiums (sold through independent agents) and $700 million of escrow, title-related and other fees. Revenue depends on how many homes and commercial properties change hands or are refinanced, which is why it moves with mortgage rates.

The second is F&G Annuities & Life, which sells fixed and indexed annuities and life insurance and had $76.2 billion of assets under management before reinsurance at September 30, up 7% in a year. F&G is listed separately (NYSE: FG). FNF owned about 82% of it a year ago and about 72% now, after distributing F&G shares to FNF holders at the end of 2025. F&G sold its Bermuda life reinsurance subsidiary on March 1, 2026, removing about $1.8 billion of assets under management. The sale added $0.05 per share to F&G's book value excluding unrealized bond losses, and the price was not disclosed.

The two businesses are very different. Title earns a high margin on little capital and swings with transaction volumes. F&G earns a spread on a large portfolio and has had returns below what its investors require: its adjusted return on equity, excluding unrealized bond losses, was 6.7% over the last four quarters, down from 8.8%. FNF's consolidated figures blend the two, and the gap between GAAP and adjusted results comes mostly from F&G's investment accounting.

Why I value the two parts separately

Adding the two segments' adjusted earnings and applying one multiple would hide the difference in quality. Title earnings are cyclical but capital-light, and the title business is the part of FNF that pays dividends upstream. F&G earnings are a spread on a portfolio, are valued by the market on book value, and arrive at FNF only as dividends of about $1.00 a share, or roughly $94 million a year on FNF's stake (my arithmetic, 72% of about 131 million shares).

For the title and corporate part I start from a normalized after-tax figure of $980 million for 2026. It is an estimate built from reported pieces: nine months of title contribution of $851 million, plus about $259 million for the fourth quarter (a 15% margin on about $2.3 billion of revenue, taxed at the 25% rate the third quarter implies, since 315 divided by 422 is 75%), less corporate costs of about $129 million a year. The corporate figure is the reported nine-month corporate loss of $13 million plus the $84 million of F&G dividend income the corporate segment includes before elimination, and then annualized. I do not include the F&G dividends in this part, because they belong to the stake valued below. Holding company cash of $545 million is not added, because the corporate figure already contains the interest it earns and $100 million to $200 million is what management calls a comfortable level.

Primary valuation: ten years of Title and Corporate cash flow, plus the F&G stake

The primary method discounts ten years of cash flow at 10.5%. FNF's stock beta is below 1 in third-party data, so the base rate is not high, but title earnings are tied to real estate cycles and the holding structure adds complexity, so I use about 10.5%, a risk-free rate near 5% plus a premium of about 5.5%. At 9.5% the Base would be $63.48 and at 11.5% $54.84, and the verdict does not change at either.

Base grows Title and Corporate earnings 4% a year for five years (a partial recovery in volumes, steady commercial work and technology productivity, offset by wage inflation) and 3% for the next five, from $980 million to $1,192 million in 2031 and $1,382 million in 2036. Terminal growth is 3%. Present value of the ten years of cash flow is $24.97 a share and the terminal value adds $24.79, a terminal value of 13.7 times 2036 cash flow. Terminal value share: 50% of the Title and Corporate value, at a ten-year horizon, below the 75% level where I would check that it rests on sustainable flows.

The F&G stake is valued on book value excluding unrealized bond losses, using the closed form: justified price-to-book equals sustainable return on equity minus growth, divided by cost of equity minus growth. At a 7.0% return, 10% cost of equity and 3% growth, that is 0.57 times, or $29.37 a share, and 72% of it is worth $2,769 million to FNF, $10.33 a FNF share, or 17% of the primary value. The 7.0% return is my estimate: it is above the 6.7% of the last four quarters because management is cutting operating costs and alternative investment income is below its long-term expectation, but below the 8.8% of a year earlier. The rate on bonds that have lost value matters here. I do not have the duration of F&G's assets and liabilities, so I could not confirm that the unrealized losses will never be realized. If I used book value including those losses, the Base would be $54.68.

The primary value is $60.10.

Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 12 times earnings

The cross-check takes the same first five years of cash flow and values the Title and Corporate businesses at the end of 2031 at 12 times earnings, discounted to today, with the same F&G stake. At the Base exit, the exit value is $14,308 million, and the secondary value is $57.27.

Why 12 times. At a 10.5% return, a 12 times multiple of earnings that can be 95% paid out implies growth of about 2.4% a year forever, a little below the 3% terminal growth in the primary method, which fits a cyclical earnings stream that does not deserve a premium. Bear uses 9 times, which implies about -0.1% growth, and Bull uses 14 times, which implies about 3.5%. Exit value share: 69% of the Title and Corporate value, at a five-year horizon, below the 85% level where I check the exit more closely. I could not build a synchronized peer table for other title insurers, so the multiple rests on the arithmetic and not on comparable companies.

What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $45.13 needs one of four things, each alone with the rest unchanged: Title and Corporate earnings of $705 million instead of $980 million, which is 28% lower; earnings growth 5.8 points a year slower than my Base for ten years, or a decline of about 1.8% a year for five years; a discount rate of 15.2% instead of 10.5%; or a sustainable F&G return on equity of 1.8% instead of 7.0%. All are less favorable than my Base, and the first fits the order data: October orders were down 17%, so the market may be pricing a normal earnings year that is lower than 2026.

If F&G's stake is worth what its stock trades for, about $2,204 million at $23.38 (a price I could not date), the market is paying $9,891 million for the Title and Corporate businesses, 10.1 times my normalized earnings. On adjusted earnings, the shares trade at 9.4 times the annualized nine-month figure of $4.83 a share, which includes F&G. The growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) is not meaningful, since earnings are flat and cyclical. A free cash flow growth check does not apply to insurers, so I rely on the earnings path above. A stress that cuts the terminal and exit values by 20% lowers the Base to $52.96, a fall of 9.7%.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C starts at a 42.5% margin of safety. I added nothing on top for the cyclical business, the holding structure or the F&G stake, because each is already charged elsewhere: a 10.5% discount rate, 95% cash conversion, a Bear case with falling earnings and a 9 times exit, a 12 times exit that implies growth below the terminal rate, and the Confidence score itself. A 42.5% discount to the $58.68 Base gives a maximum buy price of $33.74. That is below the 52-week low of $37.88, so the shares would need to fall more than they did in this cycle to get there. Because the Base is 30% above the price, a stricter reader would call the stock undervalued, and I do: the Hold comes from my margin of safety, not from a view that the shares are expensive. The valuation depends most on normalized earnings: a 10% change in the $980 million moves the Base by about 10%.

Dividend and buybacks

FNF pays a quarterly dividend of $0.52, or $2.08 a year, a yield of 4.6% at $45.13, and raised it in the third quarter by an amount not stated. Dividends were $417 million over nine months, 43% of adjusted net earnings, and with buybacks of $144 million the total return was 58% of adjusted net earnings. My normalized cash flow to shareholders, $931 million for Title and Corporate (95% of $980 million) plus about $94 million of F&G dividends, covers the dividend of about $557 million a year with room for buybacks. In 2026 so far, cash returned was more than the holding company's cash flow, as holding company cash fell by $114 million. I do not add buybacks to the value, because buying at fair value does not change value per share.

Reasons to own FNF

  • A title margin the company calls industry leading. The adjusted pre-tax title margin was 17.1% in the third quarter and 16.2% for nine months, inside a 15% to 20% normal range.
  • Commercial is strong. Direct commercial revenue was $1.2 billion in nine months, up 22%, with data center refinancings an underappreciated driver, according to management.
  • The price implies a hard year. The shares price Title and Corporate at about 10.1 times my normalized earnings if F&G is worth its market price, or at a decline of about 1.8% a year in earnings if it is worth my value.
  • A 4.6% dividend, raised, and a falling share count. Shares outstanding fell from 271 million to 268 million in a year.
  • F&G could re-rate. It trades at about 0.74 times reported book and 0.45 times book excluding unrealized bond losses, and management says it is looking at ways to unlock value.

What could go wrong?

  • Volumes keep falling. October total orders were down 17%, refinance orders down 35% and commercial orders down 6%, and management expects the title margin to move toward the low end of its range.
  • Rates. A September Fed rate hike pushed mortgage rates to 7.6% on September 30, and the finance team's interest income outlook assumes another 75 basis points over nine months, which helps investment income but hurts transactions.
  • Costs. Personnel costs rose 10% to $840 million, and about 600 people were added this year, mostly through recruiting and acquisitions. Cuts have begun but may not be enough if volumes fall.
  • F&G returns. Adjusted return on equity of 6.7% is below any reasonable cost of equity, alternative investments are earning below plan, and F&G's net sales were $1.4 billion in the quarter against $2.8 billion a year ago.
  • Unrealized bond losses. F&G's AOCI is -$2,605 million. If liabilities force asset sales, the losses become real, and I could not verify the duration match.
  • GAAP noise. GAAP net earnings were $1,164 million for nine months against adjusted $971 million, and market-value gains and losses at F&G will make the reported figure jump around.
  • Payout above cash flow. Holding company cash fell $114 million in nine months, and a dividend raise with weaker earnings reduces the buffer.

Management execution

Mike Nolan is the chief executive of FNF, Tony Park is the chief financial officer and Conor Murphy leads F&G. The record on the title business is consistent: a 17.1% margin in the third quarter, a stated normal margin range of 15% to 20%, and commercial revenue up 22% in nine months in a market with volumes at low levels. Management is open about what it does not do: it does not manage to outlooks. It followed through on the F&G distribution at the end of 2025 that reduced the stake from 82% to 72%, completed the sale of F&G Life Re, and bought back $144 million of shares in nine months. Against that, the dividend increase came with a falling cash buffer and no stated amount, personnel hiring ran ahead of volumes until staff reductions began, and the F&G return on equity has fallen for several quarters. Management credibility is 14/20.
