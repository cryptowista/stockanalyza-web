In June 2026 Howard Hughes Holdings bought the insurer Vantage Group for about $2.1 billion in cash and financed part of it with $1.0 billion of new preferred stock sold to Pershing Square. A company that was a land developer and landlord is now also a property and casualty insurer run alongside its largest shareholder's investment team. The shares closed at $75.58 on October 29, and I think they already pay for that story.

At $75.58, the shares trade 32.3% above my Base value of $51.16, so the Base is 32.3% below the price. The Bear value of $20.07 is 73.4% below the price and the Bull value of $84.96 is 12.4% above it. I assign no probabilities to the cases. The required margin of safety of 40% gives a buy range of $29.44 to $32.58 and a conservative limit of $29.44. The range between Bear and Bull is very wide, which is why Confidence is only C. The verdict is Reduce for holders who can trim without tax or other cost, and a wait for new money.

Valuation route: Holding company and conglomerate, valued as the sum of its parts with a net asset value method. Economic classification: average quality with some growth, and the insurance business too new to classify. Primary method: net asset value of operating assets, land, condominium developments and the insurer, less debt, the preferred stock and capitalized overhead, plus cash, with a 10% holding company discount. Cross-check: 10 times normalized after-tax earnings. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

## What Howard Hughes is

Howard Hughes Holdings has four parts that I value separately.

- **Master planned communities.** The company sells land and homesites in large developments such as Bridgeland and The Woodlands Hills near Houston and Summerlin near Las Vegas. Management says the remaining wholly owned land bank has about $5.6 billion of projected margin, excluding Teravalis and Floreo. That figure is undiscounted and sold over many years.
- **Operating assets.** Offices, retail, multifamily and other properties that produced net operating income of $143.7 million in the first half of 2026, including the company's share of ventures.
- **Strategic developments.** Mostly condominium towers in Ward Village in Honolulu. Management says future expected condominium revenue exceeds $4 billion, about 78% of it under contract.
- **Vantage.** A property and casualty insurer bought on June 4, 2026. Its book value was $1.8 billion at the time of the second quarter call, and management targets a return on equity at or above the mid-teens over the cycle. Pershing Square manages the investment portfolio without a fee.

The company is not a real estate investment trust, so it pays tax at the corporate level and the market gives it no yield-based valuation floor. That is the reason I do not value the operating assets at a premium cap rate and why I apply a holding discount to the total.

## What the earnings are made of

- **Land sales are lumpy and finite.** Residential land sold at an average of $1.2 million an acre in the first half of 2026, 206.7 acres in total. Every acre sold is an acre that cannot be sold again, so MPC earnings are a depleting asset and not a perpetual stream. I value them as a run-off of 18 years.
- **Condominium earnings come in steps.** The second quarter's gross margin on condominium sales was about 19%, after $575.4 million of related costs against $706.3 million of revenue. I value the remaining pipeline on a margin of about 20%, discounted for time.
- **Rental income grows slowly.** Net operating income rose 2% in the quarter. Management did not give a target.
- **Insurance earnings are unproven under this owner.** The 26-day stub shows underwriting income of $4.7 million and a loss before tax of $20.8 million. It tells me almost nothing.

I do not have the split of the $2.6 billion of cash between the parent and Vantage. I assume $0.5 billion sits at Vantage and belongs to the insurer's value. This is an estimate, and it moves the Base by about $8 a share for each $0.5 billion of error.

I apply a 10% holding company discount, which is inside the 10% to 20% range I use for a company with a clear structure and no buyback, and which gives **$49.07** a share. The reasons for a discount are the tax leakage of a non-REIT, the lack of a buyback, and the fact that parts of the value, such as the land bank, are realized only over decades. The reasons not to use a larger one are the company's cash, undrawn capacity and its large shareholder. Vantage is valued at book value, a figure that is below the $2.1 billion purchase price. I do not credit the purchase price, because goodwill of $282.2 million and intangibles of $586.4 million were part of what was paid, and they are not tangible book value.

I did not use the sum of the cases with the highest and lowest inputs for the Base. The net asset value is a point estimate with a wide error, and the sensitivity below shows how wide.

## Secondary valuation: earnings multiple

The cross-check starts from normalized after-tax earnings. Land margin of $311 million, net operating income of $287 million, less interest of about $330 million and overhead of $124 million, taxed at 25%, gives $108 million. Vantage adds a 12% return on its $1.8 billion book value, or $216 million. The total is $324 million, or **$5.43** a share. At 10 times this gives **$54.30**.

I use 10 times and not a higher multiple because the land earnings run off over about 18 years and the properties need capital to maintain. The multiple implies no long-term growth in the real estate earnings and a mid-teens return in the insurer after taxes. The interest figure is my estimate from the debt and the coupons of the senior notes, not a reported total.

The two methods share the same view of land value, net operating income and Vantage's return. They differ in that the net asset value method values each part at an asset price, and the earnings method capitalizes what the parts earn after overhead and interest. Their agreement is therefore weaker evidence than it looks, and I give it no extra Confidence.

## Valuation triangulation

- Primary (net asset value after the holding discount): $49.07, weight 60%.
- Secondary (10 times normalized earnings): $54.30, weight 40%.
- Divergence between the two: 10.1% of their midpoint, inside the 20% limit.
- Weighted Base: $51.16. The range of $49.07 to $54.30 is the model's own spread, and the real uncertainty is wider, as the Bear and Bull show.

I weight the net asset value higher because it shows each assumption for each part, so the reader can change it. In the Bear case the two methods differ by much more than 30%, because the net asset value falls faster than earnings when the capitalization rate and the cash split move against the company at the same time. That is a flag on the Bear value, not on the Base.

## Dividend and buybacks

I found no dividend on the common shares in the filings I reviewed, and management said on the call that issuing equity at current prices is not intelligent. There was no buyback discussion. Returns to shareholders come from the growth of asset value, and the preferred stock pays no cash dividend now but is a senior claim on that value.

## Reasons to own HHH

- The land bank is scarce, and management puts its remaining projected margin at about $5.6 billion.
- Master planned community pre-tax earnings rose 32% in the second quarter, with new home sales up 12%.
- The Ward Village condominium pipeline is about 78% under contract, with more than $4 billion of expected revenue.
- The Vantage investment portfolio is managed by Pershing Square without a fee, and management targets a mid-teens return on equity over the cycle.
- Liquidity is large: $2.6 billion of cash and $1.5 billion of undrawn capacity.

## What could go wrong?

- Catastrophe losses and adverse reserve development at Vantage reduce its equity. The insurer's equity share of its portfolio was about 40% in early August, and equities are expected to be at least 50% over time, so the insurer's book value will move with the stock market.
- Housing and land demand around Houston and Las Vegas slow, which delays land sales and lowers prices.
- Higher interest rates raise the cost of $5.46 billion of debt and lower property values.
- The Series A preferred stock has terms I could not read in full. An exchange into common stock would dilute holders.
- S&P's group-based view of the insurer's rating can raise the cost of capital for the whole group.

## Management execution

- The company closed Park Ward Village with 97% of units sold and $226.6 million of net proceeds after construction debt.
- It sold the Creekside Park projects for $127.3 million, about $30.2 million of net proceeds, a project-level return of about 30% according to management.
- The Vantage purchase is a large change of strategy. It has no track record under this owner, and the first quarter after closing included losses from catastrophes and prior-year reserves.
- I give credit for clear communication about capital: no equity issuance at current prices, and a plan to use joint ventures and third-party capital for real estate.

## What I would watch from here

### Green

- The third quarter report shows a full quarter of Vantage with a combined ratio below 95% and no new adverse reserve development.
- The company discloses the split of cash between the parent and Vantage and the full terms of the preferred stock.
- Land sales continue at $1.2 million an acre or more with margin above $300 million a year.

### Yellow

- Operating asset net operating income stays flat for another quarter.
- Management gives no figure for free cash flow or Vantage's return.
- Equities reach more than half of the insurer's portfolio during a weak market.

### Red

- A second quarter of adverse reserve development or large catastrophe losses at Vantage.
- A fall in land prices per acre or in new home sales around Houston.
- Terms of the preferred stock that allow exchange into common at a price near today's.

## The three most important thesis breakers

1. Vantage earns less than the cost of its capital, which turns the $2.1 billion purchase into a loss of value.
2. Land sales slow for several quarters, which removes the main source of cash for the group.
3. A higher capitalization rate on operating assets, for example 7.5%, which lowers the Base net asset value to $42.66 a share.

The weakest point of the model is the combination of the capitalization rate, the cash split and the land value, all of which are estimates. The strongest bull argument is that Pershing Square's investment record compounds Vantage's float faster than a mid-teens return, and that the land bank is worth more than my run-off model suggests, which would justify the price. The strongest bear argument is that the market pays for a story, the insurer's first results were a loss, and the assets that back $5.46 billion of debt leave little room for error.

## Final verdict: reduce at $75.58

Howard Hughes owns real assets, a long land bank and a new insurance platform with a respected investor behind it. At $75.58 the market already pays about 1.39 times my estimate of the assets, and there is no margin of safety against the estimates that matter most.

**Verdict: REDUCE at $75.58. Weighted Base $51.16, 32.3% below the price, cross-check $54.30, Confidence C (13/25). Required margin of safety 40%, buy range $29.44 to $32.58, conservative buy limit $29.44.**

For an existing holder, the original cost of the shares is irrelevant. I would trim into the recent rise, and keep a smaller position only if I accept the Bull case, which is still just 12.4% above the price. New money should wait for a price at or below $32.58, a full quarter of Vantage results and the terms of the preferred stock. A review is warranted on November 5, when the third quarter report is expected.
