On October 3 I said reduce Credo at $218.64. The shares are now $122.94, 43.8% lower, and Credo has reported no new quarter and changed no guidance since. The sell-off was about the sector. What moved my number is a rule I now apply: my October model counted $400 million of annual stock pay as free. Charged as a cost, my Base falls from $149.66 to $128.57, and the price meets it.

At $122.94 the shares trade 4.4% below my weighted Base value of $128.57, so the Base is 4.6% above the price. The Bear value of $66.35 is 46.0% below the price and the Bull value of $195.51 is 59.0% above it. I assign no probabilities to the cases. Confidence is grade C, which gives a required margin of safety of 42.5% and a maximum buy price of $73.93. The verdict is Hold: a high-quality business at roughly fair value is not the same as one that pays me for its risks, and the price would have to fall another 40% to reach my buy price.

Valuation route: a fabless semiconductor company (it designs chips and cables and hires others to build them), valued on owner free cash flow, which is free cash flow less stock compensation. Primary method: a ten-year owner free cash flow path with a terminal value, discounted at 10%. Cross-check: a multiple of owner free cash flow at the end of fiscal 2031, discounted back. Both are tested against a reverse valuation. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

The part of the old model that no longer holds is the treatment of stock compensation. In October I valued free cash flow before stock pay and expected the multiple and the discount rate to absorb it. My own stress test showed what that hid: with $400 million of stock pay charged, the Base fell to $90 to $96. The framework requires stock compensation to be treated once, as a cost or as explicit dilution. That October stress test was a rough one, since it deducted the cost from a 34.5 times multiple and grew stock pay 12% a year for ten years, and it is not how I model it now. The October call to reduce at $218.64 turned out well for a holder, but the fall came from the sector and not from Credo, and I do not count it as skill.

What Credo is

Credo designs the chips and cables that move data between servers, switches and memory inside AI data centers. Its main product is the active electrical cable (AEC): a copper cable with a Credo chip in each connector that cleans and boosts the signal. It also sells retimers, digital signal processors (DSPs) and licenses its SerDes technology (the circuitry that turns data into a high-speed signal). Credo does not make its own chips. In the latest quarter AECs drove more than 90% of the revenue increase.

On May 28, 2026 Credo closed the purchase of DustPhotonics, a silicon photonics chip designer, to move into optical links. The 10-Q puts the total consideration at $1.25 billion: $769.6 million in cash, $169.1 million in shares and $310.0 million of contingent consideration payable in up to about 3.21 million more shares if milestones are met. Goodwill and intangibles now total $1.37 billion. Management says optical revenue should pass $600 million in fiscal 2027.

Concentration is the other side. By end customer, the three largest were 28%, 33% and 13% of last quarter's revenue and a fourth was 10%. By contracting party, Customer A was 43% of revenue and 57% of receivables. Contracted but unsatisfied performance obligations were $4.2 million at quarter end, against $479.0 million of quarterly revenue, so orders carry almost no commitment.

Reasons to own Credo

  • AI clusters need more bandwidth per rack. Credo's cables and chips address the short links where copper and optical choices are being made now.
  • Proprietary SerDes and DSP technology. Customers qualify these designs for years, which creates switching friction.
  • Exceptional operating leverage. Revenue is up 114.7%, non-GAAP gross margin is 68% and non-GAAP operating margin is 48.2%.
  • The price is now near my Base and not 46% above it, and my Bull value is 59% above the price.
  • No financial debt: $764.3 million of cash funds research and integration.

What could go wrong?

  • Customers. Four end customers were 84% of revenue in the last quarter and one contracting party is 57% of receivables. Orders carry no long-term minimums.
  • AI spending and policy. The November sell-off came from worries about data center expansion after the elections, according to TIKR, and a policy change could weigh on customers' spending plans.
  • Copper against optical. Marvell's agreement to buy Lumentum is a bet that optical links will displace copper, and Credo's cables are copper. Credo's answer is DustPhotonics, which has to be integrated.
  • Stock compensation and dilution. Stock pay was larger than free cash flow in the latest quarter, the share count is up 8.6% in a year, and up to 3.21 million contingent shares are still to come.
  • Working capital. Inventory rose 24.8% in one quarter against revenue growth of 9.6%, and Credo has $222.2 million of purchase commitments.
  • Acquisition execution. $1.37 billion of goodwill and intangibles sit on the balance sheet, with $58.2 million a year of amortization from fiscal 2028.

Management execution

Management has beaten its revenue guidance, scaled revenue more than threefold in fiscal 2026 and closed a large acquisition in the same year. The offsets are a rising share count that has not matched the growth in the business, the at-the-market stock sales and steady insider selling: 352,538 shares sold in the last quarter, according to MarketBeat. The 10-K describes special performance awards with six revenue and stock price hurdles, the first at $244.70, twice today's price. Assessment: 15/20, good. The next test is whether free cash flow per share grows faster than stock compensation.

  • Revenue growth above 40%, non-GAAP gross margin at or above 66%, the three largest end customers below 65% of revenue.
