Hamilton earned $86.7 million from underwriting in the first half of 2026 and reported $277.3 million of net income to common shareholders. The gap is mostly investment returns, and the largest single source is a fund run by Two Sigma, which returned $208.5 million in the same six months before the noncontrolling interest took its share. At $36.51, the October 20, 2026 regular session close, the shares trade 10.5% below my triangulated Base value of $40.81, an upside of 11.8%. The Bear value is $22.86, 37.4% below the price, and the Bull value of $58.52 is 60.3% above it. Confidence is grade C (15/25), the required margin of safety (MOS) is 40%, and the maximum buy price is $24.48. The price is within reach of my Base and far above the buy ceiling, so the verdict is Hold.

Valuation route: a Bermuda based specialty insurer and reinsurer, valued on common book value and the return that book can sustain, not on free cash flow, because claims reserves and regulatory capital decide what can be paid out. Primary method (65% weight): a price to book that follows a five-year fade of return on equity to a normal level. Secondary method (35%): 10 times normalized earnings built from underwriting and investment income. A tangible net asset test is a floor check and carries no weight. Classification: quality growth, 8 of 12 on the insurance scorecard.

What Hamilton is

Hamilton Insurance Group writes specialty insurance and reinsurance and invests the premiums it holds until claims are paid. The listed shares are Class B on the NYSE, but the company has three share classes (A, B and C) that share the economics, so market value built on Class B alone understates it. In 2025 net premiums earned were $2.11 billion, up 21.6%, the combined ratio was 92.9% and book value per share rose from $22.95 to $28.50.

The investor presentation puts the premium mix at roughly half insurance and half reinsurance, with casualty close to half of gross premiums written. That matters more than the headline margin. Casualty claims are slow: a policy written today can produce its real losses in five to ten years, so a good combined ratio now is a hypothesis, and reserve development is how it gets tested. The same presentation shows combined ratios above 100% in 2021 and 2022 and profitable underwriting in 2023 to 2025.

The second engine is the investment side. A bond and cash portfolio earns a steady yield. Alongside it, Hamilton allocates capital to the Two Sigma Hamilton Fund, which in the first half returned $208.5 million against $26.4 million from fixed income, short-term and cash holdings. Part of the fund is owned by outside investors and the manager earns an incentive allocation, so the common shareholder sees only what is left.

The primary valuation: price to book with a return fade

The primary method follows the book value forward five years. Each year earnings equal opening book times the return on equity, a share of that is kept to grow book, and the rest is what could be paid out. After year five the business earns a normal return forever, and the exit price to book is (terminal return - growth) / (cost of equity - growth). In my Base the return fades from 18% to 15%, book grows 5% a year, the terminal return is 14%, terminal growth is 3% and the cost of equity is 11%, my assumption and not a universal hurdle. The exit multiple is 1.375 times book.

The exit price at the end of year five is $49.70, a future figure and not today's value. Discounted at 11%, the payouts are worth $12.93 and the exit $29.50, so the primary Base is $42.43. The payouts are what the capital could support, not a promised dividend. The exit carries 69.5% of the value, which is the main weakness of this method: most of what you pay for is the return Hamilton earns after the fifth year. A second calculation, discounting the earnings above the 11% cost of equity on each year's book, reproduces $42.43 to the cent.

The Bear path (8% rising to 11% return, terminal return 11%, which gives exactly 1.0 times book) is worth $27.32. The Bull path (22% fading to 18%, terminal return 17%) is worth $57.95.

The secondary valuation: 10 times normal earnings

The second method takes the normalized earnings above and applies a multiple: $3.78 times 10 is $37.80. A multiple of 10 is a 10% earnings yield and fits a business whose earnings are sustainable but volatile. It is my assumption, not a peer median, and I use 8 times for the Bear ($14.56) and 11 times for the Bull ($59.56).

This is not an independent check. It uses the same view of combined ratios, fund returns and tax as the primary method, only built from components instead of a return on book. When both agree it shows my own assumptions are consistent, not that they are right.

For a check that needs no forecast, I use tangible common net assets: $2,850.8 million less $83.2 million of intangibles, divided by 100.652 million shares, is $27.50 a share. Taking a 5% haircut to net claims reserves of $3,319.2 million, with no tax shield, removes $1.65 and leaves $25.85. That is a coverage reference, not a liquidation value and not a second valuation of the business.

A price check against history

Reported book value per share was $16.14 at the end of 2022, $18.58 in 2023, $22.95 in 2024 and $28.50 in 2025, then $28.91 in June 2026. From the end of 2020 ($16.23) to the end of 2025 that is 11.9% a year, but over 2023 to 2025 it was 23.9% a year. The recent pace reflects a very good stretch for both underwriting and the fund, and I do not treat it as the steady state. Hamilton listed in November 2023, so there is no long trading history, and I could not build a clean series of historical price to book to compare with. History gets no weight.

The shares trade at 1.26 times reported book and 1.29 times my diluted book. My primary Base value is 1.50 times diluted book.

The growth-adjusted check is conditional. A third-party forecast page shows 2025 operating EPS of $4.84 and an estimate of $5.09 for 2026, growth of 5.2%, a forward earnings multiple of 7.2 and a PEG of 1.39. I do not trust that figure: the estimate sample is a single analyst, the net income and EPS rows on the page do not reconcile to one accounting basis, and insurance earnings swing with investment marks. A broker page that shows a PEG of 0.11 and a return on equity of 31.7% has no period bridge, so I disregard both. Free cash flow growth is not meaningful for an insurer. The measure I use instead is book value plus dividends, which grew 8.5% in the first half of 2026 and 24.2% in 2025.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C calls for a margin of safety of 35% to 50%. I use 40%, toward the lower half because the capital is real and reported, and add nothing on top: the pricing cycle, reserves, fund concentration and terminal value are already handled in the scenarios, the confidence score and the sensitivity grid, and charging them again would count one risk twice. The maximum buy price is $40.81 x (1 - 40%) = $24.48. It is a decision reference, not an order to place. I would review accumulation between $20 and $25, ask for fresh evidence between $25 and $30, and look for what has broken below $20.

Three numbers answer different questions: what the shares are worth ($40.81), the return from buying at $36.51 and following the primary Base path for five years (15.0% a year) and the price that gives a 10% annual return on that path ($44.12). The 10% is an illustration, not a requirement I know you have, and it does not replace value or margin of safety. A 15% modeled return next to a $24.48 buy price looks odd, and it is meant to: the return depends on payouts and a terminal return that could be wrong, while the margin of safety pays for that.

The dividend

Hamilton declared a special dividend of $2.00 a share, about $206 million, in February 2026 and paid it on March 30. It is already out of the business and inside the June book value, so I do not add it to value. Against $36.51 it is a historical 5.48%, not a yield: reliable recurring dividend guidance is not available, and I do not annualize a special payment. For an income investor the question is not the $2.00 but whether repeat earnings can fund a regular payout and still support underwriting growth.

Reasons to own

  • An underwriting record that is improving. Combined ratios of 92.9% in 2025 and 92.5% in the first half follow losses in 2021 and 2022, and good underwriting builds capital that does not depend on the share price.
  • More ways to grow. A US platform with a stronger rating (Hamilton Select, upgraded to A in May) widens the market without a large acquisition.
  • Light financing debt. Debt is 5.25% of common equity, which removes one source of pressure, although claims leverage stays.
  • A real record of book value growth. Book value per share rose from $16.14 to $28.50 over 2022 to 2025, through a weak earlier period.
  • A Bull case with real upside. It is worth $58.52, and today's price does not need the best recent returns to repeat.

Main risks

  • Casualty reserves. Claims can surface years after premiums are booked, and repeated adverse development would erase apparent profits and reduce book value.
  • Losses and portfolio at the same time. A catastrophe year and a weak market can arrive together, when liquid assets are also needed to pay claims.
  • One fund manager. Two Sigma's future net returns, fees and operations can differ from the record, and the gross return is not all the common shareholder's.
  • Softer pricing. Premium growth at inadequate rates destroys value later.
  • Trapped capital. Subsidiary solvency, ratings and collateral limit what moves up to the parent, and consolidated cash is not holding company surplus.
  • Tax and expenses. Recent tax credits and a lower expense ratio may not last.
  • Dilution and governance. Awards offset buybacks, and three share classes complicate the picture.
  • Model risk. The terminal return carries most of the primary value, and both methods share the same return drivers.

Management execution

Management's outcomes are visible without guidance to test them against: underwriting improved over three years, book value per share rose from $18.58 at the end of 2023 to $28.50 at the end of 2025, buybacks and the special dividend returned cash, and the Select upgrade supports the expansion. I score management credibility 14/20: execution 4/5, capital allocation 3/5, disclosure 4/5, alignment 3/5. The first half buyback averaged about $29.97, above the $28.91 book value, which is defensible if the shares were worth more, but I have not rebuilt every purchase against the book value on its date. Fund related compensation, voting arrangements and adjusted earnings definitions deserve attention. There is no numeric guidance I could score as delivered or missed, and I do not penalize the absence.

What I would watch from here

Supports the thesis

  • A four-quarter combined ratio at or below 95% with a sound current-year loss trend, and prior-year reserve development stable or favorable without depending on releases.
  • Normalized return on equity of 15% or more, and book value plus distributions growing more than 10% a year.
  • Group capital coverage above 180% on the same definition, an A rating with a stable outlook, and a multi-year net fund return of 8% to 12%.

Watch closely

  • A combined ratio of 95% to 100%, or favorable releases hiding weak current-year underwriting, and adverse prior-year development of up to 2% of annual premiums.
  • Return on equity of 11% to 15%, capital coverage of 150% to 180%, or a negative rating outlook.
  • Awards that offset buybacks, a larger fund allocation without better disclosure, or fund returns of 0% to 8%.

Breaks the thesis

  • A combined ratio above 100% persistently, or adverse development above 2% of annual premiums more than once.
  • Normalized return on equity below 11% for two annual periods, or capital coverage below 150%, a capital raise or limits on distributions.
  • A rating downgrade, or material fund losses together with capital stress.

The most important thesis breakers

  • Casualty reserves. Repeated adverse revisions that push normalized return on equity to about 11% or below.
  • The fund. A net return well under the 9% I assume, at the same time as a weaker combined ratio.
  • Capital and ratings. A fall in capital coverage or a downgrade that limits access to business.

Final verdict: hold at $36.51

Hamilton is a better underwriter than it was three years ago, and it carries more capital than a screen based on price to earnings would suggest. But the cheapness is overstated by investment gains and by broker ratios that do not line up with the filings, the price sits close to a normalized Base, and a moderate error in reserves or in the return the fund can keep would absorb the discount. A new purchase is not supported by this model at a price above $24.48.

Verdict: HOLD at $36.51, watchlist for new money. Triangulated Base value $40.81, upside 11.8%, Confidence C (15/25), required margin of safety 40%, maximum buy price $24.48.

For holders this means keeping the position and reading the October 29 report for book value, reserves and the fund's return, because the gap to Base is small. For new money it means waiting for a lower price or for another year of clean reserves. Underwriting is the part of Hamilton you can audit. The fund is the part that moves the answer.
