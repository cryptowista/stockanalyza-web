## What Enbridge is

Enbridge runs four businesses: liquids pipelines, natural gas transmission, gas utilities and renewable power. The Mainline moved 3.1 million barrels a day in 2025 and was apportioned (shippers asked for more space than existed) for nine months of the year. In 2024 it bought three US gas utilities from Dominion Energy, which made it the largest gas utility in North America. It has over 8 gigawatts of renewable power, with more than 2 gigawatts under construction.

The company says it has met its financial guidance for 20 years in a row and has raised its dividend for 31 years in a row. The secured growth backlog is about C$38 billion, after C$14 billion of projects were approved in 2025 and C$1 billion was added in the third quarter. This autumn it also agreed or closed about US$3.6 billion of purchases: Tallgrass crude oil assets for US$2.55 billion (waiting for regulatory approval), Salt Creek Midstream for US$0.6 billion (closed October 9) and Laramie Energy assets for US$485 million (expected to close in the fourth quarter). KKR and Apollo are buying into the Westcoast gas business, with about C$0.7 billion paid and C$2.0 billion more committed through 2028.

## Distributable cash flow

DCF is the metric Enbridge manages to and the one its dividend is measured against. Broadly, it is adjusted EBITDA less maintenance capital, interest, current taxes, preferred dividends and payments to minority holders, so it is a cash figure after the cost of the debt that funds growth. It is not free cash flow, because it ignores growth spending, which is where the debt goes.

## Primary valuation: dividends plus an exit price

I value a share as the dividends it pays over five years plus the price it should sell for at the end of 2031, discounted at 8.5% (a Canadian 10-year yield of about 3.7% plus a 4.8 point equity premium, my assumption). The dividend starts at C$3.88 and grows 3% a year, in line with the 2026 raise. The exit price is DCF per share in 2031 times a multiple of 11 in the Base. Today's multiple is 11.7 times, so the Base assumes a small decline, which I justify below.

## Secondary valuation: EV/EBITDA after all claims

The second method values the whole enterprise at a multiple of 2026 adjusted EBITDA and subtracts the C$120.3 billion of claims. I use 12.5 times for the Base, below today's 13.3 times and below the US and Canadian peers I checked (Kinder Morgan 14.1 times, Williams 17.3 times and TC Energy 16.4 times, from StockAnalysis between October 27 and November 6). The discount reflects Enbridge's higher leverage and slower growth per share. At 12.5 times the equity trades at 10.5 times DCF, a DCF yield of 9.5% and a dividend yield of 6.4%, which at an 8.5% cost of equity assumes only 2.1% a year of long-term growth, below the 3% dividend raises and below the 5% target. ## Cross-check against dividend history

A plain dividend model, where the price equals next year's dividend divided by the cost of equity minus growth, gives $51.53 at 3% dividend growth, $47.01 at 2.5% and $56.97 at 3.5%. It shares the dividend path with the primary method, so it confirms the order of magnitude and is not an independent test. The growth-adjusted check is not useful here: the price is about 22.8 times my estimate of 2026 adjusted earnings of C$2.98 (nine months plus a fourth quarter equal to last year's C$0.88). Divided by the 5% growth target that is a ratio of 4.6, but earnings per share for a company with this much interest and depreciation say little about the cash it can pay out.

## Dividend and capital return

The dividend is C$0.97 a quarter, C$3.88 a year (about $2.75), a 5.7% yield at the current price, raised about 3% for 2026 (the 31st consecutive increase). It takes about 67% of my DCF estimate for the year, which leaves C$4.3 billion to reinvest. Enbridge does not buy back shares; it issued them in September. The dividend is covered by cash flow, but the growth spending beyond it depends on lenders and new shareholders. Payments come in Canadian dollars, so a US holder is exposed to the exchange rate, and Canadian withholding tax applies to non-residents under the treaty rules for their country.

## Reasons to own Enbridge

- Contracted and regulated cash flow: guidance has been met 20 years in a row, and the Mainline has been full enough to apportion capacity.
- A dividend that has risen 31 years in a row and is covered with about C$1.92 a share of DCF left after the payout.
- A C$38 billion backlog, with over C$3 billion of gas transmission projects placed in service in the third quarter alone.
- Exposure to growing gas demand through pipelines, storage, the LNG supply chain and power contracts for data centers.
- Partners (KKR, Apollo) are funding part of the growth at asset level, which reduces the need for new shares.

## What could go wrong?

- **Funding the growth:** a C$6 billion yearly gap between retained cash and investment means more debt or more shares. Each C$3 billion of new shares is about 2% more shares.
- **Leverage:** 5.1 times debt to EBITDA is above the company's target. Higher rates or a ratings move would raise financing costs and could force asset sales.
- **Interest rates:** each point on the cost of equity moves the value by about $2, or 4%.
- **Currency:** earnings and dividends are largely in Canadian dollars; a weaker Canadian dollar lowers the US value.
- **Regulation and rate cases:** the Ohio utility has a stipulated 9.59% return on equity and a decision is expected by the first quarter of 2027.
- **Execution and approvals:** the Tallgrass purchase still needs regulatory approval, and project costs can overrun.
- **Accounting basis:** the debt inside joint ventures is not in net debt, which makes the EBITDA multiple look lower than it is.

## Management execution

Management has hit its financial guidance for 20 years, grew EBITDA 7% in 2025 and placed C$5 billion of projects in service that year. The weaker side is per share growth: the company's own 2023 to 2026 outlook was 7% to 9% EBITDA growth but only nearly 3% a year in DCF per share, and shares rose 3.4% in two years. Leverage went from 4.8 to 5.1 times in nine months. The company is open about it, calling the ratio slightly above target, but the September equity raise shows leverage has a ceiling. I score capital allocation at 7.5 of 10: strong execution, with the cost of buying growth showing up in the share count.
