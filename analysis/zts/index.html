In the first half of 2026 Zoetis spent $1.19 billion buying back its own shares and paid another $447 million in dividends. Free cash flow for the same six months was $829 million. The shares it bought cost about $106 each on average (11.0 million shares for $1.17 billion), and they trade at $81.98 today.

At $81.98 the shares trade 13.3% below my triangulated Base value of $94.58. The Bear value is $56.42, 31.2% below the price, and the Bull value is $141.56, 72.7% above it. Confidence is grade C (17/25), the required margin of safety (MOS) is 35%, and the central maximum buy price is $61.48. Depending on which of my two methods you trust, that threshold sits anywhere from $54.65 to $66.03. The previous close was $79.62, so the intraday quote is 3% higher, and the verdict is the same at either price. The 52-week high is $148.30, which puts the shares 44.7% below it.

Valuation route: a mature animal health company (vaccines, parasiticides, dermatology and pain medicines, diagnostics), valued on owner cash: operating cash flow less all capital spending and stock compensation, after interest. Primary method: that cash capitalized at 20 times in the Base, 15 times in the Bear and 24 times in the Bull. Secondary method: a five-year discounted cash flow with refinancing costs and a terminal value that pays for its own growth. Classification: quality growth company, 7 of 12 on the scorecard. What Zoetis is

Zoetis sells medicines, vaccines and diagnostics for pets and livestock. Dogs and cats are the profitable core. In 2025 revenue was $9.47 billion, GAAP net income $2.67 billion and diluted earnings per share $6.02. Management now guides 2026 revenue to between $9.12 billion and $9.32 billion, a midpoint of $9.22 billion, 2.6% below 2025.

The second quarter showed where the pressure is. U.S. companion animal sales fell 11%, from $1,176 million to $1,044 million. Dermatology category sales fell from $473 million to $398 million, down 15.9%. In its release the company blames "persistent macro-driven price sensitivity" and heightened competition, and says lower Librela sales also hurt. Price sensitivity eases when pet owners feel better off. Competition doesn't, and it is the half of that sentence the valuation has to be built around. International companion animal sales did grow, up 5% on an organic basis, so this is a U.S. problem for now, not a global one.

In August management cut the 2026 outlook sharply. The adjusted earnings per share range went from $6.85 to $7.00 down to $6.15 to $6.25, a 10.5% cut to the midpoint. The revenue range fell from $9.68 to $9.96 billion to $9.12 to $9.32 billion, a 6.1% cut to the midpoint. The five largest product lines made up about 42% of 2025 revenue, so a loss of share in one franchise is not absorbed by the others. A recovery is plausible. A return to the premium valuation the shares once had is not something I assume.

Which cash to value

I value owner cash: operating cash flow minus all capital spending minus stock compensation. Shares issued to employees are paid for by owners, so I deduct them. I also deduct every dollar of capital spending, not only the part that maintains the business. Zoetis spends about $650 million a year against depreciation of about $475 million, and management does not say how much of the gap is growth. If $50 million to $150 million of it were growth spending, owner cash would be $2,150 million to $2,250 million, and even the high end adds only $7.26 a share. I leave it out.

The shape of the maturities is the issue. Several individual maturities in the next five years are larger than a quarter of one year's cash flow before financing, so I model the cash after interest year by year. Face debt is $9.15 billion, against a carrying value of $9.05 billion, and cash plus short-term investments is $1.67 billion.

The primary valuation: owner cash times a multiple

The primary method capitalizes normalized owner cash at a multiple. The multiple is where the valuation sits, so the Base builds it up: a starting multiple of 14 for a mature healthcare company, plus 3 for the growth I expect to return, plus 2 for the returns the franchises earn, plus 2 for repeat demand, minus 1 for the forecasting and competition problems. No premium for the balance sheet and no penalty for dilution. That is 20 times.

The last column is a plain check: a multiple M equals (1 plus g) divided by (the 9% rate minus g), and I solve for g. The 9% is my assumption and a simplification, not a required return. It shows that 20 times asks for cash that grows about 3.8% a year forever, close to the 4% growth in my Base, but that is not proof that the growth lasts. The Base does not assume the old premium multiples. The Bear needs only low single-digit growth and durable remaining franchises. If the decline is structural, $1,500 million of owner cash at 10 times is worth $36.28, so the Bear is not a floor.

All the value of this method sits in the capitalized figure. There is no explicit forecast period, so the continuation value is 100% of the total. The DCF below puts a calendar on the same business.

Both methods use the same debt, the same shares and deduct all capital spending, so none of the $7.2 billion gap in enterprise value between them comes from different claims. It comes from what the multiple assumes about durability against what the DCF assumes about growth, margin and reinvestment. Both lean on the same thing: how long the franchises last against competition. Their agreement therefore does not raise Confidence. The market sits close to the DCF and below the multiple.

  • Shared: the durability of the product portfolio, the starting scale of the business, competition, the debt and the ability to reinvest.
  • Different: the primary method capitalizes current owner cash, while the DCF uses sales, margins, explicit reinvestment, a discount rate and a terminal return on capital.
  • What the DCF tests: whether operating cash conversion and the debt-adjusted enterprise value really support a 20 times multiple. It supports roughly 16.6 times.

A check against history, growth and cash growth

A secondary data source lists year-end price to free cash flow for 2021 to 2025 at 66.5, 51.5, 55.9, 32.0 and 24.3 times, a median of 51.5. That is a five-point sample and it uses cash flow before stock compensation, so it is context and not an entitlement. On the same cash flow before stock compensation ($2,200 million) the shares today trade at 15.4 times, and on my stricter owner cash at 16.1 times. The shares have been re-rated from 24.3 times at the end of 2025 to about 15 times, and the cash flow hasn't fallen by anything like that. I do not use this as a reason to pay the old multiple.

Management's adjusted earnings per share midpoint for 2026 is $6.20, which puts the shares on 13.2 times. On the GAAP midpoint of $5.60 it is 14.6 times. The $0.60 gap is purchase accounting and acquisition items, and recurring research costs are not part of it. Third-party estimates for adjusted earnings are $6.21 for 2026 and $6.53 for 2027, growth of 5.2%, which gives a growth-adjusted P/E (PEG) of about 2.6. That uses calendar-year growth, not a rolling next twelve months, and I could not verify a peer median, so it is a diagnostic, not a conclusion. I class the growth as normalizing and uncertain: it is a recovery from a weak 2026, not proven structural growth.

On cash growth, my Base owner cash goes from $2,100 million to $2,468.50 million four annual steps ahead (roughly 2030), a gain of $368.50 million, or 4.12% a year. Shares are held flat, so the per share growth is the same, $5.08 to $5.97. The owner cash margin stays at about 22.8% to 22.9% of sales. The forward yield on today's price at that 2030 cash flow is 7.3%, against 6.2% now. Reliability is low, because the growth is largely a recovery from a weak base and the capital spending catch-up and working capital are my assumptions. In the stress cases, 20% less cash in 2030 makes the growth rate -1.5% a year, 40% less makes it -8.4%, and 5% more shares cuts per share growth to 2.9%. The Bear scenario ends 2030 at $1,974 million and the Bull at $2,947 million.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C calls for a margin of safety of 35% to 50%. I use 35%, the low end, because current cash generation and debt are measurable, with no add-ons. Competition and demand risk is already in the scenarios and the multiple, debt principal is deducted once, refinancing cost is in the cash after interest, and terminal dependence is in the Confidence score, so none of them is charged a second time. The central maximum buy price is $94.58 x (1 - 35%) = $61.48. Taken separately, the stricter DCF value gives $54.65 and the cash multiple gives $66.03. Above $66.03 neither method qualifies as a buy. Between $54.65 and $66.03 the answer depends on which method you believe. Even at $60, a 9.5% discount rate puts the DCF-based entry price near $45.17, so there is no universally safe price. It is a decision reference, not an order to place.

Three things are reported separately: what the shares are worth ($94.58), the modeled return from buying at $81.98, and the price that earns a chosen return. If the model's owner cash is paid out in full each year and the shares are sold after five years for $97.26, buying at $81.98 returns about 10.1% a year. That is an illustration and not a forecast: actual dividends are far below owner cash, and delayed distributions would change the return. For a required return of 8%, 10% or 12% the entry prices on those same flows are $89.40, $82.40 and $76.10, before any margin of safety. A 10% return at today's price is a choice about return, not a Buy signal.

The dividend

The company declared a fourth quarter dividend of $0.53 a share on October 8, payable December 1 to holders of record on October 30. Four equal payments make $2.12 a year, a yield of 2.59% at $81.98. That is 34.2% of the 2026 adjusted earnings per share midpoint of $6.20 and 37.9% of the GAAP midpoint of $5.60. Annual dividends of about $876 million are 41.7% of my Base owner cash, a cover of 2.4 times.

The dividend itself looks covered. The buyback is the piece that isn't: first half buybacks plus dividends were $808 million more than free cash flow. A 2.59% yield doesn't pay for a loss of market share. I make no assumption about future dividend changes, and as a non-U.S. investor your return in your own currency also depends on the exchange rate, withholding and your account type.

Reasons to own

  • A large cash generator. A diversified platform of veterinary medicines, vaccines and diagnostics still produced about $2.2 billion of owner cash in 2025, after research costs and all capital spending.
  • Repeat demand. Prevention and chronic treatments are bought again and again, even if brand choice is contested.
  • No need for the old multiple. At 16.1 times owner cash, the shares don't need the 30 to 60 times of the past to produce a reasonable return.
  • Breadth outside U.S. pets. International and livestock sales offset part of the U.S. weakness, and international companion animal sales grew 5% organically in the second quarter.
  • Options beyond today's portfolio. Innovation and diagnostics add possibilities, provided they earn more than the capital they use.

These reasons justify research and monitoring. They don't show that $81.98 offers the downside buffer I require.

What could go wrong?

  • Dermatology and parasiticide competition. The franchise that fell 15.9% in the second quarter is the one with the most to lose, and lower share or lower prices would hurt both cash and how long it lasts.
  • Vet visits and affordability. Repeated price increases may meet owner resistance and not turn into lasting revenue.
  • Librela. Label changes, prescribing behavior or adverse publicity can reduce adoption. Regulators publish adverse event reports for it, and I treat that as a monitoring risk, not as proof of harm or of an incidence rate.
  • Litigation. Securities and derivative suits may cost cash and attention. I add no settlement amount, because I have no basis for one.
  • Debt and refinancing. $4.85 billion falls due between 2027 and 2030, including the 0.25% convertible in 2029. A 7.5% replacement rate would cost an extra $97 million a year before tax.
  • Cash conversion. First half free cash flow was only 64% of GAAP net income ($829 million of $1,292 million). A strong second half is built into my Base, and inventory and receivables can hide a weaker picture.
  • Acquisitions. The Neogen genomics business costs $160 million with about $90 million of historical sales, and the terms of the VitalRADS purchase were not fully disclosed. I exclude both the acquired earnings and the price. Adding $160 million of assets and $160 million of cost nets to zero, and a plus or minus $80 million difference in value would move the answer by about $0.19 a share.
  • Patents and product life. The FDA register shows protection for Apoquel into 2030 and 2033 and for Simparica Trio into 2032 and 2033, product by product. Patents do not stop a competitor's newer drug from taking share first.
  • Currency. Operating results are translated from many currencies, and a non-U.S. investor carries a second exchange rate.

Management execution

The record is mixed. The 2026 outlook was cut by 10.5% on adjusted earnings per share and 6.1% on revenue between May and August, and a forecast that moves that far in three months does not earn the benefit of the doubt. A new chief financial and operating officer, Jay Saccaro, started on August 17. I treat that as a chance of better execution, not as evidence of it. On capital allocation, the buyback bought stock at about $106 on average in the first half, nearly 30% above today's $81.98. It was paid for partly out of liquidity, and the company issued a $2.0 billion convertible in December 2025. A lower share count is only worth it if the price paid is lower than the value received. I give management and allocation 5 of 10 inside business quality, and no premium for the balance sheet. What I want to see is guidance that is met, cash conversion that is transparent, and buybacks paid from surplus.

What I would watch from here

Supports the thesis

  • U.S. companion animal sales flat or growing for two quarters, and dermatology stable on a comparable basis.
  • Annual owner cash at or above $2,100 million, and free cash flow at or above 85% of net income over a full year.
  • Buybacks paid from surplus after dividends, and the 2027 to 2030 refinancing done at a manageable cost.
  • No further guidance cut, and no new restriction on Librela.

Watch closely

  • U.S. companion animal declines that narrow but stay negative, and single-digit dermatology declines.
  • Owner cash between $1,700 million and $2,100 million, and free cash flow between 70% and 85% of net income.
  • Net debt to operating profit plus depreciation between 2.0 and 2.75 times, or refinancing at higher coupons.
  • Buybacks that draw on cash, a new Librela review, or timelines slipping in the pipeline.

Breaks the thesis

  • U.S. companion animal sales worse than -10% for two more quarters, or a persistent double-digit dermatology decline.
  • Owner cash below $1,700 million with no temporary cause, or free cash flow below 70% of net income with a rising inventory.
  • Net debt above 2.75 times, no credible refinancing path, or debt-funded buybacks while cash falls.
  • Another guidance cut above 5%, or a material restriction on Librela.
