What Brightstar is

Brightstar Lottery PLC was International Game Technology until July 2025, when it sold its Gaming and Digital business and changed its name and ticker. What remains is a lottery company. It supplies lottery systems and runs facilities-management contracts for lotteries, and it holds the Italian operator licenses for the Lotto game and for Scratch & Win instant tickets. It also has a stake in lottery operations in New Jersey and in Brazil. Fiscal years end December 31.

The business is built on long contracts, but it does not own all of what it consolidates. Revenue was $2,511 million in 2025, $2,512 million in 2024 and $2,529 million in 2023, essentially flat. The ownership matters more than the headline:

  • Lotto (Lottoitalia): 61.5% owned. A license for nine years began on December 1, 2025, and its final payment of €1.43 billion ($1.675 billion) was made in April 2026.
  • Scratch & Win (Lotterie Nazionali): 64% owned. The concession expires in September 2028, and no renewal price has been announced.
  • New Jersey (Northstar): about 65% effective interest. Brazil: two 50% joint ventures that are not consolidated.
  • Customers: the largest customer was about 37% of 2025 revenue, and Italy is about 40% of the retained business (annual report, as I have it).
  • Control: De Agostini held about 46% of the ordinary economics and about 63% of the votes at February 2026, including loyalty votes. Special voting shares are not counted as extra shares.

In 2025 income attributable to minority holders was $136 million, against $135 million of consolidated continuing income. That is the reason I do not value consolidated EBITDA.

It deducts the license payments in the years they fall due (2034, 2037, 2043) and values a Gordon continuation after 2043 less the license payments after that date.

  • What agreement would prove: little. The two Base values are 59.6% apart, so there is no agreement to give credit for, and in the Bear case both are floored at zero, which makes their match meaningless.

History, peers and growth diagnostics

A clean history of the market price against owner cash for the retained business does not exist, because the old International Game Technology price included the sold gaming business and the $3 special dividend. At today's value of about $1,746 million (185 million shares), the three-year average of $306.7 million of parent cash before fees is 5.7 times. The 2025 figure of $162 million is 10.8 times. Those ratios leave out license costs, and the all-in cash for 2025 was negative, so I do not pick the cheapest of them.

For a peer I looked at Pollard Banknote (TSX: PBL), a lottery products company. On my calculation from its reports it trades at about 5.5 times adjusted EBITDA, including its share of a joint venture and lease liabilities, with an EBITDA margin of about 19% against about 46% for Brightstar. Pollard has no Italian operating licenses, and I cannot value the minority stakes in Brightstar's subsidiaries from outside, so I give the comparison no weight and do not apply 5.5 times to Brightstar. I did not build a sector median.

Third-party rounded estimates put adjusted earnings per share at about $0.52 in 2026 and $0.72 in 2027, against $0.91 in 2025. That is 18.2 times this year's adjusted earnings, growth of 38% and a ratio of growth to earnings multiple of 0.47. It looks cheap, but the base is depressed, the business changed and the figure ignores license amortization, interest and stock pay that the cash view includes. I give it no weight.

Reasons to own Brightstar

  • An embedded operating moat. Reliable lottery systems, retailer networks and regulatory execution are hard to replace and support repeat contracts.
  • The Lotto term is secured. The nine-year license from December 2025 is in place and paid.
  • Cash recovery after the investment peak. If capital spending really falls to $250 million, parent cash would rise from $52 million to $330 million, and the Bull reaches $19.79.
  • Execution on the sale and funding. Management closed the Gaming sale, returned capital, repaid more than $2 billion of debt and refinanced part of the bonds in September.

These justify watching. They do not show that the cash arrives in time for the dividend.

What could go wrong?

  • Concession economics. Italy and a large public counterparty are a big part of revenue, and the 2028 Scratch & Win renewal is unpriced. A fee of €1,400 million alone takes the Base to $6.70.
  • Funding and the dividend. Fees, contract capital spending and maturities come before the cash recovery. A debt cost of 7.5% takes the Base to $4.76, and refinancing that fails could force dilution, a cut or worse.
  • False cash normalization. The $400 million ambition excludes up-front fees, and a drop in capital spending is not yet shown. Capital spending of $350 million in 2029 gives $4.01.
  • Ownership leakage. Minority claims, restrictions on moving cash, loyalty voting control and stock pay mean a good consolidated year can still be a weaker year for ordinary holders.
