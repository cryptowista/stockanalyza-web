In the third quarter Synchrony earned $864 million for common shareholders, down from $1,057 million a year earlier. Earnings per share fell only from $2.86 to $2.68. The gap is the share count: in nine months the company retired 38.3 million shares, taking the total from 347.4 million to 311.4 million. Buybacks are a real part of this story, and they are also the reason "EPS growth" at a card lender needs a second look at the aggregate earnings and the capital behind them.

At $73.64 on October 23, 2026, the shares trade 3.7% above my Base value of $71.00, so the Base is 3.6% below the price. The Bear value of $43.70 is 40.7% below the price and the Bull value of $91.60 is 24.4% above it. A deep credit stress gives $26.44, which is a stress test and not a floor. I assign no probabilities to the cases. The required margin of safety of 30% gives a buy range of $41.50 to $62.26 and a conservative limit of $41.49. For existing holders this is a hold. For new money it is a watchlist name.

Valuation route: Banks, a deposit-funded consumer credit bank. Economic classification: partial, 8 to 10 of 12, with Quality Growth as the supported floor and Compounder not established. What Synchrony is

Synchrony is a bank holding company that runs private-label and co-branded credit cards, installment loans and savings products through merchant partners. Five sales platforms report as one operating segment, so a sum-of-the-parts valuation is not justified. Deposits of $83.0 billion fund about 83% of the balance sheet, and loans of $103.3 billion carry an allowance of $10.3 billion, or 10.01% of loans.

The business depends on a short list of partners. Amazon, Lowe's, PayPal including Venmo, Sam's Club and TJX generated 54% of 2025 interest and fees and held 52% of receivables. Their contracts run to 2030 to 2035, and 22 of the 25 largest relationships expire in 2028 or later. Long contracts are evidence of a durable franchise, not a promise that the economics stay the same at renewal, because the merchant owns the customer relationship at the checkout.

Newer programs (OnePay with Walmart, Amazon Pay Later, the Lowe's commercial portfolio, Versatile Credit) extend the same activity and do not change the risk class. They are also too young to show their eventual returns, so I model them and do not give them credit for results that do not exist yet.

The severe test (all uninsured and 20% of other deposits) leaves a $7.3 billion gap that would need collections, collateralized borrowing or asset actions. That does not signal distress, but it is why I treat distributions as the first thing to go in a stress.

Primary valuation: residual income

The method values the common equity from its book value plus the present value of profits above the required return, with the cash that can be paid out under a capital rule. I forecast five years ending October 26 of each year to 2031 and discount at a 12.5% cost of equity: a 4.96% ten-year Treasury yield (Federal Reserve, October 22) plus an estimated 5% equity premium times a risk loading of 1.5 gives 12.46%, rounded to 12.5%. The 1.5 is my loading, not a measured regression beta. Base assumptions, all estimates:

  • Loan growth of 3% a year, net charge-offs of 5.75%, an allowance of 10% of loans and operating costs of 5.35% of average loans.
  • Retained capital at the current ratio: the higher of $13,584 million and 12.851% of risk-weighted assets, so growth is funded before anything is distributed.
  • A 25% tax rate, a 1% tax on buybacks, and a constant preferred coupon of $119.7 million.
  • A terminal return on common equity of 17% and growth of 3% after year five, which gives a justified price of 1.47 times book.

Dividend and buybacks

The board declared a dividend of $0.34 a share, with a record date of November 5 and payment on November 16, 2026. Annualized, $1.36 is a 1.85% yield. Only the declared quarter is a commitment, and future dividends depend on the board and on regulators. At $428 million in year one, the Base dividend is covered 6.25 times by common earnings, with a payout of 16%.

Do not read the buyback pace as an income yield. Over twelve months the company paid $420 million in dividends and $3,840 million for treasury stock, a total of $4,260 million against common earnings of $3,242 million. The excess released capital that had built up, and it will not repeat indefinitely. I add no buyback yield to the holder return in the model.

Reasons to own SYF

  • An embedded distribution advantage. Long merchant relationships, underwriting data and servicing scale at the point of purchase are hard to copy, even though merchants keep bargaining power.
  • A positive return spread. After normalized credit costs, growth reserves, operating costs and preferred dividends, the Base still earns about 18% on common book against a 12.5% cost of equity.
  • Capital and funding capacity. A 12.9% CET1 ratio against a 7.0% requirement and $83 billion of deposits allow for growth and distributions when earnings exceed the capital retained.
  • Per-share record. Common book per share compounded at 16.9% a year over five years, and 38.3 million shares were retired in nine months.

What could go wrong?

  • Credit and program economics. New cohorts could season into higher losses while reserves rise, and retailer sharing may offset less than the 25% I assume. Lower normalized profitability cuts both distributions and the multiple.
  • Partner and regulatory economics. A concentrated partner group can reprice at renewal, and fee or interest rules can change. The earlier late-fee rule was vacated, and an inquiry or proposal is not an enacted cap.
  • Capital and funding. Deposit repricing or credit stress can force earnings retention and halt buybacks at the moment shares get cheaper.
  • Execution and allocation. Fraud losses doubled in the quarter ($91 million against $43 million a year earlier), and buybacks above intrinsic value can destroy value while EPS still grows. Incentives tied to EPS and return on equity can reward a smaller denominator.

Management execution

Management credibility scores 10 of the 14 points I can assess, within a possible range of 10 to 16 out of 20. Three dimensions (guidance versus outcomes, capital conversion commitments and acquisition returns) cannot be reconstructed from public data and are not counted as zero. Regulatory capital discipline, metric definitions and per-share outcomes earned the top marks.

  • Q3 common income fell 18% while EPS fell 6%: buybacks softened the decline without reversing the aggregate fall.
  • The 2025 incentive plan recorded a miss on receivables growth and a zero payout for that component, though the overall annual award paid 115.4% of target.
  • Half of the long-term award depends on cumulative EPS and average return on equity, which is why I check aggregate income and capital next to the per-share figures.
  • The June 2026 reclassification of internal-use software raised reported tangible equity, and common book did not change. I use common book to avoid rewarding a presentation change.

What I would watch from here

Green

  • Seasoned loan growth with stable or falling year-on-year delinquencies (30+ day today: 4.34%)
  • Allowance at or above 9.5% of loans without reliance on releases (today: 10.01%)
  • Aggregate normalized common income growing with a funded capital bridge
  • Fraud losses falling from $91 million a quarter without cutting essential spending
  • OnePay and co-brand growth disclosing usable returns

Yellow

  • Net charge-offs drifting toward 6% or 30+ day delinquencies up 50 basis points year on year
  • EPS rising while aggregate common income keeps falling
  • Fraud staying near twice the prior year, or costs above 5.7% of loans
  • Deposit cost rising faster than loan yields
  • Buybacks at prices well above the model's fair values

Red

  • Net charge-offs above 6.5% for two quarters without pricing or sharing compensation
  • CET1 below 11%, or falling more than 100 basis points alongside large buybacks
  • Rapid deposit runoff or a rollover problem
  • Loss or permanent repricing of a major partner
  • A binding fee or capital rule that invalidates normal returns

Nothing is red today. The next official earnings date is not yet announced. The dividend record date is November 5 and the payment date November 16. The monitoring points are loss and delinquency development, growth reserves, fraud and servicing expense, after-sharing profit before provisions, and the actual capital retained.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Persistent credit deterioration that retailer sharing does not compensate.
  2. Capital or funding erosion that stops the funded operating plan.
  3. Loss or repricing of a major program that permanently cuts common returns.

Stress test and independent check

I recomputed every value in this analysis separately, with 50-digit decimal arithmetic and a different solution path for the buyback and tax loop: 2,958 checks, no discrepancies. That checks the arithmetic, not the forecasts. A second review challenged the claims, normalization, retailer sharing assumption, capital policy, peer definitions and the strongest opposing view. The inputs that matter most are the cost of equity, the terminal return and the yield on loans, with loan losses and retailer sharing next. The strongest bull argument is that new programs season well, fraud and servicing costs fall, and returns above 17% last, so the 17.84% return the price needs is feasible and my value may be too low. The strongest bear argument is that sharing absorbs less than assumed, young books need both higher losses and higher reserves, and merchants take more of the economics, while buybacks above intrinsic value destroy value even as EPS grows. My $43.70 and $26.44 are not floors, and a low price to earnings is not a margin of safety on its own.

Final verdict: hold at $73.64, watchlist for new money

Synchrony is a profitable, cyclical consumer bank priced close to what I think it is worth. A low headline price to earnings does not offer a margin of safety once normal credit losses, growth reserves and the capital that supports receivables are counted. The price is above all six entry cases.

Verdict: HOLD at $73.64, watchlist for new money. Base value $71.00, 3.6% below the price, cross-check $74.83, Confidence B (21.25/25). Required margin of safety 30%, buy range $41.50 to $62.26, conservative buy limit $41.49.

For an existing holder, the original cost of the shares is irrelevant, and nothing here says to sell: the price sits near my value, and a review is warranted if it approaches the Bull value of $91.60 with unchanged fundamentals, or if the thesis breaks at any price. New money waits for a price at or below $62.26, and a clean credit and funding picture. If $41.49 ever appears, check why before treating it as a bargain.

The 11.66% a year that a buyer at $73.64 can earn in the Base depends on a smaller share count as much as on a bigger business, and the shares only shrink for as long as the credit book lets the company keep releasing capital.
