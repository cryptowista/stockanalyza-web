A screener will tell you Innodata trades at about 10 times free cash flow. In the first half of 2026 the company reported $159.1 million of free cash flow on $182.2 million of revenue, which would be an 87% cash margin. It is not that kind of business. The cash came from $65.2 million of customer advances and a $71.9 million rise in payables and accruals, including $61.8 million of project costs incurred but not yet paid at June 30. Take those out and the trailing twelve-month owner cash is about $38 million (my estimate), a 2.0% yield on the $1.9 billion market value.

At $54.92, an intraday trade at 7:46 pm Prague time (1:46 pm New York) on October 20, 2026, the shares trade 7.5% above my triangulated Base value of $51.07, a downside of 7.0%. The Bear value is $24.47, 55.5% below the price, and the Bull value of $71.92 is 31.0% above it. Confidence is grade C (15/25), the required margin of safety (MOS) is 40%, and the maximum buy price is $30.64. The price is close to my Base and well above the buy ceiling, so the verdict is Hold.

Valuation route: a listed provider of data engineering for AI developers, a services business paid per project, not a subscription software company. Primary method: 25 times normalized owner cash in 2028, discounted to today. Secondary method: 18 times 2028 operating profit. Weights are 60% and 40%. A ten-year cash flow model ($55.03) is a cross-check and carries no weight. Classification: quality growth, with no compounder premium.

What Innodata is

Innodata builds the data that AI developers train and test their models on: large training datasets, human evaluation of model output, and, more recently, programs for agentic and robotic systems. Its customers are a small number of large technology companies, and it competes with Appen, Scale AI, TELUS Digital, Surge AI and the customers' own teams. Smaller Synodex and Agility businesses sit alongside the main AI data services unit.

Revenue went from $86.8 million in 2023 to $170.5 million in 2024 and $251.7 million in 2025, a 70.3% compound rate over two years. In the second quarter of 2026 it reached $92.1 million, up 58%, with an adjusted gross margin of 49% against 43% a year earlier. That is a real improvement, but it is project revenue. At-will programs can be cut, and nothing in the filings turns it into subscription income.

The customer mix is the sharper story. The largest customer fell from 56% of revenue in the first quarter to 37% in the second, and a second customer rose from 17% to 34%. In dollars, from the rounded percentages, the first dropped from about $50.5 million to $34.1 million a quarter while the second doubled from about $15.3 million to $31.3 million (my arithmetic). Concentration moved, it did not disappear: the top two accounts were 71% of second quarter revenue.

The net financial assets of $110.4 million are worth $3.11 a share and are added to enterprise value once. I treat the customer and project balances as a reserve, not as spendable surplus. It is an economic adjustment, not restricted cash, and forecast cash flows assume ordinary future receipts and payments, so the reserve is not charged twice. Leases stay in operating costs, and the undrawn credit line is not counted as cash. If I used the release's $134 million instead, Base would be $51.42 to $51.70. If I counted all gross cash with no reserve, Base would be $54.70.

There were 34.383 million common shares on July 31, 2026. About 2.0 million options were exercised in the first half for $10.9 million of proceeds, which is most of the increase from 32.3 million at year end and is already in the June cash. My valuation count is 35.5 million shares: the common shares plus an allowance for earned options, with future awards charged as a cash-like cost in forecast profit and not as extra shares. A full statutory count near 36.5 million would give $49.67.

The bigger share question is the new at-the-market (ATM) program, filed on August 6: the company may sell up to $300 million of stock through five sales agents, paying up to 2% commission. It has no obligation to sell, and the 8-K gives no specific use for the money. If it sold the full amount at $54.92 it would issue 5.46 million shares, about 16% more, and raise $294 million net. If the cash keeps its value, Base becomes $51.44. If half of it is wasted, $47.85. A buyer who sees only the dilution and ignores the cash is making a mistake, but so is one who ignores that the cash needs a return. I do not know what was sold after August.

The primary valuation: 25 times owner cash in 2028

My Base starts from 2026 revenue of $360 million, which is the consensus level and above the guidance floor of about $352 million, and grows it 28% in 2027 and 25% in 2028, to $576.0 million. At a 21% operating margin after SBC, operating profit is $121.0 million. After 24% tax, $14.4 million of depreciation, capital spending at 3.5% of sales ($20.2 million) and $13.8 million of working capital to fund growth, owner cash in 2028 is $72.3 million.

A buyer at the end of 2028 paying 25 times that cash would pay $1,808.6 million, a 4% cash yield. That multiple is my assumption for a business still growing fast, not a peer median, and it would be too rich for a mature outsourcing company. I discount the exit value and the interim cash back to October 20 at 10% (a 2.197 year transition), add the $110.4 million of net financial assets, and divide by 35.5 million shares. The primary Base is $47.70.

A price check against history

Historical cash gives little to hold on to. Free cash flow was close to zero in 2023, $27.1 million in 2024 and $35.6 million in 2025, and then the first half of 2026 jumped. I could not build a clean multi-year history of enterprise value to owner cash with matching definitions, so history gets no weight in my value. On today's numbers the shares are 5.95 times trailing sales and 32 times trailing operating profit after the net financial assets.

The growth-adjusted check is not decisive either. Four analysts expect adjusted EPS of $1.65 in 2026 and $2.09 in 2027, which is 33.3 times and 26.3 times at $54.92, with a PEG of 0.99 on 26.7% growth. These are non-GAAP figures, and the 2027 numbers sit behind a paywall in more detail, so I read the PEG as a rough diagnostic. The snapshot I was given also shows 2026 free cash flow of $88.4 million, below the $159.1 million already reported for six months, which suggests the analysts expect part of the prepayment cash to go back out.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C calls for a margin of safety of 35% to 50%. I use 40% and add nothing on top: concentration, customer-funded cash, SBC and the share sale program are already in the scenarios and the bridge. The maximum buy price is $51.07 x (1 - 40%) = $30.64.

Four numbers answer different questions: what the shares are worth ($51.07), the return from buying at $54.92 and holding to the end of 2028 (5.7% a year), the price that gives a 12% annual return ($48.36) and the price that leaves a 40% margin of safety ($30.64). The return path assumes no distributions, no share sale, normal working capital and $135.7 million of retained owner cash, and sells at $62.03 a share at the end of 2028. It leaves out interest on the cash, fees and personal taxes, and it is not today's value.

The dividend

There is none and I assume none. This is a capital appreciation case. A large cash balance and an undrawn credit line do not create a dividend or a buyback.

Reasons to own

  • Growth and operating leverage that show up in the accounts. Revenue grew 56.1% in the first half, and the operating margin rose from 14.7% to 18.0%.
  • A better mix. The adjusted gross margin is 49%, up from 43% a year earlier, so growth has not come at the cost of margin.
  • A second large account. If the customer that went from 17% to 34% of revenue stays and grows, concentration risk becomes growth, not exposure.
  • Reusable data and research. Datasets and evaluation tools may serve more than one customer, which would add profit without matching labor.
  • A positive balance sheet after the reserve. Net financial assets of $110.4 million remain after counting customer and vendor money as not yours.

Main risks

  • Two customers. They were 71% of second quarter revenue, and the largest one's revenue fell by about a third in a single quarter. Programs can be cancelled.
  • A cash reversal. $128.8 million of customer advances and unpaid project costs will go out as projects settle, so reported cash flow can turn negative.
  • Dilution. SBC is rising, and the $300 million share sale program could add about 16% to the share count if used in full at today's price.
  • Project, not subscription, revenue. Renewal depends on delivery, and customers can bring work in house or switch providers.
  • Cost pressure. Cloud, labor and licensing costs can pull margins below 20% while revenue still grows.
  • Leadership change and capital use. A new CEO and an untested plan for new cash add execution risk.

What I would watch from here

Supports the thesis

  • 2026 growth at 40% or more with the operating margin heading to 20% or 21%.
  • Top two customers below 60% of revenue, and the largest customer stable or growing.
  • Customer advances and project payables settled without a sharp fall in cash, and ATM shares, proceeds and use reported together.

Watch closely

  • Top two customers between 60% and 75%, or the largest customer's revenue falling again with replacement elsewhere.
  • Operating margin between 15% and 20%, or capital spending and cloud costs above plan.
  • Cash growth that depends on timing, or issuance and its use that are not clear.

Breaks the thesis

  • Guidance cut below 40% growth, or top two customers above 75%, or the loss of a major client.
  • Normalized owner cash weakening after advances and payables settle, or a shortfall that makes cash quality impossible to verify.
  • Share issuance used on projects that earn less than the cost of capital, or a restatement or control weakness.

The most important thesis breakers

  • A major client. Losing or sharply cutting one without profitable replacement.
  • Cash conversion. Weak owner cash after the advance-funded obligations are settled.
  • Capital allocation. ATM-funded spending that earns too little, or compensation dilution that keeps climbing.

Stress test and independent check

I rebuilt the model from its stated inputs with separate code and reproduced: the twelve-month and first half bridges, including the $117.9 million working capital swing and the $110.4 million of net financial assets; the annual rows for Bear, Base and Bull; the $47.70 and $56.12 values and the $51.07 blend; the $55.03 cash flow value; the 16.2% divergence; the $30.64 buy price; the $62.03 end-2028 value, the 5.7% return and the $48.36 entry; the 1.46 point reverse margin; the ATM cases ($51.44 and $47.85); the rate, margin and capital spending tables; and the $99.52 joint case. I found no construction error. Cutting every future cash flow by 20% and 40% gave $45.46 and $39.85 in my run against $45.51 and $39.96; the difference is the 2026 stub, which the original does not cut and is immaterial.

From the company's documents I confirmed the second quarter and first half results, the cash and Treasury balances, the $67.0 million of advances and $61.8 million of unpaid project costs, operating cash flow, capital spending and SBC, the option exercises and proceeds, the July 31 share count, the ATM terms, the November 5 reporting date, the CEO change, the customer percentages, the revenue and growth targets for 2023 to 2025 and the 2026 guidance changes, and the four-analyst consensus. I could not re-read the $9.5 million pension claim, the financed licenses, the first half customer shares (46% and 26%), the $13.1 million expected capital spending, the $88.4 million consensus free cash flow for 2026, the controls conclusion and the current ratio, so those come from the supplied analysis.

Three further points. Discounting at year end is conservative, and mid-year timing would give a Base of about $51.22, 0.3% higher. The price is an intraday trade of $54.92; the October 19 close was $54.00, 1.7% lower, and the verdict is the same at either price. And two screen figures do not reconcile: the broker page shows EPS of $1.43 with a P/E of 41.86, which would be 38.4 times, and a price to free cash flow of 29.25 against my 10.3 on reported cash flow.

Main data limits: I do not know October cash or how much stock the company has sold under its program, the release and the filing define cash after prepayments differently, and customer contract terms are not public. These are why Confidence is C.

Final verdict: hold at $54.92

Innodata is growing fast, earns a real 18% operating margin, and its second large customer is arriving as the first one shrinks. But the free cash flow that makes the screens look cheap is largely other people's money, the price already sits near my Base, and the buy ceiling of $30.64 is 44% below it. At $54.92 there is no margin of safety in either direction.

Verdict: HOLD at $54.92, watchlist for new money. Triangulated Base value $51.07, downside 7.0%, Confidence C (15/25), required margin of safety 40%, maximum buy price $30.64.

For holders this means keeping the position and watching the November 5 report, because the gap to Base is small. For new money it means waiting for a lower price or for owner cash to hold up after the projects settle. About half of the $250 million in cash and Treasury notes belongs, for now, to customers and suppliers, and the stock is priced as if all of it were Innodata's.
