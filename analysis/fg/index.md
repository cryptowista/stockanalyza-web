## What F&G is

F&G sells retirement savings products, mainly fixed indexed annuities through independent marketing organizations, banks and brokers, plus indexed life insurance, pension risk transfer deals and funding agreements for institutions. It collects the premiums, invests them with Blackstone as the main manager, and earns the spread between what the portfolio yields and what it credits to policyholders. Assets under management before reinsurance reached a record $74.7 billion in June, up 8% in a year.

The record is less useful than it looks. Retained assets, the part F&G keeps and earns a spread on, were $55.9 billion, up 1%, because a growing share of new business is passed to reinsurance partners for a fee. That shift is deliberate and needs less capital, but it means the headline growth figure and the earnings base no longer move together.

Fidelity National Financial owns about 70% of the shares after handing 12% to its own shareholders at the end of 2025. Chris Blunt, chief executive since 2019, retired on June 30, 2026. Conor Murphy, the former finance chief, took over, and Michael Bailey joined from Corebridge as finance chief in August.

## Which earnings figure to trust

- **Reported net income is not the measure.** It swings with the market value of derivatives, embedded options in the annuities and other items. The June quarter loss of $81 million came from $144 million of such swings. Over time it matters, but quarter to quarter it hides more than it shows.
- **Adjusted net earnings are the company's measure.** They remove those swings but keep the actual return on alternatives. I use them as the starting point and convert them to a return on equity.
- **The equity base excludes unrealized bond losses.** Accumulated other comprehensive loss, mostly from bonds bought before rates rose, was $1.66 billion, or $12.15 a share. The policy liabilities are not marked to market, and 97% of the bonds are investment grade, so I use book value without these losses, $45.92 a share. A forced sale would turn part of the loss real, which is a Bear risk.
- **The alternatives drive the gap.** Each percentage point of return on the $4.0 billion portfolio is about $32 million after tax, or 0.5 points of return on equity (my arithmetic at a 21% tax rate). The first half earned about 7%, against the 12% to 14% the company assumes.
- **The yardstick moved.** In January 2026 the company moved about $6 to $7 billion of loan-like assets out of the alternatives bucket and raised the expected return on what was left from 10% to 12% to 14%. Past shortfalls were restated on the new basis: $145 million in 2024, $216 million in 2025 and $93 million in the first half of 2026.

## Primary valuation: residual income on book value

An insurer is worth its book value plus the present value of what it earns above its cost of equity, or minus what it earns below it. I start from $45.92 a share, project the return on equity for five years, carry the last year forward with 2.5% growth, and discount at 10.5%. Earnings not paid out stay in book value; I assume 65% of earnings goes to dividends and buybacks. The Base value is **$28.26**, or 0.62 times book.

- **Why 7.5%:** today's run rate is 6.7% with alternatives at about 7%. If they earn 9%, still well below the company's 12% to 14%, the return rises to about 7.6%. Fee income from reinsurance partners grows, but tighter spreads on new business offset part of it.
- **Why 10.5%:** a 10-year Treasury yield of about 4.4% plus 1.26 times a 5% equity premium gives 10.7%. I use 10.5%, which leans slightly generous, and show 11.5% in the grid.
- **What the value is made of:** book value $45.92, minus $5.80 for five years of earning below the cost of equity, minus $11.86 for the years after. The Base value is below book because F&G does not earn its cost of equity in any year of my Base. Two thirds of the discount to book comes from the years after 2031.
- **Buybacks:** the model treats buybacks as if they happened at book value. Buying at 0.47 times book is worth more than that, about $0.86 of book value per share for each $100 million spent, but the authorization had only about $12 to $15 million left after June. I leave the gain out of all cases.

## Cross-check: the stock's own price-to-book history

A second model built on the same return on equity would only repeat the first, so the check is the market's own history. At six quarter ends from December 2024 to June 2026, F&G traded at 0.92, 0.73, 0.69, 0.68, 0.54 and 0.57 times book excluding unrealized losses (my arithmetic from market value and reported equity). The median of 0.68 times applied to $45.92 gives **$31.40**, 11% above my Base. Most of those quarters had a twelve-month return on equity of 8% to 10%, above today's run rate, so the history is a ceiling for the current earnings rather than a target.

There is no meaningful growth-adjusted multiple here. Reported earnings per share swing between profit and loss with market values, and adjusted earnings per share fell from $4.30 in 2024 to $3.64 in 2025, so a price to earnings to growth ratio has no stable denominator.

## Valuation triangulation

- Primary (residual income, Base): $28.26. This is the central value.
- Cross-check (median price-to-book history): $31.40, 10.5% from the Base, below the 20% level that would need a closer look.
- Nearby assumptions (cost of equity 10% to 11%, long-run return 7.25% to 7.75%): $25.45 to $31.48.

I do not average the two. The history comes from a period of higher returns, and averaging would lift the Base on evidence that is weaker than the earnings themselves.

## Dividend and buybacks

F&G pays $0.25 a quarter, $1.00 a year, a 4.65% yield at $21.50. It was raised by 14% in late 2025. At the first-half run rate of about $3.06 of adjusted earnings per share, the payout is about a third. The holding company also pays about $165 million of interest and $17 million of preferred dividends a year, and the cash for all of it comes from statutory dividends of the insurance subsidiaries, which the company does not report quarterly. In the first half it bought back 4.5 million shares at an average of $26.44, about 0.57 times book.

## Reasons to own F&G

- The price is 0.47 times book excluding unrealized losses and assumes today's weak earnings forever, so even partial recovery in the alternatives adds value quickly, about $2.80 a share per point of return.
- The bond portfolio has held up: 97% investment grade, credit impairments of 6 basis points a year over five years and 2 in the first half of 2026.
- Core retail sales are growing while the company walks away from fixed-rate annuities that do not meet its return threshold, which is a sign of pricing discipline rather than chasing volume.
- Reinsurance partners and a sidecar take a growing share of new business for a fee, which needs less capital and should lift the return on equity over time.
- A 4.65% dividend covered about three times by current adjusted earnings, and buybacks done at about half of book.

## What could go wrong?

- Alternatives. The $4.0 billion portfolio is 64% of equity and 73% private equity. If it keeps earning 6% to 7%, the return on equity stays near the price's assumption and the discount to book is justified.
- Parent support. Moody's put the rating on negative outlook over Fidelity National's falling stake. A downgrade would raise funding costs and could hurt sales through banks and brokers.
- Reinsurance counterparties. Recoverables are $20.9 billion, 3.3 times equity. Most are backed by assets F&G holds, but the exposure is large for a company this size.
- Credit. Private structured securities and loan pools are a large part of the portfolio. Losses have been low, but a recession would test them, as in the Bear.
- Rates and surrenders. A sharp rise in rates would deepen unrealized losses and tempt policyholders to move money. Most retail annuities carry surrender charges, which limits but does not remove this.
- Governance. Fidelity National controls the board and capital decisions, and the leadership changed in the middle of 2026.

## Management execution

- Asset growth has been delivered: $74.7 billion before reinsurance, a record.
- Returns have not. The alternatives have missed the expected return every year since 2022, and the twelve-month adjusted return on equity fell from 8.8% to 8.0% in a year.
- The January 2026 redefinition of the alternatives raised the expected return at the same time as the results fell short of it, which makes the shortfall harder to compare with the past.
- Capital decisions have been sound lately: buybacks at about 0.57 times book, a capital ratio above target, and fixed-rate annuity sales cut when returns were too low. The 2025 share sale at $35 was below book value.
- Guidance is qualitative. Management gave no numbers for 2026 earnings, which leaves each quarter as the only test.

On my ten-point management check (two points each), F&G scores 10 of 20: strong on balance sheet discipline, buybacks and dividend coverage, weak on investment returns, earnings conversion and consistency of definitions.

## What I would watch from here

The September quarter report on November 5 should show four things for the Base to hold: adjusted earnings of at least $0.75 a share, alternatives back at 7% or more annualized, book value excluding unrealized losses of at least $46 a share, and a capital ratio still above 400% after the new loan fund charges. A new buyback authorization would also matter at this price.

### Green

- Annualized return on equity of 7.5% or more for two quarters.
- Alternatives returning 9% or more annualized.
- Moody's outlook back to stable, or Fidelity National stating explicit support.

### Yellow

- Return on equity between 6.0% and 7.5%, where the first half of 2026 sits (6.7%).

- Fidelity National cutting its stake further without a support agreement.

- Book value excluding unrealized losses below $44 a share from credit or alternatives write-downs.

## Final verdict: hold at $21.50

Book value is real, the bonds are fine and the dividend is covered. What is missing is a return on that book anywhere near what it costs to own it. Half of book is a fair price for an insurer that earns 6%; it becomes cheap only once the alternatives start earning what the company's own long-term assumption says they should.

**Verdict: HOLD at $21.50, watchlist for new money. Base $28.26, 31.4% above the price, Bear $19.50, Bull $35.55, cross-check $31.40, Confidence C (15/25). Required margin of safety 42.5%, maximum buy price $16.25, range $14.64 to $18.10 under nearby assumptions.**

New money buys only at or below $16.25. For an existing holder the original cost is irrelevant, and a Base value above the price supports holding with a 4.65% dividend; I would review a holding above the Base of about $28. The next test is the September quarter report on November 5, 2026.
