## The tender offer, in plain terms

On November 9, 2026 DoubleDown signed a tender offer and cooperation agreement with DoubleU Games, a Korean-listed company that owns 1,661,191 of the 2,477,672 common shares, or 67.05%. The board, on the advice of a special committee of independent directors, unanimously recommends that holders tender. The price moved from the $11.25 non-binding proposal of April to $16.25, so the committee did get more. The deal is also a going-private transaction under U.S. rule 13e-3, the rule that adds extra disclosure when a controlling holder buys out the minority.

## What DoubleDown is

DoubleDown Interactive, based in Seoul with its U.S. operations in Las Vegas, makes free-to-play social casino games (DoubleDown Casino and others) and a smaller real-money iGaming business in Europe, SuprNation. In the third quarter social casino was $76.8 million of revenue and iGaming $17.4 million. The company says the social casino market as a whole is in secular decline, so the business case is not growth: it is a high-margin cash machine that has moved from paying app stores a cut to selling directly to players. Direct-to-consumer sales were 58.0% of social casino revenue in the third quarter, against 20.9% a year earlier and 10% for all of 2024.

In 2025 the company bought WHOW Games for $61.6 million in cash, a German social casino developer, which lifted revenue and the payer conversion rate but not the underlying trend. In the same year it wrote off $8 million of goodwill on SuprNation. The chief executive is In Keuk Kim and the chief financial officer is Joseph Sigrist. The company gives no financial guidance that I found.

## Primary valuation: ten years of owner free cash flow

The primary method discounts ten years of owner free cash flow at 10%, the framework default for an established company, and I test 9% and 11%. Base shrinks cash flow 3.0% a year for five years and 2.0% for the next five, reaching $99 million in 2031 and $89 million in 2036, with terminal growth of -1.0%. That matches the trend: social casino revenue is falling about 3% a year, offset by margin gains that cannot go on indefinitely. The terminal value is 9.0 times 2036 cash flow. I add 90% of the excess cash, $432 million. The primary value is **$27.49**. Terminal value share: 23% of the value at a ten-year horizon, well under the 75% level at which I would check it.

## Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 5.5 times operating profit

The cross-check takes the first five years of cash flow and values the business at the end of 2031 at a multiple of operating profit. Operating profit starts at $147 million (trailing, before the impairment) and falls 2.5% a year to $130 million in 2031. At the Base exit of 5.5 times, the business is worth $712 million in 2031, and I add the excess cash. The secondary value is **$22.42**.

**Why 5.5 times.** Owner free cash flow is 78% of operating profit, and at a 10% required return a multiple of 5.5 times implies growth of -3.7% a year forever. That fits a business with shrinking sales, and it is close to the -3% and -2% of my Base path. Bear uses 3.0 times, implying -9.5% a year, a business that loses a tenth of its cash flow every year. Bull uses 7.0 times, implying -1.9%, a smaller decline. Today's enterprise value is only 1.4 times operating profit, so every case is a re-rating of the operating business. Exit value share: 40% of the secondary value comes from the exit at a five-year horizon, under the 85% level at which I would test it.

## What is the market already pricing in?

At $15.08 the market value is $747 million and the enterprise value, after net cash of $540 million, is $207 million: 1.4 times trailing operating profit and 1.3 times adjusted EBITDA of $158 million. Owner free cash flow of $115 million is 56% of that enterprise value. Run backward on the Base case, $15.08 needs cash flow and operating profit that shrink 16 percentage points a year faster than my Base, or a discount rate of 25%, or a starting cash flow of $30 million, 74% below mine. No exit multiple alone gets there: with a zero exit value the Base would still be $20.49, because the cash and the first five years of cash flow are already worth more than $15.08.

The market is not pricing the business at all. It is pricing the offer, with some discount for the chance that it fails and some for the fact that a controlling holder, not the minority, decides what the minority gets. At $16.25 the offer capitalizes the operating business at 1.8 times operating profit and 1.7 times adjusted EBITDA, and at 7.3 times trailing earnings.

The growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) is not meaningful, because earnings per ADS fell 44% in the quarter and the three-year trend is negative, and I use it as a diagnostic only. Free cash flow quality is high because capital spending is almost zero, but the trend is flat to down: operating cash flow was $136.8 million in 2025 against $148.4 million in 2024. As a stress test, taking 20% off both the terminal and exit values lowers the Base to $23.44, 6.1% below the Base.

## How the buy price is set

Confidence C starts at a 42.5% margin of safety. I added nothing for control, because it is charged in the cash discount in my scenarios and in the governance score, and I added nothing for the 20.3% divergence, which is a warning and not a flag, and was charged to Confidence instead. A 42.5% discount to the $24.96 Base gives a **maximum buy price of $14.35**. That is 5% below today's price and 9% below my Bear value. The framework allows a buy only at or below that price, so the verdict is Hold. It is a close call: the price is only 5.1% above the line, and the tender offer of $16.25 is 13% above it.

## Reasons to own DDI

- **Cash.** $582.1 million of cash and short-term investments against $41.9 million of loans and leases, which is $10.90 an ADS of net cash.
- **Margin and conversion.** A 39.8% operating margin in the third quarter, and capital spending of $0.2 million in nine months, so nearly all operating profit becomes cash.
- **A cash offer.** $16.25 is a hard number from a controlling holder with a signed agreement, a recommending special committee, and a bridge loan facility, and it came after the price was raised from $11.25.
- **Cheap on every measure I use.** 6.7 times trailing earnings, 1.4 times operating profit and a 15% owner cash flow yield.
- **Direct sales shift.** Direct-to-consumer reached 58.0% of social casino revenue, and cost of revenue fell to 23.5% of revenue from 29.1%.

## What could go wrong?

- **The offer fails.** Reaching 95% needs about 85% of the public ADSs. If the committee-approved price is rejected, the stock could return to the $13 level or lower.
- **The minority does not share in standalone value.** My Base is +54% above the offer, but a 67% owner can set the price, and a second step follows at the same price.
- **Shrinking core.** Social casino revenue fell 3.5% in the quarter and management calls the market one in secular decline. Revenue per payer fell from $272 to $216.
- **Earnings quality below the line.** Finance costs of $10.0 million, mainly currency losses, and tax up 31% took profit down 44% while operating profit rose.
- **Acquisitions.** A $61.6 million purchase of WHOW in 2025 and an $8 million write-off at SuprNation show capital allocation is not all upside.
- **Information.** I could not read the fairness opinion range or the full offer to purchase, and there are no estimates or guidance, so the standalone path rests on my assumptions.

## Management execution

Management has done what a shrinking business should do: raised the operating margin to 39.8% from 36.5% in a year, moved more than half of social casino revenue to direct sales, kept capital spending near zero and kept net cash above $500 million. On the deal, the special committee negotiated a price 44% higher than the first proposal. Against that, the company is controlled by the buyer, two acquisitions have had mixed results, the cash has grown unused apart from a $61.6 million acquisition, and the parent has lent the company money. Management credibility is **12/20**: strong on margins and conversion, limited by the conflict of interest and the capital allocation record.
