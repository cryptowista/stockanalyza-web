Bausch Health guides its parent company, everything except the Bausch + Lomb eye-care business, to $1.40 to $1.475 billion of adjusted operating cash flow in 2026. The whole company, 373.99 million shares at $6.04, is worth $2.26 billion on the stock market. On my normalized estimate of $1.3 billion of parent cash, that is a 54.5% yield, the kind of number a screener flags in green. Then read the debt note. The parent owes $15.1 billion of principal, I estimate its cash interest at $1.35 billion a year, and a drug that is about 22% of consolidated revenue is about to be repriced by Medicare.

At the October 9 close, the shares trade 36.9% below my triangulated Base value of $9.57. The Bear value is $0.00 and the Bull value is $29.23, and a second method puts the Bull case as high as $49.16, so the upper end is disputed. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 70%. The framework caps a practical requirement at 60% and prefers a watchlist verdict to a deceptively precise price above that, so no maximum buy price is issued. The question is not whether 2026 is a good year. It is how much of that cash is still there after 2028.

Valuation route: healthcare. Subtype: mature specialty pharmaceuticals and aesthetic devices with a controlling listed eye-care investment, not a pre-revenue drug developer. Economic classification: Standard (3/12). Primary method: sum of the parts, with the parent valued at a multiple of normalized EBITDA after stock compensation and the Bausch + Lomb stake added at a marked market value. Secondary method: a patent cliff free cash flow model of the parent, ten years plus a terminal value. Values are present fair value estimates, not 12 month price targets.

What Bausch Health is

The parent comprises four businesses: Salix, the gastrointestinal drug franchise built around Xifaxan; International, which sells pharmaceuticals and consumer products outside the US; Diversified, a portfolio of mature brands; and Solta, aesthetic devices. Bausch + Lomb (BLCO), the eye-care company, is listed separately. Bausch Health holds 310,449,643 BLCO shares, about 87% of the total, and consolidates it in its reported accounts. BLCO still has its own equity holders, $5,128 million of debt and $367 million of cash, so I do not treat BLCO's cash flow as Bausch Health's. The parent is valued on its own, and the BLCO stake is added at its quoted price after a haircut for the cost of turning it into cash.

Xifaxan is the concentration. The company's filings put it at roughly 85% of Salix sales. On Salix first half revenue of $1,397 million, that indicates about $1,187 million, or 22.1% of consolidated revenue. Annualized, it is about $2.4 billion, roughly 44% of the parent's guided revenue. The 85% mix is approximate and the product sales are not reported exactly, so treat these as orders of magnitude. Three dates define the risk:

  • January 2027. Under Medicare price negotiation, the maximum fair price of Xifaxan is $1,000 per 30-day supply, against a 2024 list price of $2,696 per 30-day supply, a 63% reduction in list price. That is not a 63% cut in company net sales. Actual payer mix, rebates and net revenue per patient are not disclosed in enough detail for me to calculate the real hit.
  • January 2028. A settlement with Actavis (Teva) permits specified generic options, with an earlier entry if another qualifying generic launches first. Authorized generic economics could keep some cash with the company, but the amount is not public.
  • October 2029. The injunction on the first Norwich application, upheld by the Federal Circuit in April 2024, blocks FDA approval of that application until October 2029. It is not exclusivity against every generic. According to the second quarter 10-Q, other proceedings, including Norwich II and an Amneal case, are unresolved.

The second quarter looks strong on the headline lines. Net income attributable to Bausch Health was $258 million, against a first half loss of $1,165 million. The gap is a single non-cash item: a $1,426 million goodwill impairment recorded in the first quarter. It is not a cash expense, but it shows that asset values were marked down. Goodwill fell from $11,271 million to $9,793 million.

Why cash after all interest is the metric

Management guides to EBITDA, and EBITDA is the wrong place to stop for a company with this much debt. The right question is how much cash reaches the common shares after every contractual cost. Two details matter. First, part of the interest on older notes is charged against recorded debt premiums and appears in financing cash flows, not operating cash flow, so conventional free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital spending) flatters the result. Second, the company adds back stock compensation, which is a real cost to owners.

For the first half of 2026, the 10-Q describes $66 million of premium reduction on older secured notes and $47 million on the 2032 notes, $113 million in total. The earnings reconciliation subtracts $100 million. I have not been able to reconcile the $13 million gap, and I carry both numbers as a range.

For the enterprise value methods I start from EBITDA, not cash. The guided parent adjusted EBITDA midpoint is $3,062.5 million. I deduct $150 million of recurring items the company adds back, giving $2,912.5 million. For scale, consolidated first half add-backs other than impairments and stock pay were about $85 million (restructuring and transformation $33 million, acquisition costs $22 million, litigation $16 million, other $14 million and smaller items), which is $170 million annualized and includes Bausch + Lomb.

Then comes stock compensation. Consolidated share-based compensation was $106 million in the first half, of which Bausch + Lomb recorded $72 million, so about $34 million, or $68 million a year, belongs to the parent. That is my estimate of the parent's run rate and it is deducted from EBITDA: normalized EBITDA after SBC is $2,844.5 million. Bausch + Lomb's stock pay is already inside the quoted price of its shares, so charging it again to the parent would count it twice.

Shares, dilution and the debt

Add 12.716 million time-based restricted stock units and 4.713 million performance units at target and you reach 391.4 million, before options. The remaining 4.796 million options need exercise-price-specific treatment. Counting every option and the maximum 9.426 million performance shares would give 400.9 million, which is a ceiling, not an expected count. I use 395 million diluted shares in every main scenario, a conservative allowance and not a filed count. Secondary valuation: patent cliff cash flow model of the parent

The second method forecasts the parent's cash year by year and discounts it, so it tests durability and not another multiple. It uses unlevered free cash flow (FCFF): cash after operating costs, taxes, reinvestment and working capital, before interest. For 2027 the Base bridge is $3,062.5 million of EBITDA, minus $150 million of recurring costs, $250 million of cash taxes, $100 million of total reinvestment and $162.5 million of working capital and other items, which gives $2,400 million. These allowances are mine and are not a management breakdown. I then deduct the $68 million of parent stock pay, so 2027 Base FCFF is $2,332 million.

The valuation date is October 11, so the rest of 2026 sits 0.222 years out and each later year one year further. History and growth diagnostics

A synchronized multi-year history of the parent's EV to EBITDA, with a consistent split of Bausch + Lomb, debt premiums and minorities, is not available. The old Valeant perimeter and the post-IPO Bausch Health perimeter cannot be compared, so I do not invent a historical mean or a peer premium. The historical layer gets no weight.

A forward price to earnings to growth (PEG) ratio is not available either. I have no same-date positive adjusted EPS forecast and growth rate, and GAAP earnings distorted by an impairment cannot be divided by adjusted growth. Cheap pre-cliff adjusted earnings can also give a misleadingly low PEG even when EPS is positive. The patent cliff distortion flag is high, the accounting basis mismatch flag is high if the two are mixed, and the PEG gives no growth premium and sets no value.

The cash history is short. From the annual report, reported operating cash flow minus capital spending minus premium interest charged outside operating cash flow was $535 million in 2023, $965 million in 2024 and $727 million in 2025. That is a 16.6% a year increase over two years, but a 24.7% fall in the last year, so the series is not steady. It also includes 100% of Bausch + Lomb's cash and excludes acquisitions and contingent payments. Per year-end common share the same measure is $1.465, $2.624 and $1.962, and the share count grew 0.72% a year. A normalized three to five year free cash flow growth rate and a properly diluted per-share series are not available.

For the twelve months to June 2026, operating cash flow of $1,800 million, minus $369 million of capital spending and $212 to $225 million of premium interest, leaves $1,206 to $1,219 million, 53.4% to 54.0% of the basic market value of $2,258.9 million. That is a consolidated diagnostic yield, not an owner yield. My forward parent figure is $1,300 million over $2,386 million of market value on 395 million shares, or 54.5%, and I rate its reliability as low.

The yield does not last. Using the Base FCFF before stock pay and a constant cash interest allowance of $1,350 million a year, which is my estimate, with no tax shield credit, levered cash is about $1,050 million in 2027 and $50 million in 2030. That is $2.66 a share falling to $0.13, a 63.8% a year decline, or a 2.1% forward yield on the current price. Stock pay is not deducted here because this test is about cash. It is a stress proxy, not an equity model and not a dividend forecast.

How the buy price is set

A Confidence score of 10/25 is grade D, which starts the MOS at 50%. I add 5 points for financial leverage, because the equity is a thin residual behind the creditors, 10 for binary legal and regulatory exposure beyond the scheduled patent erosion, and 5 because the two methods disagree by more than 30% in the Bull case. Concentration and opacity add nothing extra, since they are already in the cliff assumptions and the Confidence score. The uncapped requirement is 70%, above the 60% practical maximum.

Above that ceiling, the framework prefers a no-buy or watchlist verdict to a precise-looking price. No maximum buy price and no buy zone are issued. For orientation only, 70% below $9.57 is $2.87, and a 50% to 60% requirement, which would need the Confidence grade to improve to C and the overlapping risks to shrink, would give $3.83 to $4.79 on an unchanged Base. Neither is a price alert. At $6.04, the 36.9% discount to Base is not enough even for the lower, hypothetical range. Reactivation needs a funded maturity plan, an updated cash and share bridge and a better quantified post-cliff cash flow.

Dividend and capital return

Bausch Health is not an income stock. Neither valuation assumes a common dividend, and cash goes to debt service first. A subsidiary's value is not the parent's distributable cash. A dividend from Bausch + Lomb would be counted once and only after its own debt and capital needs, and I assume none. No buyback is credited.

Reasons to own BHC

  • The cash is real in 2026. Guided parent adjusted operating cash flow is $1.40 to $1.475 billion, and my normalized estimate of $1.3 billion is 54.5% of the market value.
  • A separately quoted asset. The 310.4 million Bausch + Lomb shares are worth $5.37 billion at $17.30, about 2.4 times Bausch Health's whole market capitalization.
  • The price is low against the operations. It implies 3.97x normalized EBITDA after stock pay, below my 4.5x Base multiple.
  • Diversification beyond Xifaxan. International, Diversified and the Solta device business provide cash that does not depend on one drug.
  • Time was bought. The 2025 refinancing moved some maturities out, and only $673 million of parent principal falls due in 2027.

The Bull case of $29.23 includes none of the pipeline. It is what happens if erosion is slower, the stake can be monetized and financing stays open. An investor needs to accept a real chance of permanent loss, and to explain why their view of post-cliff cash is better supported than the cautious one.

What could go wrong?

  • Thesis breaker: earlier or deeper Xifaxan erosion and no way to refinance or monetize assets without harming common equity. A 40% cut to Base cash flow removes all the equity in the cash flow model.
  • Reimbursement. The $1,000 maximum fair price starts in January 2027. The net effect on company revenue cannot be measured from the list price reduction alone.
  • Patents and generics. The Norwich ruling covers one application. The 2028 settlement date and the unresolved proceedings remain central to the cash flow curve.
  • Leverage and refinancing. $7,960 million of parent principal falls due from 2028 to 2030. Asset value does not guarantee cash in the right legal entity at the right time.
  • The Bausch + Lomb stake. 68.3% of it was pledged under the 2025 financing. Minority holders, tax structure, its own leverage and its share price all limit what the stake is worth to the parent.
  • Replacement. RED-C failed twice. Larsucosterol is investigational and still costs money.
  • Accounting. Conventional free cash flow misses interest charged against debt premiums, and the $13 million difference between the two H1 figures is unresolved.
  • Dilution and legacy claims. A distressed equity raise could fund survival while harming existing holders, and the $200 million reserve is not a verified worst case.

I assign no probability to any of these, and the price does not prove they are all discounted.

Management execution

The record is mixed. Management raised 2026 guidance, parent operations improved, and the refinancing program bought time. Against that, the 2025 debt exchanges are financing, not repayment of operating debt, the new debt is more expensive, the separation of Bausch + Lomb is unfinished, and a $1,426 million goodwill impairment landed in the first half. The failed RED-C program reduces confidence in automatic pipeline replacement, though it does not suggest anything was concealed. I assert no fraud, covenant breach or imminent insolvency. Assessment: 5/10, mixed. The next test is the third quarter report on October 28, 2026, which should show parent cash and debt, cash after all interest and any funding plan for 2028.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Post-cliff cash cannot fund or refinance the parent's obligations on terms that leave common equity something. A 20% cut to Base cash flow leaves $3.70, and a 40% cut leaves nothing.
  2. The Bausch + Lomb stake cannot be turned into value for Bausch Health shareholders, because of the pledge, taxes, minority holders or creditors capturing the proceeds. A haircut of 20% instead of 10% takes the Base to $8.21 and a BLCO price of $12 to $5.82.
  3. On the upside, a quantified Xifaxan bridge showing slower net erosion, together with a funded 2028 to 2030 maturity plan, would move the stock toward Undervalued and require a new valuation. One good quarter or one favorable court ruling does not do that alone.

Bausch Health has a good 2026 and a harder 2028.
