Restaurant Brands International reported a third quarter in which net income from continuing operations rose 60% and adjusted earnings per share rose 7%. The gap between the two figures is a $133 million income tax benefit from internal reorganizations, not a change in what the restaurants earn. The business itself grew steadily: system-wide sales rose 6.2% and adjusted operating income rose 5.0% in the quarter. At $73.01, the shares already pay for that pace for a long time.

At $73.01 on October 29, 2026, the shares trade 24.2% above my Base value of $58.77, so the Base is 19.5% below the price. The Bear value of $41.69 is 42.9% below the price and the Bull value of $74.50 is 2.0% above it. I assign no probabilities to the cases. The required margin of safety of 30% gives a buy range of $38.83 to $44.60 and a conservative limit of $38.83. For existing holders this is a hold, and the 3.6% dividend is a reason to wait. For new money, it is a watchlist name.

Valuation route: Franchised restaurant brands with a leveraged balance sheet, valued on free cash flow. Economic classification: quality franchise with moderate growth and high leverage. Primary method: ten years of free cash flow to shareholders per share, with stock pay treated as a cash cost, and a terminal value. Cross-check: 14 times adjusted EBITDA after stock pay, less net debt. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

What Restaurant Brands is

Restaurant Brands owns five quick-service brands and licenses almost all of the restaurants to franchisees: Tim Hortons, Burger King, Popeyes, Firehouse Subs and a large international business. At September 30, 2026 the system had 33,401 restaurants. The company collects royalties on franchisee sales, rent from some properties and supply chain income, and it carries little of the cost of running a restaurant.

Tim Hortons and the international segment are the main earners. In the first nine months of 2026, Tim Hortons produced $833 million of adjusted operating income and international $596 million, together about 70% of the total of $2,061 million. Burger King contributed $388 million, Popeyes $177 million and Firehouse Subs $47 million.

The company also owns a small restaurant holdings segment, which holds restaurants bought from the Carrols franchisee. Management plans to sell most of them to new franchisees and close the segment. Burger King China was moved into a joint venture with CPE in January 2026, and Restaurant Brands kept a 17% equity interest.

What the earnings are made of

  • Adjusted earnings per share is the figure that matters. It is stated on a fully exchanged basis, so it treats the Partnership exchangeable units as common shares. Adjusted net income of $505 million over 457 million diluted shares gives $1.10.
  • Reported net income is not a guide. Net income from continuing operations was $704 million in the quarter, but $133 million of it was a discrete tax benefit. The adjusted tax rate was about 17.4%.
  • Stock pay is a real cost. Adjusted EBITDA adds back about $34 million of share-based compensation a quarter. I deduct about $136 million a year in my cash flow model and in the earnings multiple cross-check.
  • Free cash flow is solid. Operating cash flow was $1,308 million over nine months, capital spending $181 million, and free cash flow $1,127 million, up from $996 million.

Over the last twelve months, my free cash flow is $1,580 million, less about $50 million of franchisee inducements, which I estimate from the nine-month total of $38 million, and $136 million of stock pay. That gives about $1,394 million, or $3.05 a share on 457 million diluted shares.

Primary valuation: free cash flow per share

  • Base: free cash flow of $3.05 a share growing 7.0%, 7.0%, 6.5%, 6.0%, 5.5%, 5.0%, 4.5%, 4.0%, 3.5% and 3.0% over ten years. That is $3.26 in year one and $4.16 in year five, a five-year growth rate of 6.4% a year. Cost of equity 9.5%, terminal growth 2.5%.
  • Result: $55.47 a share. The terminal value is 53.9% of it, over a ten-year horizon.

The growth path is below management's long-term algorithm of 8% organic operating income growth. I use less because interest expense does not fall as fast as operating income grows, net restaurant growth is 3.0% against the 5% target, and Popeyes is shrinking. The 9.5% cost of equity reflects a stable franchise with 4.0 times leverage. A terminal share of 54% is below the 75% attention level for a ten-year horizon.

Secondary valuation: EBITDA multiple

Adjusted EBITDA of $3,117 million less $136 million of stock pay is $2,981 million. At 14 times, the enterprise value is $41.7 billion, less net debt of $12.6 billion, which leaves $29.1 billion, or $63.72 a share. The market currently pays 14.8 times the unadjusted figure.

I use 14 times and not a higher multiple because growth in operating income is 5% to 7% a year, not 8%, and because 4.0 times leverage means that a small change in the multiple moves the equity value by a larger percentage. Each turn of the multiple is worth about $6.5 a share. The range of 11.5 times in the Bear and 16.5 times in the Bull shows how much of the answer the multiple carries.

The two methods share the same operating income growth and the same cash costs. They differ in the cost of equity, which the first method uses and the second does not. Their agreement is therefore weaker evidence than it looks, and I give it no extra Confidence.

Historical cross-check and growth diagnostic

Adjusted earnings per share rose from $3.34 in 2024 to $3.69 in 2025, up 10.7%, and the first nine months of 2026 are up 11.0% from the same period of 2025. Growth is structural for the royalty business and international expansion, and acquisition-affected for Burger King, where the restaurants bought from Carrols changed the base. I did not collect a long history of the share price multiple, so the historical component of the valuation score is neutral.

Part of the recent growth in earnings per share came from lower interest expense: $372 million in nine months against $391 million a year earlier, and from the deconsolidation of Burger King China. That support will fade as the debt falls more slowly.

Valuation triangulation

  • Primary (free cash flow per share): $55.47, weight 60%.
  • Secondary (14 times EBITDA after stock pay): $63.72, weight 40%.
  • Divergence between the two: 13.8% of their midpoint, inside the 20% limit.
  • Weighted Base: $58.77. The unweighted range of $55.47 to $63.72 is the honest uncertainty, and $58.77 is a point inside it.

I weight the cash flow model higher because it shows growth, interest and the cost of equity separately. Both values are below the price, so the weights do not change the verdict.

Dividend and buybacks

The company declared $0.65 a share for the fourth quarter, payable January 5, 2027, and targets $2.60 for 2026, a yield of 3.56%. It paid $875 million of dividends and distributions in nine months and bought back $444 million of shares and units, a total of $1.3 billion returned. The $2.60 dividend takes about 85% of my free cash flow of $3.05 a share, so it is covered, but it leaves little room for faster debt repayment. Buybacks of $275 million in the third quarter are about 0.8% of the market value in a single quarter.

Reasons to own QSR

  • An asset-light royalty model with $1,127 million of free cash flow in nine months, up 13%.
  • System-wide sales growth of 6.2% and net restaurant growth of 3.0%, led by the international segment at 9.0%.
  • Burger King comparable sales of 8.3% show that the turnaround is working, with $198 million of the planned $550 million remodel spending already funded.
  • A 3.56% dividend, and leverage down from 4.4 times to 4.0 times in a year.

What could go wrong?

  • Tim Hortons, the largest earner, has comparable sales of 1.5% and benefited from tariff refunds that will not recur.
  • Popeyes comparable sales are falling, with a decline of 4.5% over nine months.
  • Net debt of $12.6 billion is a heavy load. A rise of one point in the cost of refinancing costs about $0.14 billion of interest a year on the whole amount, and the cost of equity rises with it.
  • Franchisees carry the capital costs of remodels and new units, so weak franchisee profits can slow growth.
  • Reported and adjusted figures differ, and the adjustments change from year to year.

Management execution

  • Organic operating income growth is 7.8% over nine months and management says it is on track for 8% for the year.
  • Leverage fell to 4.0 times and the dividend was raised to $2.60 for 2026.
  • The Carrols restaurants and the start-ups in China and Brazil are being sold or closed out, a simplification of the portfolio.
  • I give a neutral view on the Popeyes decline, which management has not yet reversed.

What I would watch from here

Green

  • Tim Hortons comparable sales above 3% without one-time help.
  • Net restaurant growth moves toward 4% and 5%.
  • Net leverage falls below 3.8 times without a cut in the dividend.

Yellow

  • Burger King comparable sales fall back below 4%.
  • Popeyes comparable sales stay negative into 2027.
  • The gap between adjusted and reported earnings widens again.

Red

  • Organic operating income growth falls below 5% for two quarters.
  • Refinancing at rates well above the current coupons.
  • Franchisee distress or closures at Burger King in the United States.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Tim Hortons turns from slow growth into decline, which removes the largest source of profit.
  2. The Burger King recovery fades once the remodel spending ends, and comparable sales fall back to zero.
  3. The cost of equity rises to 10.5% or the multiple falls to 11.5 times, either of which cuts the value to the $41 to $48 range.

The weakest point of the model is the cost of equity together with the multiple, both of which are judgments. The strongest bull argument is that Burger King's recovery and international growth let operating income grow at the 8% target for years, and the market is right to pay for it. The strongest bear argument is that a 4.0 times leveraged franchisor with a shrinking Popeyes and a slow Tim Hortons deserves a lower multiple than the market pays.

Final verdict: hold at $73.01, do not add

Restaurant Brands is a high-quality royalty business with a rising dividend and falling leverage. At $73.01, the market pays for growth that I can only partly underwrite, and there is no margin of safety.

Verdict: HOLD at $73.01, do not add. Weighted Base $58.77, 19.5% below the price, cross-check $63.72, Confidence B (18/25). Required margin of safety 30%, buy range $38.83 to $44.60, conservative buy limit $38.83.

For an existing holder, the original cost of the shares is irrelevant, and nothing here says to sell: the price is close to my Bull value, the dividend is covered and the business is improving. A review is warranted if the price rises well above the Bull value of $74.50 without a change in fundamentals, or if Tim Hortons turns negative. New money waits for a price at or below $44.60.
