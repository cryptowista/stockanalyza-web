Seven weeks ago this site said hold MITT at $6.31 and buy at $5.25 or less. The stock didn't stop at $5.25. It closed at $4.45 on November 6, two days after third quarter results, and at $4.56 today. Book value fell 7.3% in the quarter. The price fell 28% in the same seven weeks. On the old numbers this would be a buy. On the new numbers it isn't, and the reason is worth more than the headline discount.

My Base value is now $6.84, 49.9% above the price. The Bear value is $3.59 and the Bull value is $9.08. I assign no probabilities to the cases. The verdict is Hold: the price is below my Base by a wide margin, but this is a leveraged, externally managed mortgage REIT with a dividend it did not earn last quarter, a merger vote on December 2 and no book value update since September 30. That earns a 42.5% margin of safety, which puts the buy price at $3.93.

Valuation route: a residential mortgage credit REIT that buys and securitizes non-agency and home equity loans, valued on the earnings its common equity can sustain after preferred dividends and all running costs, divided by a cost of equity, on book value pro forma for the Cherry Hill merger. The cross-check is a price to book multiple taken from MITT's own trading history and listed peers. Values are present fair-value estimates in US dollars, not 12-month price targets.

What MITT is

TPG Mortgage Investment Trust, known as AG Mortgage Investment Trust until December 2025, buys residential mortgages that the government agencies do not guarantee, packages them into bonds and keeps the riskiest slices. Since 2021 it has bought more than $12 billion of loans and done 38 securitizations. The loans on its books are largely fixed rate (93%) with a weighted FICO score of 751 and a current loan to value of 61%; 1.4% are 90 or more days late. It also owns 66% of Arc Home, a mortgage originator, and carries five problem commercial loans inherited with Western Asset Mortgage Capital.

The growth area is home equity: second mortgages and credit lines taken by owners who don't want to give up a 3% first mortgage. Home equity was 37% of MITT's equity at September 30. Management makes a good case for it. About half of all US mortgages carry rates below 4%, so every rise in rates makes a cash-out refinance less attractive and a second lien more so. The catch is that the product has only about three years of performance data behind it, as management itself said on the call.

MITT is externally managed by AG REIT Management, a TPG affiliate, which earned a $2.3 million fee in the third quarter.

Which numbers to trust

For a mortgage REIT, corporate free cash flow says almost nothing: loan purchases, securitizations and repayments swamp it. The numbers that matter are book value per common share, the earnings the common equity can keep producing, and whether those earnings show up as book value plus dividends over time.

MITT reports earnings available for distribution, or EAD. It excludes all realized and unrealized gains and losses and "transaction related expenses". Some of those expenses are one-off, like the $2.0 million spent on the Cherry Hill deal in the third quarter. Others are not: $2.1 million was spent on underwriting, legal and diligence fees to securitize and buy loans, which is what the business does every quarter. Over the first three quarters of 2026 these costs averaged about $1.0 million a quarter. I deduct $4.0 million a year from EAD for them, plus $1.5 million a year for credit losses and realized drift that EAD never sees.

The test over a longer period is mixed. From the end of 2023 to September 30, 2026, MITT reported $2.33 a share of EAD. Book value plus dividends grew by $1.39 a share (my arithmetic, from about $10.20 to $9.27 plus $2.32 of dividends), about 4.7% a year. Through 2025 economic returns ran ahead of EAD; the third quarter of 2026 took all of that back and more.

Preferred stock, debt and the merger

The common shares are the junior layer of a crowded capital structure. Ahead of them sit $228.0 million of preferred stock, $99.5 million of 9.5% senior notes due February 15, 2029, and $1.1 billion of recourse borrowing. Common book value was $294.8 million on September 30, so the preferred stock alone is 77% of it. Preferred dividends took $5.2 million in the third quarter, $0.16 a share, more than the management fee and all corporate expenses together.

The securitized debt, $6.1 billion, has no recourse to MITT, which is why the 13.7x GAAP leverage overstates the risk. The recourse part is what can force a sale. $711.6 million is repo on retained bonds at a 75% advance rate, where a fall in bond prices triggers a margin call.

MITT came through September without selling, which is evidence in its favour. It is not a large buffer. The 2029 notes are callable at par and are the next refinancing; at 9.5% they cost $9.5 million a year.

On December 2 MITT and Cherry Hill shareholders vote on the merger. Each Cherry Hill share gets 0.3063 MITT shares, $0.41 in cash from MITT and $0.52 from MITT's manager. MITT issues about 11.6 million new shares, 36% more than the 31.8 million it has, and pays about $15.8 million in cash. It also takes on $109.6 million of Cherry Hill preferred stock. In return it gets Cherry Hill's common equity, $2.73 a share on September 30, about $105 million.

Primary valuation: what the common equity can earn

A mortgage REIT that pays out nearly all it earns is worth what its common equity can earn year after year, divided by the return investors require. If that return on equity is below the cost of equity, the shares are worth less than book value, and that is my Base. I build the earnings from the third quarter run rate and add or subtract the pieces that are changing.

Management guides to $0.15 to $0.20 a share of extra annual EAD in 2027 from selling the commercial loans and reinvesting about $28 million; I count about half of that, because commercial property sales take time and MITT itself calls them "an adventure". I take only the low end of the merger savings and give Cherry Hill's equity a 9% return, well below the 20% its own EAD suggests, because that EAD has not turned into book value. The result is $0.94 a share, a 10.9% return on $8.64 of pro forma book value.

I use a 13% cost of equity (my estimate): a small, externally managed REIT with preferred stock and recourse debt ahead of the common shares, which trades about 400,000 shares a day. The first two years earn 85% and 98% of the steady level while the savings and reinvestment arrive, and the value after year three is the steady earnings divided by 13%. That ending value is 71% of the total over a three-year horizon, which is normal for a business valued on steady earnings. The primary Base value is $7.08.

Secondary valuation: price to book

The cross-check ignores my earnings estimate and asks what multiple of book value the market normally pays for MITT and its peers. From January to September 2026 MITT traded between about 0.63 and 0.88 times its latest book value: 0.88 at the January high of $9.27 against $10.48 of book, 0.79 in July and 0.63 at the September analysis. Angel Oak Mortgage REIT, the closest listed peer in non-agency loans, trades at roughly 0.72 times its September GAAP book value; Ellington Financial, with a much longer record of protecting book value, trades around book. I use 0.75 times the $8.64 pro forma book value for the Base, which gives $6.48, 0.55 times for the Bear and 0.90 times for the Bull.

The two methods share the book value but not the earnings or the cost of equity. They agree within 9% in the Base, which says the earnings assumption is not out of line with how the market has valued this business when it was calm. It does not say the market is calm now.

How the buy price is set

The required margin of safety follows the confidence grade. Grade C starts at 42.5%, and I add nothing for leverage, the merger or the dividend, because those are already in the scenarios and the cost of equity. $6.84 times 0.575 is $3.93. On the earnings method alone it would be $4.07, on the book multiple alone $3.72. The price of $4.56 is above all three.

The verdict holds up to single changes. A 12% cost of equity lifts the buy price to $4.14, earnings $0.10 higher lift it to $4.19, and even a 35% margin of safety gives $4.44. For a buy at $4.56 my Base value would have to be $7.93, which needs steady earnings near $1.18 a share, close to my Bull: the merger savings at the top of the range and the commercial capital reinvested on management's schedule.

For an existing holder the original cost is irrelevant. The price sits between my Bear and Base values, which is a level to hold, not to add. I would revisit a sale only if the dividend is cut and book value keeps falling at the same time.

Dividend

The common dividend has been $0.24 a quarter since the first quarter of 2026, after four increases in eight quarters. EAD covered it 1.08 times in the first quarter, exactly in the second and 0.88 times in the third; for the first nine months EAD was $0.71 against $0.72 paid. My Base earnings of $0.94 cover the annual $0.96 about 0.98 times, and only if the merger savings arrive. MITT alone, on my numbers, earns $0.83. The fourth quarter dividend is usually declared in mid-December. A cut to $0.20 would not surprise me; a cut to $0.12 would mean the Bear case is arriving.

Reasons to own MITT

  • The price already assumes a lot goes wrong. At 0.49 times book value the market prices steady earnings of about $0.59 a share, close to my Bear.
  • The financing held in September. 85% of the debt is securitized with no recourse to MITT, warehouse lines don't mark to market, and recourse leverage was 2.0 times. MITT did not have to sell into the worst month of the year.
  • The loans are good. FICO of 751, current loan to value of 61% and 1.4% of loans 90 days late; home equity loans sit at a combined 64% loan to value with 0.6% late.
  • Home equity has a real tailwind. Higher rates make cash-out refinancing worse for owners with 3% mortgages and second liens better.
  • The manager is paying part of the merger. TPG contributes about $20 million of the cash going to Cherry Hill shareholders.

What could go wrong?

  • Another spread shock. A further 10% fall in the retained bonds would call for about $71 million of margin, close to all of MITT's liquidity.
  • The dividend is cut and book value keeps falling. Together they would move the stock toward my Bear value of $3.59.
  • Cherry Hill does not earn its keep. Its agency bonds and servicing rights add interest rate and prepayment risk to a credit business, and its book value lost 13.6% last quarter.
  • Home equity credit turns. Second liens lose first and most in a falling housing market, and the product has only a few years of data.
  • The capital structure. After the merger about $338 million of preferred stock will sit ahead of roughly $375 million of common equity, at fixed or floating rates between about 8% and 11%.

The option market adds little here: MITT options exist but trade thinly, and I could not get reliable current implied volatility or skew, so I make no option-based risk call.

Management execution

The record is better than the price. The Western Asset Mortgage Capital merger in 2023 worked, the company has run with low recourse leverage, it issued ten securitizations in 2025 and it came through September without forced sales. Against that, management forecast $0.95 of EAD for 2026 in the merger documents and needs $0.24 in the fourth quarter to get there, book value at September 30 was $0.68 below the $9.95 it projected for year end, and with the stock at half of book value the company is issuing shares for Cherry Hill instead of buying its own. The EAD definition also leaves out securitization costs that recur.

I score management credibility 12 out of 20: two points each for investment returns and balance sheet discipline, one point each for the other eight areas.

What I would watch from here

Green

  • Pro forma book value at or above $8.60 after the merger closes (MITT alone: $9.20 or more)
  • Quarterly EAD of $0.24 or more, with the dividend held at $0.24
  • Liquidity above $100 million and recourse leverage at 2.0 times or less
  • Two legacy commercial sales closed near their marks by the first quarter of 2027
  • Shares bought back while the stock trades below 0.7 times book

Yellow

  • Pro forma book value between $8.00 and $8.60
  • Quarterly EAD between $0.20 and $0.24 (third quarter: $0.21)
  • Liquidity between $70 million and $100 million (September 30: $84.7 million)
  • Recourse leverage between 2.0 and 2.5 times

Red

  • Pro forma book value below $8.00
  • Dividend cut below $0.20 a quarter, or EAD below $0.20 for two quarters
  • Liquidity below $70 million or recourse leverage above 2.5 times
  • Non-agency loans 90 days late above 3%
  • Commercial loans sold more than 15% below their September marks

The fourth quarter report, likely in February 2027, is the next real test. It should show the first pro forma book value, EAD of at least $0.22 a share without help from one-off gains, at least one hotel loan sale closed, and the first evidence of the merger savings. Before that come the merger votes on December 2 and the dividend declaration in mid-December.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. A second spread shock that forces sales to meet margin calls, which turns marks into realized losses.
  2. A dividend cut combined with further book value losses, which leads toward the Bear.
  3. Merger savings and commercial redeployment both arriving on management's schedule, which leads toward the Bull and would put a buy within reach.

The weakest point of the model is the fourth quarter so far: MITT gave no update, and if non-agency spreads did not recover in October, the Base book value is too high. Peer comments point both ways; Angel Oak spoke of a couple of points of recovery by November 3, while MITT's price fell 21% in the two days after its report. My conservative choices are deducting securitization costs from EAD and a low return on Cherry Hill's equity; my generous one is a 13% cost of equity for a stock the market prices at a 21% yield. The strongest bull argument is that even the Bear case roughly breaks even from here. Final verdict: hold at $4.56

In September I bought the argument that MITT's book value would hold and the dividend would grow into coverage. Book value moved 7.3%, the price 28%, and the market is now charging a 21% yield for the risk that the dividend goes next. My Base says it is overdone; my margin of safety says not yet by enough.

Verdict: HOLD at $4.56, watchlist for new money. Base $6.84, 49.9% above the price, Bear $3.59, Bull $9.08, Confidence C (16.25/25). Required margin of safety 42.5%, maximum buy price $3.93, range $3.72 to $4.07 under the two methods.

New money waits for $3.93 or less, or for a fourth quarter that shows the dividend covered and pro forma book value at or above $8.60, which would lift the confidence grade. The dividend is the argument for owning MITT at $4.56. The book value, so far, is the argument for waiting.
