Charter just became a much bigger company. In August it closed its all-stock acquisition of Liberty Broadband and then, weeks later, completed the $34.5 billion purchase of Cox Communications, adding roughly 6 million customers and pushing the combined footprint past 45 states. It also became a much more indebted one. Small changes in enterprise value, EBITDA or debt now translate into very large changes in what the common equity is worth, and that leverage is the whole story here.

At $112.91 on September 25, 2026, Charter trades 51.7% below my triangulated Base value of $234, an upside of 107.2%. The Bear value of $111 sits essentially at the current price, meaning the market is already pricing in a fairly grim outcome. That should make this an easy buy, except the framework's required margin of safety for a leveraged, integrating company like this is 55%, which puts the maximum buy price at about $105. Charter is close, but not quite there.

What Charter is

Charter sells broadband internet, cable video, mobile phone service and business connectivity under the Spectrum brand. The Cox deal is the headline event: it adds scale, an estimated $500 million of annual cost synergies, and access to markets Charter didn't previously serve, in exchange for roughly $12 billion of assumed debt and leases, about 33.6 million exchangeable common units, and convertible preferred units equivalent to another 12.6 million shares issued to Cox's former owner. The Liberty Broadband transaction, completed separately, retired about 38.6 million Charter shares and issued about 33.9 million new ones, a net reduction of 4.7 million.

The character of the business is changing in a way the framework treats seriously. This is no longer simply "buy depressed cable equity and wait for multiple expansion." The thesis now depends on retaining Cox's customers through the Spectrum rebrand, delivering the promised synergies, and using the combined cash flow to bring leverage down, all while the core broadband business keeps losing customers.

Which numbers matter for a leveraged cable operator

Headline Adjusted EBITDA is not enough here. Charter's 2025 standalone Adjusted EBITDA was about $22.7 billion, but free cash flow was only about $5.0 billion once required network and customer capex, interest and taxes are subtracted. Any EV/EBITDA valuation has to net out the full debt load, not a headline leverage ratio, because a small change in the multiple or in debt produces an outsized swing in the equity value that's left over.

Combining Charter and Cox, normalized owner free cash flow is estimated at Bear $4.5 billion, Base $6.0 billion, Bull $7.5 billion. The Base case assumes partial synergy capture and gradual capex and interest normalization, not a broadband turnaround.

Shares, dilution and the post-close capital structure

The valuation uses 163.6 million fully diluted economic shares, combining pre-close Charter shares, the net Liberty reduction, and the Cox common and preferred-equivalent units. Charter has historically been an aggressive share repurchaser, but Cox is a major one-time dilution event that has to be judged against the economics it brings in, not treated as a continuation of the old buyback story. Base post-close net debt and senior claims are estimated at about $110 billion.

Historical cross-check

Public historical datasets place Charter's five-year average EV/EBITDA near 6.6x, above both the Bear and Base multiples used here. Cox changed the capital structure enough that history is only a loose cross-check, not an input; returning to 6.5x is not assumed in Base and would require broadband stabilization, delivered synergies and real deleveraging first.

What is the market already pricing in?

At $112.91 and 163.6 million diluted economic shares, equity value is about $18.5 billion. Add the Base $110 billion of net debt and senior claims and enterprise value works out to roughly $128.5 billion, or about 4.7x normalized $27.5 billion EBITDA. That's well below both the Bear (4.8x) and Base (5.3x) multiples used in this analysis, which means the market is pricing in prolonged broadband pressure, limited synergy delivery, or slow deleveraging, not a stabilization case.

Bear, base and bull scenarios

Bear: broadband erosion persists, integration proves costly, and leverage stays high; double pessimism is justified here because operating deterioration and financing risk reinforce each other. Base: broadband losses gradually stabilize, mobile and commercial revenue offset part of the decline, synergies emerge on schedule, and debt starts falling. Bull: convergence materially improves retention, synergies are fully delivered, and deleveraging drives the equity convexity that leverage always cuts both ways on.

Triangulated Bear/Base/Bull is $111 / $234 / $379. The framework does not assign explicit scenario probabilities for a company this soon after a transformative, still-integrating deal; the wide dispersion itself is the signal, reflected in the low 15/25 Confidence score and the 55% required margin of safety.

How the buy price is set

Confidence grade C starts the margin of safety at 40%. Ten points are added for the extreme leverage and another five for the character change and integration risk, for a total of 55%. Applied to the $234 Base value, that gives a maximum buy price of about $105. The preferred buy zone is $90 to $105; below roughly $90, the framework says to re-underwrite the fundamentals rather than assume it's simply cheaper, because a move that far through Bear territory may reflect genuinely new adverse information.

Dividend

Charter common stock does not pay a regular dividend, so this is not an income name. Newly issued preferred securities tied to the Cox transaction are separate instruments from the common-equity thesis discussed here.

Reasons to own CHTR

  • Depressed valuation. The current EV/EBITDA multiple sits well below Charter's own five-year average and below even this analysis's Bear case.
  • Equity convexity. If enterprise value holds while debt is paid down, the equity value rises faster than the underlying business improves.
  • Mobile momentum. 12.5 million mobile lines and 1.7 million net adds over twelve months support the broader convergence and retention strategy.
  • Cox scale and synergies. The deal was priced near Charter's own EV/EBITDA multiple and targets about $500 million of annual cost synergies.
  • High replacement cost. Fixed-line broadband infrastructure remains expensive for a new entrant to replicate at scale.

What could go wrong?

  • Structural broadband decline. If internet customer and revenue losses don't stabilize, this is the main thesis breaker.
  • Balance sheet risk. Roughly $110 billion of debt creates nonlinear downside and refinancing exposure if credit markets tighten.
  • Integration risk. Cox customer retention or synergy delivery could disappoint during the Spectrum rebrand.
  • Capital allocation risk. A return to aggressive buybacks before leverage comes down would work against the deleveraging thesis.

Management execution

Management has a long history of aggressive share reduction and consistent network investment, and priced Cox near Charter's own trading multiple with a credible synergy target. The flag is structural: leverage plus a history of buyback appetite magnifies both good and bad outcomes, and deleveraging should take priority until broadband trends actually stabilize. Also watch refinancing conditions and buyback pace: debt-funded or aggressive repurchases while leverage is still elevated would be a red flag on their own. Next major review point is the next combined-company earnings or filing, expected around late October 2026.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Structural broadband erosion that never stabilizes.
  2. Combined leverage fails to decline from current levels.
  3. Cox integration damages cash generation rather than adding to it.

Adversarial review

Primary values were independently recomputed from EBITDA times multiple, minus net debt, divided by 163.6 million diluted economic shares; secondary values from normalized FCF times P/FCF; and the triangulation at the stated 60/40 weights. All of it reconciled with what the source analysis reported.

The strongest counter-thesis is that cable broadband has entered secular decline as fiber and fixed wireless keep gaining share, and that Cox simply adds more leveraged legacy cable assets to a shrinking business, making the apparent cheapness an equity-stub illusion rather than a bargain. That is a credible risk, which is exactly why this analysis uses depressed multiples, a C Confidence grade and a 55% margin of safety rather than treating the discount as free money. Upside here requires stabilization and deleveraging, not a return to the cable growth story of a decade ago.

Final verdict: hold at $112.91, watchlist for new money

Charter is genuinely, deeply undervalued against its triangulated Base value, and the market is pricing in more distress than the numbers currently support. But cheap is not the same as safe when net debt runs to roughly $110 billion and a major acquisition is still mid-integration. The framework doesn't convert "cheap" into "buy" while leverage, broadband erosion and integration uncertainty all remain this elevated.

Verdict: HOLD at $112.91, WATCHLIST for new money. Triangulated Base value $234, upside about 107%, Confidence C (15/25). Required margin of safety 55%, maximum buy price about $105.

A move into the $90 to $105 buy zone without further thesis deterioration would satisfy the modeled entry point. The main thesis breaker is persistent combined-company broadband and EBITDA decline that prevents deleveraging.
