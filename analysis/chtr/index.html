What Charter is

Charter sells broadband internet, video, mobile phone service and business connectivity under the Spectrum brand. In 2026 it closed two deals that changed its structure: the all-stock acquisition of Liberty Broadband and the purchase of Cox Communications. The result is a larger operator with a more complicated capital structure.

  • The listed company owns Class A shares of Charter Holdings. Advance/Newhouse holds exchangeable units, and Cox's former owners hold common units and $6.0 billion of convertible preferred units paying 6.875%.
  • The preferred converts at $477.41 a share, far above today's price, so I treat it as a $6.0 billion claim ahead of the common, not as shares.
  • Per-unit values below divide by all common-equivalent units, 164.64 million, so the price is comparable with what each economic owner holds.

History and growth diagnostics

Valuation history. Public data sets put Charter's long-run EV/EBITDA around 6.6x. The Cox deal changed the capital structure enough that history is only a loose cross-check here. Today's 4.9x is below the Base multiple and below that history, but Base does not assume a return to 6.6x.

PEG (SM-19, diagnostic only). Reported earnings per share of $38.43 give a P/E of about 2.7 on a heavily leveraged equity with a changed share count, so a growth multiple built on it is not meaningful. Flag: PEG_NOT_MEANINGFUL. Growth type: ACQUISITION_AFFECTED.

Free cash flow growth quality (SM-20, diagnostic only). My Base owner free cash flow rises from $5.55 billion in 2026E to $7.58 billion in 2028E, a compound growth of 16.9% a year, or from $33.71 to $46.04 a unit with a constant unit count. That growth does not come from rising profits. EBITDA falls in the same period. It comes from capex dropping by $3.5 billion and from lower integration cost, so the flags are CAPEX_TIMING_DISTORTION and ACQUISITION_AFFECTED. Forward owner cash flow yield on the $17.0 billion market value is 32.6% for 2026E, 38.1% for 2027E and 44.5% for 2028E, which tells you how leveraged the equity is and does not describe a normal yield. Forecast reliability: LOW. Stress test: 2028E owner free cash flow 20% lower gives a primary value of $234, 40% lower gives $177. Neither stress takes the primary value below today's price.

The consequences are written into the rules: Confidence drops a grade and is capped at B before other penalties, and the margin of safety rises by 5 to 10 points. The divergence says one thing clearly: the answer depends on whether cash conversion improves as capex falls, and this analysis cannot prove that it will.

What is the market already pricing in?

Reverse valuation: at $103.38 the market value of $17.0 billion plus claims gives an enterprise value of $134.7 billion, or 4.9x 2026E adjusted EBITDA and 5.1x 2027E EBITDA after stock pay. Using the same 7x multiple as my Base, the price is consistent with 2028E owner free cash flow of only $2.57 billion. That is 34% of my Base figure of $7.58 billion. Put the other way, the price equals about 2.4x my Base 2028E owner cash flow. My Base case implies 6.3x 2027E EBITDA, against 4.9x today.

The market therefore expects either a sharp EBITDA decline, capex that stays near today's level, or trouble refinancing. On the primary method alone even my Bear case, with standalone EBITDA falling about 5% a year and capex above $11 billion, is worth $111, above the price. The Bear value of $66 is lower only because the EV/EBITDA method puts Bear equity below zero.

How the buy price is set

Confidence is D, which starts the margin of safety at 50%. Leverage, structural disruption, terminal value dependence and method divergence would add 30 more points, for 80%. The framework caps the required margin of safety at 60% and says that anything above that should be a no-buy or watchlist case. At 60% the maximum buy price is $239.14 x 40% = $95.65.

Dividend and capital return

Charter does not pay a common dividend. Repurchases were $838 million of shares in the second quarter, are paused through the third quarter and are expected to resume in the fourth. With claims at 4.3x EBITDA, buybacks before leverage falls would work against the deleveraging case. The Cox preferred units pay 6.875% on $6.0 billion, about $0.41 billion a year, which is paid before the common receives anything.

Reasons to own CHTR

  • Price against cash. The shares sell at 2.4x my Base 2028E owner cash flow and 4.9x EBITDA.
  • Capex is guided down. Management has said capex falls to $7.5 to $8.0 billion by 2028 to 2029, which is the engine of the Base case.
  • Mobile growth. 12.5 million lines and 406,000 net additions in the latest quarter support convergence and retention.
  • Scale and synergies. Cox adds customers and a cost synergy opportunity that I put at $0.8 billion by 2029 in Base.
  • Equity leverage works both ways. If enterprise value holds and debt is repaid, the common gains faster than the business improves.

What could go wrong?

  • Structural broadband decline. Internet customers fell by 172,000 and internet revenue fell 3.2% in the latest quarter. Fiber and fixed wireless keep taking share.
  • Capex does not fall. Each $1 billion of extra 2028E capex lowers the primary Base value by about $37 a unit at 7x.
  • Balance sheet. A $1.5 billion change in net debt moves the triangulated value by roughly $9 to $10 a unit, and refinancing a large stack in a weaker credit market raises interest cost.
  • Integration. Cox customer retention and the Spectrum rebrand are unproven, and a full combined quarter has not been reported.
  • Finance leadership. Charter announced a chief financial officer transition on August 31, 2026, and Reuters reported that the current CFO would step down on October 15 with an interim CFO named.

Management execution

Management has a record of aggressive buybacks and steady network investment, and it bought Cox at a multiple close to Charter's own. It has not given a leverage target or a post-close free cash flow figure, and the CFO is changing in the middle of the integration. Management credibility scores 9/20.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Capex does not fall toward the $7.5 to $8.0 billion guide, which removes the main source of Base cash flow growth.
  2. EBITDA erosion accelerates faster than the 2.5% a year in Base, so the cash flow falls before capex does.
  3. Leverage does not decline, or refinancing is done at much higher cost, because $117.7 billion of claims leave very little room for error.

Adversarial review

I recomputed every table from the inputs listed above: the owner cash flow path, the three valuation methods, the triangulation, the grids and the net debt sensitivity. The arithmetic reconciles. The strongest counter-thesis is that cable broadband is in permanent decline, that Cox simply adds leveraged legacy assets, and that the apparent cheapness is an artifact of a thin equity layer. That could be right, and it is why Base carries negative EBITDA growth and negative terminal growth, why the preferred is a claim ahead of the common, and why stock pay and integration cash are deducted.

Final verdict: hold at $103.38

Charter is cheap on every cash flow measure I can build, and the Bear case alone does not take the common to zero on the primary method. But cheap is not the same as a buy price. With Confidence D, a 57% disagreement between methods and $117.7 billion of claims ahead of the common, the framework asks for a 60% margin of safety, and the stock is 8.1% above the price that gives me.

VERDICT: HOLD (WATCHLIST) at $103.38; Triangulated Base $239; upside 131.3%; required MOS 60%; maximum buy price $95.65. The main thesis breaker is capex that stays near $13 billion while EBITDA keeps shrinking.
