On October 11 I called Charter a Hold at $103.38 and put the Base value at $239. The shares now cost $135.87, up 31%, and my Base is $217, down 9%. The third quarter report landed in between: broadband losses got worse, pro forma adjusted EBITDA fell 4.0%, and management added a $2 billion cost program. The price moved toward my old Base while my new Base moved away from it.

At the November 24, 2026 close of $135.87, the shares trade 37.5% below my triangulated Base value of $217, an upside of 60.0%. The Bear value is $59 and the Bull value is $405. That still reads like an easy buy, and it is not, for the same three reasons as in October. The equity is a thin slice of the enterprise, so small errors in EBITDA or debt move the value a long way. The two valuation methods land 53% apart. And broadband customers are still leaving. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 60%, so the maximum buy price is $86.98. The shares are 56.2% above that and would need to fall 36.0% to reach it. Would I buy at $135.87? No, and the price ladder below shows what each entry price would mean.

What Charter is

Charter sells broadband internet, video, mobile phone service and business connectivity under the Spectrum brand. In August 2026 it completed the Cox Communications deal and the all-stock acquisition of Liberty Broadband. The result is the largest cable operator in the United States, with a more complicated capital structure.

  • The listed company owns Class A shares of Charter Holdings. Advance/Newhouse holds exchangeable units, and Cox's former owners hold common units and $6.0 billion of convertible preferred units paying 6.875%.
  • The preferred converts at $477.41 a share, far above today's price, so I treat it as a $6.0 billion claim ahead of the common, not as shares.
  • Per-unit values below divide by all common-equivalent units, 175.1 million, so the price is comparable with what each economic owner holds.

Capex of $13.1 billion, cash interest of $5.8 billion, taxes, integration cash and stock pay take most of it before anything reaches the common. In 2026E my Base owner free cash flow is $6.01 billion, a fifth of EBITDA. Only the fourth quarter of 2026 ($1.26 billion after stock pay) enters the valuation, because the first nine months are already in the September 30 net debt. History and growth diagnostics

Valuation history. Public data sets put Charter's long-run EV/EBITDA around 6.6x. Today's 5.1x is below that, but the Cox deal changed the capital structure enough that history is only a loose cross-check, and Base does not assume a return to 6.6x.

PEG (diagnostic only). Trailing reported earnings of $9.66 a share include a $6.3 billion impairment and a $1.4 billion debt gain, so a growth multiple built on them is not meaningful. Flag: PEG_NOT_MEANINGFUL. Growth type: ACQUISITION_AFFECTED.

Free cash flow growth quality (diagnostic only). My Base owner free cash flow rises from $6.01 billion in 2026E to $7.12 billion in 2028E, a compound growth of 8.9% a year, with no change in unit count. That growth does not come from rising profits. EBITDA falls in the same period. It comes from capex dropping by $3.1 billion, so the flags are CAPEX_TIMING_DISTORTION and ACQUISITION_AFFECTED. Forward owner cash flow yield on the $23.8 billion market value is 25% for 2026E, 28% for 2027E and 30% for 2028E, which tells you how leveraged the equity is and does not describe a normal yield. Forecast reliability: LOW. Stress test: 2028E owner free cash flow 20% lower gives a primary value of $210, 40% lower gives $159. Neither stress takes the primary value below today's price.

The primary and secondary Base values differ by 53% of their midpoint, well above the 30% line where the framework flags a valuation divergence. The consequences are written into the rules: Confidence drops a grade and the margin of safety rises. I weight the primary method at 60% because it values the cash the common actually receives after interest, and the secondary at 40% because it reads the same business through a multiple that is only an assumption. The weights are a judgment, and the bias test at the end shows 50/50 and 70/30. The weighted value is a point inside a wide range, not a narrowing of it: both $261 and $152 remain defensible.

What is the market already pricing in?

Reverse valuation: at $135.87 the market value of $23.8 billion plus claims gives an enterprise value of $138.0 billion, or 5.1x 2026E adjusted EBITDA and 5.4x 2027E EBITDA after stock pay. Using the same 7x multiple as my Base, the price is consistent with 2028E owner free cash flow of only $3.62 billion. That is 51% of my Base figure of $7.12 billion. In October the price implied 34% of my Base figure, so the market now prices in more of my cash flow, while my own cash flow estimate came down.

One more way to see it: if the EV/EBITDA multiple stays at 5.13x, Base 2027E EBITDA of $26.37 billion gives an enterprise value of $135.2 billion at the end of 2027. If all $7.8 billion of owner free cash flow from Q4 2026 and 2027 repays debt, claims fall to $106.4 billion and the equity is worth $164.78 a unit. That is 21.3% above today's price, or 19.1% a year over about 14 months. Most of that return is debt paydown, not a rerating, and it is a return before any further EBITDA decline in Bear.

Dividend and capital return

Charter does not pay a common dividend. It repurchased $19 million of shares in the third quarter at an average of about $135, after pausing repurchases around the closings. With claims at 4.2x EBITDA, buybacks before leverage reaches 3.5x would work against the deleveraging case, and my model assumes none. The Cox preferred units pay 6.875% on $6.0 billion, about $0.41 billion a year, which is paid before the common receives anything.

Reasons to own CHTR

  • Price against cash. The market value is 3.3x my Base 2028E owner cash flow, and the enterprise is 5.1x EBITDA.
  • Capex is guided down. Capex falls from about $13.0 billion in 2026 to at or below $11 billion in 2027, the main engine of the Base case.
  • Mobile growth. 13.5 million lines, +508,000 pro forma in the quarter, support convergence and retention.
  • Long, fixed debt. Average debt life is 11.1 years at an average cost of 5.3%, so the balance sheet has no near-term wall, and leverage is guided below 3.9x at the end of 2026.
  • Equity leverage works both ways. If enterprise value holds and debt is repaid, the common gains faster than the business improves.

What could go wrong?

  • Structural broadband decline. Legacy Charter lost 156,000 internet customers against 109,000 a year earlier. Fiber and fixed wireless keep taking share.
  • EBITDA guidance keeps slipping. Management has cut its 2026 EBITDA outlook twice, and the third quarter was down 3.0% excluding transition costs.
  • Capex does not fall. Each $1 billion of extra 2028E capex lowers the primary Base value by about $36 a unit at 7x.
  • Balance sheet. Each $1 billion change in claims moves the triangulated value by about $2.28 a unit, and any refinancing above the 5.3% average cost of debt raises interest cost.
  • Integration and asset values. A $6.3 billion franchise impairment says the acquired and existing franchises are worth less on the books than a quarter ago, and Cox customer retention is still unproven.
  • Finance leadership. Charter announced a chief financial officer transition on August 31, 2026, in the middle of the integration.

Management execution

Prior estimate against actual, as the framework asks on every update: management has cut its 2026 EBITDA outlook twice and reported a 4.0% pro forma decline in the third quarter. It has now added a $2 billion cost program and a new operating model for sales, and promises "significant free cash flow growth over the next several years" without a number. The execution on capex is better: pro forma capex fell 11.2% in the quarter and 2027 is guided at or below $11 billion. Management credibility scores 8/20, down from 9/20 in October, because the EBITDA path was missed twice and the headline metric now excludes transition expenses.
