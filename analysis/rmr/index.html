RMR Group: an 11% dividend that is covered before interest but not after it, and the verdict is hold at $16.61

RMR, Nasdaq. Published November 4, 2026. Price as of the November 3, 2026 regular session close. Latest reported quarter: April to June 2026 (fiscal third quarter). Fiscal year results are expected around November 12, 2026.

Hold at $16.61

At $16.61 RMR trades 21% below my Base value of $21.02, but 37% above my maximum buy price of $12.08. About a third of the Base value is listed stakes, real estate and cash, and the market gives the fee business a low multiple for good reasons: one family controls it, four related REITs pay most of the fees, and one of them just went through bankruptcy. Confidence is C, so the required margin of safety is 42.5%. The shares traded near $16.79 on November 4, which does not change the verdict.

RMR calls its dividend "well covered", and on its own measure, distributable earnings of 48 cents a unit in the June quarter, it is. That measure is calculated before interest. RMR now owns four properties with $141 million of mortgages and has $25 million drawn on its credit line, and the June quarter's interest bill was $3.2 million. After it, the quarter earned 38 cents a unit, against a 45-cent dividend.

At $16.61 on the November 3, 2026 close, RMR trades 21.0% below my Base value of $21.02. The Bear value is $13.00, 21.7% below the price, and the Bull value is $28.33, 70.6% above it. I assign no probabilities to the cases. The verdict is Hold, with new money waiting for $12.08 or less.

Valuation route: an external manager of four listed REITs, a mortgage REIT and private real estate funds, valued as a sum of the parts: recurring fee earnings after tax, incentive fees, and listed stakes, owned real estate and cash at market or cost less a discount. The cross-check capitalizes distributable earnings after interest. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets.

What RMR is

RMR runs real estate companies it does not own. Its staff are the management of Service Properties Trust (hotels and net lease retail), Industrial Logistics Properties Trust, Diversified Healthcare Trust, Office Properties Income Trust and the mortgage lender Seven Hills Realty Trust, plus private funds, the Sonesta hotel company and a residential business. Fees come from contracts: a base fee on the lower of each REIT's historical cost or its enterprise value, property management fees, construction supervision and, in good years, incentive fees for beating a REIT index.

In the June quarter the four listed equity REITs and Seven Hills paid $33.4 million of the $45.4 million of fee revenue, 73%. Adam Portnoy is chief executive of RMR and, through ABP Trust and the voting Class B shares, controls it. The managed REITs have trustees in common with RMR, which is the source of both the business and the main criticism of it.

The structure matters for the per-share math. Public shareholders own The RMR Group Inc., which owns 17.09 million of the 32.09 million units of the operating company. ABP Trust owns the other 15.0 million units, which can be exchanged for shares. I value the operating company per unit and add the small amount that sits only at the listed company.

Which earnings figure to trust

  • Adjusted EBITDA mixes three businesses. It contains fee earnings, the operating profit of real estate RMR owns ($3.5 million in the June quarter), and dividends from its stakes in Service Properties and Seven Hills ($1.7 million). I separate them, because the stakes and buildings are valued on the balance sheet.
  • Fee earnings are about $55 million a year before tax. That is adjusted EBITDA less the real estate profit, the stake dividends and the part of pay settled in RMR shares, averaged over the last two quarters and checked against the September quarter guidance (my arithmetic).
  • Distributable earnings ignore interest. They are adjusted EBITDA less tax distributions to the owners. Interest was $3.2 million in the June quarter and $2.6 million in March, so after interest the last two quarters average $1.48 a unit a year, not the $1.84 the company's measure gives.
  • Incentive fees sit outside all of these. They were $23.6 million for calendar 2025, from Diversified Healthcare and Industrial Logistics, and nothing in several years before that. In August management said the REITs were on pace for more than $40 million for 2026. Since then Diversified Healthcare has fallen 15% and Industrial Logistics 25% in three months, and the fee is measured at December 31.

Primary valuation: sum of the parts

I value fee earnings after a 21% tax at 10 times, the multiple for flat earnings at a 10% cost of equity, add incentive fees and the balance sheet, and divide by 32.09 million units. Listed stakes, joint ventures and the owned real estate get a 15% discount for the reasons above. The Base value is $21.02.

Cross-check: distributable earnings after interest

The second view ignores the balance sheet marks and counts only cash earnings. The last two quarters averaged $1.48 a unit a year after interest, and $6 million a year of incentive fees adds $0.15. At 10 times, the same multiple as above, that gives $16.27, almost exactly the price.

The two values differ by 25%, more than the 20% level where I stop and explain. The reason is the balance sheet. The earnings view counts Service Properties only through its $0.20 dividend, a 3.2% yield, and gives nothing for $38 million of spare cash, the OPI shares or the joint ventures, which pay little or nothing. The sum of the parts counts them at market less 15%. Which one is right depends on whether RMR ever turns those stakes into cash for its owners, and its record so far is to put cash into them.

Valuation triangulation

  • Primary (sum of the parts, Base): $21.02. This is the central value.
  • Cross-check (earnings after interest at 10 times): $16.27, 25% below, explained by the balance sheet.
  • Nearby assumptions (cost 9% to 11%, fee earnings $50 to $60 million): $18.53 to $24.05.
  • Balance sheet at full marks, no discount: $21.98.

I keep the sum of the parts as the central value, because the stakes and buildings are real and marked to market, but the cross-check is the reason the buy price is low. It shows what the stock is worth if the balance sheet never pays out.

How the buy price is set

Confidence is C, which carries a default margin of safety of 42.5%. I add nothing to it, because the client, incentive fee and balance sheet risks already sit in the scenarios and the discount. That gives a maximum buy price of $12.08.

With a cost of equity between 9% and 11% and fee earnings between $50 and $60 million, the same margin gives buy prices from $10.66 to $13.83. The price of $16.61 is above all of them, so the decision does not depend on which is right. The 52-week low is $14.73.

With no change in valuation and flat fee earnings, the return over the next few years is roughly the dividend, 10.8% a year if it is kept, or about 8.9% if it is cut to what recurring earnings after interest cover. Anything above that needs the market to change its mind about the fee business.

Dividend

RMR pays $0.45 a quarter, $1.80 a year, a 10.8% yield at $16.61, unchanged for at least the last two years. The operating company pays $0.32 a unit to all owners, and the listed company adds $0.13 from cash that built up because tax distributions it receives exceed the tax it pays. Management says that cash, $15.4 million in June, can fund the top-up for more than two years.

Reasons to own RMR

  • The fees are contractual, recurring and earn a margin above 40% before interest, and $37.5 billion of assets sit under management.
  • Listed stakes, owned real estate and spare cash are worth about $5.43 a unit after my discount, a third of the price, so the fee business costs about 7 times after-tax earnings.
  • Diversified Healthcare and Industrial Logistics have been strong performers over three years, and if that holds through December, 2026 incentive fees could be well above my $25 million.
  • A 10.8% yield, paid at the same rate for at least two years.
  • The family owns 15 million units and receives the same distribution, so its interest in the cash payout matches the public shareholders'.

What could go wrong?

  • Client concentration. Five related REITs paid 73% of fee revenue in the June quarter, and the base fee shrinks when they sell assets, as Service Properties is doing.
  • Contract strength. The OPI bankruptcy replaced a twenty-year contract with one that can end without penalty in 2028. Another client in trouble could test the same path.
  • Incentive fees. They depend on the REITs' share prices against an index on December 31, and two of them have fallen 15% to 25% since the summer.
  • Capital allocation. RMR used cash and its credit line to buy into a client's share offering and owns buildings with $141 million of mortgages, which adds debt to an asset-light business.
  • Governance. The Portnoy family controls the voting shares, the managed REITs and RMR share trustees, and the tax receivable agreement pays ABP Trust from the listed company's tax savings.

Management execution

  • The managed REITs have mixed records: Diversified Healthcare and Industrial Logistics rebounded strongly, while Office Properties went bankrupt and Service Properties needed new equity and a reverse split.
  • RMR kept OPI as a client through the restructuring, but on weaker terms, and wrote off $19.1 million of the old contract's value.
  • Guidance is specific and was met in the June quarter: adjusted EBITDA and distributable earnings were inside the ranges given in May.
  • Private capital raising, the planned source of growth, has been slow, and the company is now buying real estate with its own balance sheet.
  • The dividend has been held at $0.45 for at least two years and is described in full, including the part paid from the listed company's cash.

On my ten-point management check (two points each), RMR scores 8 of 20: credit for disclosure and keeping guidance, little for capital allocation, per-share growth and governance.

What I would watch from here

The fiscal year report around November 12 should show four things for the Base to hold: September quarter distributable earnings of at least $0.48 a unit, an incentive fee estimate for 2026 of at least $20 million if the year ended on September 30, guidance for the December quarter near today's level, and no further drop in the owned retail center's occupancy.

Green

  • Incentive fees for 2026 of $25 million or more, paid in early 2027.
  • Fee revenue above $47 million a quarter, from higher REIT values or new private capital.
  • Cash from the stakes or real estate returned to owners or used to repay the credit line.

Yellow

  • Distributable earnings after interest below $0.40 a unit a quarter, where they sit today ($0.38 in June).
  • More balance sheet money put into managed REITs or new owned buildings.
  • The listed company's spare cash falling below $8 million.

Red

  • Another managed REIT restructuring its contract or announcing a strategic review.
  • No incentive fees for 2026.
  • A dividend cut below the $0.32 the operating company pays per unit.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. OPI ends its contract in 2028 or another client follows its route, taking fee earnings toward the Bear's $44 million.
  2. Incentive fees stay near zero, leaving the dividend paid from cash that runs out.
  3. More of the balance sheet goes into supporting managed REITs at prices that do not recover.

The weakest point is the incentive fee, because the 2026 figure alone moves the value by about $0.60 and the later average by about $1.48, and both depend on share prices of other companies on one date. The strongest bull argument is that a third of the price is assets at market and the fee business costs 7 times earnings. The strongest bear argument is that RMR keeps putting its cash into the same family of companies, so the balance sheet should be valued like the cross-check, at about today's price.

Final verdict: hold at $16.61

RMR is cheap if you count the balance sheet and fairly priced if you count only the cash it earns. The 11% yield sits between the two: covered by the company's own measure, not covered after interest, and topped up by incentive fees that are decided by the stock market on December 31.

Verdict: HOLD at $16.61, watchlist for new money. Base $21.02, 26.5% above the price, Bear $13.00, Bull $28.33, cross-check $16.27, Confidence C (15/25). Required margin of safety 42.5%, maximum buy price $12.08, range $10.66 to $13.83 under nearby assumptions.

New money buys only at or below $12.08. For an existing holder the original cost is irrelevant, and a Base value above the price supports holding while the dividend is paid; I would review a holding above the Base of about $21. The next tests are the fiscal year report around November 12 and the 2026 incentive fee in early 2027.
