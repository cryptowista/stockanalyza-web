## What MGIC is

MGIC insures mortgage lenders, and in practice Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, against losses when a borrower with less than 20% down stops paying. The borrower pays the premium, usually monthly, until the loan is paid down or the home gains enough value for the cover to be cancelled. MGIC founded the modern private mortgage insurance industry in 1957 and is one of six active insurers, with about $307 billion of insurance in force across about 1.1 million loans.

The business has three moving parts. Premiums come in as long as loans stay insured, so persistency, the share of policies still in force a year later, matters as much as new business. Claims arrive only when a delinquent loan ends in a sale for less than the debt, so home prices matter more than unemployment alone. And capital is set by the GSEs' rules, known as PMIERs: MGIC must hold assets above a required minimum, and it uses reinsurance to cut that requirement by about half.

## What changed in Washington

On October 22, 2026 the President signed an executive order on housing costs. It allows the FHFA to have Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac build a pilot that identifies borrowers who qualify to cancel their mortgage insurance, using automated home valuations, and requires servicers to tell them. The pilot would apply only to loans made after it starts. Borrowers already have the right to cancel, but many do not ask, so a proactive program would shorten policy lives on future loans. The text was narrower than an earlier draft, and the acting FHA commissioner said in September that the administration is comfortable with today's mortgage insurance structure, which lowered the risk of an FHA premium cut.

Mortgage rates went the other way. The 30-year fixed rate passed 7% in late September and averaged 7.32% in the week to October 22. High rates keep existing borrowers from refinancing, which helps persistency, and slow new business.

## Which numbers to trust

- **Losses are reported after releases.** Losses incurred were 10.0% of premiums in the third quarter and about 10% over the last four. New delinquencies alone cost 24% of premiums at MGIC's own claim assumption. The truth over a cycle lies in between, and I use 20%.
- **Book value is understated by the bond marks.** The $1.62 a share of unrealized losses will flow back as the bonds mature if they are held, so I value the company on book value excluding them, $25.68.
- **GAAP and adjusted earnings agree.** Net income of $175.0 million and adjusted operating income of $174.7 million differ only by small investment gains and losses, so there is no hidden adjustment to undo.
- **Return on equity is flattered by releases.** The reported 14.2% falls to about 12% with my normal claims cost.

## Primary valuation: book value and normal earnings

I rebuild a normal year from today's run rate: premiums of $954 million and investment income of $244 million, the third quarter times four; claims at 20% of premiums; costs of $182 million, about the third quarter's pace; interest of $36 million; and 21% tax. That gives $623 million of net income, $3.09 a share and a 12.1% return on book value excluding bond marks. With a 10% cost of equity and 2% growth, the justified price is 1.26 times book. - **Why 20% for claims:** it sits between the roughly 10% MGIC actually incurred after releases and the 24% it books on new delinquencies. In a recession the number would be far higher for a year or two; that belongs in the Bear, not in every year.
- **Why 10%:** the beta is about 0.6, but mortgage insurance is a single bet on U.S. housing credit that nearly broke the industry in 2008. I round up for that.
- **Why 2% growth:** insurance in force grows 2% to 3% a year and MGIC pays out roughly everything it earns, so book value per share grows mainly through buybacks.

Rules of thumb from the model: each 5 points of claims cost move the value by about $2.30 a share, and each point of cost of equity by about $3.60.

## Cross-check: earnings power

The second view ignores book value and growth. Normal earnings of $3.09 a share, paid out in full and capitalized at the 10% cost of equity, are worth **$30.94**, 4.1% below the Base. The two agree because MGIC earns only a little more than its cost of capital in a normal year, so most of the value is the book itself.

## How the buy price is set

Confidence is B, which carries a default margin of safety of 30%. I add nothing to it, because the housing cycle already sits in the Bear and the claims assumption. That gives a maximum buy price of **$22.57**.

With claims between 15% and 25% of premiums and a cost of equity between 9.5% and 10.5%, the same margin gives buy prices from $19.71 to $25.83. The price of $28.64 is above all of them, so the decision does not depend on which is right. The 52-week range is $24.69 to $31.89.

## Dividend

MGIC pays $0.17 a quarter, $0.68 a year, a 2.4% yield at $28.64, raised for six straight years. The dividend costs about $137 million a year against normal earnings of about $620 million, so it is well covered; the buyback is the bigger part of the return and the first to shrink if credit turns.

## Reasons to own MGIC

- Capital well above requirements: $2.8 billion of PMIERs excess and reinsurance bought out to 2029.
- A shrinking share count: about 11% fewer shares than a year ago, bought near 1.1 times book.
- Conservative reserving: new delinquencies are booked at a 7.5% claim rate while recent ones end at 2% to 4%.
- High mortgage rates keep borrowers in their loans, which keeps premiums coming on the existing book.
- The price needs claims near 28% of premiums to be fair, a cushion against normal credit.

## What could go wrong?

- Housing and jobs. A recession with falling home prices turns delinquencies into claims. Today almost none do: MGIC reserves new delinquencies at a 7.5% claim rate and recent groups have ended near 2% to 4%. In a downturn that gap closes from the other side.
- The end of reserve releases. Favorable development has supported earnings for several years. If credit simply normalizes, reported profit falls toward my normalized figure even without a recession.
- Policy. A proactive cancellation program, lower GSE fees that favor FHA loans, or changes to capital rules would all reduce returns. The October order affects only future loans, but its details are not written yet.
- Premium yield. Net premium yield has drifted down as more premium is ceded to reinsurers and older, higher-priced policies run off.
- Buybacks at a premium. MGIC buys back roughly its whole profit at about 1.1 times book. That adds value only while returns stay above the cost of capital.

## Management execution

- Expenses are falling: underwriting and other expenses were $93.7 million in the first half of 2026 against $105.2 million a year earlier, and the full year is guided toward the low end of $190 to $200 million.
- The reinsurance program is broad and renewed early, including excess of loss cover already bought for 2027 business, and it cut the capital requirement by about $3.1 billion at mid-year.
- Capital return is consistent: share count down about 11% in a year and six straight years of dividend increases.
- Reserving is conservative, which is why releases keep coming. That is a strength for the balance sheet and a reason to discount the reported profit.
- Growth is limited by the market, not by management: insurance in force grows about 2% to 3% a year.

On my ten-point management check (two points each), MGIC scores 15 of 20: credit for capital discipline, reinsurance, cost control and disclosure, less for growth and for buybacks above book.

## What I would watch from here

The earnings call on November 5 should explain the rise in new delinquency notices and confirm the capital plan for 2027. Over the next quarters the reserve release line matters most: as long as older delinquencies cure better than reserved, reported profit stays above my normal level.

### Green

- Delinquency rate back below 2.4% after the seasonal peak.
- PMIERs excess staying above $2.5 billion while buybacks continue.
- Book value excluding bond marks growing 8% or more a year.

### Yellow

- Reserve releases shrinking toward zero for two quarters.
- New notices above 15,000 a quarter outside the seasonal pattern.
- FHFA publishing a cancellation pilot that applies broadly.

### Red

- Reserve strengthening on prior years instead of releases.
- PMIERs excess below $1.5 billion.
- Buybacks continuing while the delinquency rate rises above 3%.

## The three most important thesis breakers

1. A housing downturn that turns the 7.5% claim assumption from conservative to optimistic.
2. Policy changes, starting with the cancellation pilot, that shorten policy lives on new business.
3. Buybacks that continue at a premium to book into a credit downturn.

The weakest point is the claims cost, because five points move the value by about $2.30 and the last decade gives little evidence of what a normal recession costs this book. The strongest bull argument is that the market already assumes claims near 28% of premiums. The strongest bear argument is that mortgage insurance has long quiet periods and short violent ones, and a 30% margin of safety is the minimum for that.

## Final verdict: hold at $28.64

MGIC is a well-run insurer with more capital than it needs, and its stock is modestly below what a normal year is worth. Part of today's profit is the reserving of the past being too careful. That is a good sign about the balance sheet, and a reason not to pay for it twice.

**Verdict: HOLD at $28.64, watchlist for new money. Base $32.25, 12.6% above the price, Bear $22.17, Bull $40.46, cross-check $30.94, Confidence B (20/25). Required margin of safety 30%, maximum buy price $22.57, range $19.71 to $25.83 under nearby assumptions.**

New money buys only at or below $22.57. For an existing holder the original cost is irrelevant, and a Base value above the price supports holding while capital stays strong and credit normalizes slowly. The next tests are the call on November 5 and the delinquency trend through the winter.
