Bending Spoons reported third-quarter revenue of $780.2 million, up 125% from $346.7 million. Organic growth, the part from businesses it already owned a year ago, was 3%. The rest was bought: AOL, Eventbrite, Vimeo, Tractive, Airtable and others. That is not a flaw in the model, it is the model, and the company says so. But it changes what the shares are: not a software business growing 125%, but an acquirer that has to keep finding deals at good prices, and keep paying for them with debt and new shares. At $45.25 the market pays for that to continue for a very long time.

My Base value is $22.29, the Bear value is $9.09 and the Bull value is $39.68, so the price sits above all three. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 42.5%, so the maximum buy price is $12.82. The shares would need to fall 72% to reach it. The price history is short and sharp: the IPO priced at $29.00 on June 30, the stock closed at $53.46 on October 14 after Berkshire Hathaway's placement was announced, was $40.50 on November 11 and is $45.25 now.

What Bending Spoons is

Bending Spoons, founded in Milan in 2013, buys established digital products that have stalled, moves them onto its own engineering, pricing and subscription systems, cuts costs, and uses the cash to buy the next one. Its portfolio includes AOL, Eventbrite, Evernote, Vimeo, WeTransfer, Brightcove, Komoot, Meetup, Harvest, MileIQ, Tractive, Airtable and, since October 26, Miro. In its prospectus it reported more than 500 million monthly active users and more than 9 million monthly paying customers in March 2026. Management said on the third-quarter call that about one-third of revenue comes from consumer subscriptions.

The financial history is the acquisition history. Revenue was $387 million in 2023, $671 million in 2024 and $1.31 billion in 2025. Adjusted operating income, the company's own measure that adds back amortization of acquired intangibles, deal costs and reorganization costs, was $613.2 million in 2025, a 47% margin. Operating income on the normal accounting basis was $277.9 million, a 21% margin. The gap between the two is large and recurring: in 2025 it was $150 million of amortization, $85.5 million of transaction costs, $78.6 million of reorganization costs and $20.8 million of other items. Stock compensation is not added back to adjusted operating income, which matters for the valuation below.

The company went public on July 1, 2026. The IPO priced at $29.00, above the $26 to $28 range, and sold 57.97 million shares, 34.4 million of them new, for about $1.68 billion in total. The shares closed the first day about 38% higher. Since then the company has raised capital almost every month: a $330 million private placement to Berkshire Hathaway at $33.00 a share (closed October 29), $1.8 billion of senior secured bonds (settled around November 4), a $1.3 billion private placement of 32.5 million shares at $40.00 announced November 19, and a pending $3.68 billion purchase of Mailchimp from Intuit.

Why I use cash after interest and a full tax bill

Adjusted operating income is a useful operating measure, but it is not what shareholders receive. Between it and shareholder cash sit reorganization costs that have recurred every period, interest that has more than doubled, and taxes. Cash taxes paid were only $23.5 million over nine months, helped by deductions I could not break down, so I do not assume that repeats: I use a 22% tax charge on pre-tax profit after reorganization costs and interest.

For the base year I start from the fourth-quarter guidance midpoint of $480 million annualized, $1,920 million, which includes Airtable and Miro for a full year. Reorganization costs are 6% of that ($115 million), against $50.8 million in the second quarter and $25.1 million in the third, so I assume a decline as integrations finish, but not zero. Capital spending is $25 million, because the business is almost capital free: $9.2 million in nine months. Net interest is $500 million, my estimate (see the balance sheet section). I do not deduct stock compensation separately, because it is already inside adjusted operating income: it was $65.6 million over nine months.

Primary valuation: ten years of cash flow to shareholders

The primary method discounts ten years of cash flow after interest and full tax at 11%. A risk-free rate near 5%, an equity premium of about 5% and a point for a levered, newly listed acquirer come to about 11%. At 10% the Base would be $24.01 and at 12% $20.88, and the verdict does not change at either.

Base grows adjusted operating income 4% a year for five years and 2.5% for the next five, from $1,920 million to $2,336 million in 2031 and $2,643 million in 2036. That sits near the roughly 4% organic growth in full-year guidance (about 5% in the fourth quarter) and below the 6% to 10% that older cohorts show. Capital spending starts at $25 million and rises 3% a year, tax is 22% of profit after reorganization and interest, and interest is held at $500 million, so net debt stays at $3,480 million. Terminal growth is 2.5%. The table shows the three tiers of cash flow for the first five years.

That works only if leverage stays near 2x and credit markets stay open, and the Base case keeps net debt flat while adjusted operating income grows.

The value of future acquisitions

The company says it can deploy about $5 billion a year on average, with larger deals possible. I use $3.68 billion in 2027 for Mailchimp and $3.0 billion a year after that in Base, below management's figure because it describes its engineers and operators, called Spooners, as its scarcest resource and the limit on deal pace. A deal creates value for shareholders only if its after-tax return on the price paid beats the cost of the money, which I set at 9.5% for debt and equity together. The surplus, discounted, is the acquisition line.

The Base return of 11% comes from Mailchimp itself. It generated $1.27 billion of revenue in the twelve months to October 31, 2026, flat to down 1%, and costs $3.68 billion, 2.9 times revenue. If it reaches a 40% adjusted operating margin, which is below the 48% to 54% the group shows, it earns $508 million, or $396 million after 22% tax, which is 10.8% of the price. Management said Mailchimp's price is near the 2.5 times revenue average of 2023 to 2025 deals and that returns should still be very high but somewhat lower than before. My Bull return is 14% (margin near 50%) and my Bear return is 8%. In Base the acquisition line is worth $2.64 a share, about 12% of the primary value. Even with no future acquisitions at all, the Base would be $19.65.

Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 9 times adjusted operating income

The cross-check takes the same first five years of cash flow and values the business at the end of 2031 at a multiple of adjusted operating income, less net debt. At the Base exit of 9.0 times, the enterprise is worth $21,024 million then, equity is $17,544 million after $3,480 million of net debt, and the secondary value, with the same acquisition line, is $22.89.

Why 9 times. Today's enterprise value of $36.3 billion is 18.9 times annualized fourth-quarter adjusted operating income. At an 11% return on equity, a 9.0 times exit on 2031 income implies unlevered cash flow growing about 2.3% a year forever, which matches the 2.5% terminal growth in the primary method and fits a business whose organic growth is 3% to 6% and whose profit must be reinvested to hold its position. Bear uses 7.0 times, which implies about 0.0% growth, and Bull uses 12.0 times, which implies about 4.3%. A 12.0 times multiple already assumes growth well above the group's organic rate.

Exit value share: 71% of the cash flow value comes from the exit, at a horizon of five years, below the 85% level where I check that the exit is supported by sustainable flows. I could not build a synchronized peer table for listed acquirers such as Constellation Software or for the media and software assets Bending Spoons owns, so the multiple rests on the arithmetic above and not on comparable companies.

What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $45.25 needs one of five things, each on its own with the others unchanged: core adjusted operating income growing 11.4 points a year faster than my Base for ten years, an exit multiple of 33 times instead of 9, a discount rate of 5.3% instead of 11%, an after-tax return on acquisitions of 24% instead of 11%, or 9.7 times my acquisition volume, about $29 billion a year, at an 11% return. None of those is a small step. The price is not asking for a good outcome, it is asking for a very good one that lasts: the market is pricing Bending Spoons as an acquirer that can deploy tens of billions at returns well above its funding cost for a decade.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C starts at a 42.5% margin of safety. I added nothing on top for leverage, the short listing history or the acquisition record, because each is already charged elsewhere: an 11% discount rate, full tax against cash taxes of $23.5 million, a Bear case with falling income and value-destroying deals, a 9.0 times exit, and the Confidence score itself. A 42.5% discount to the $22.29 Base gives a maximum buy price of $12.82. That is 56% below the IPO price of $29.00, so the stock would need a full collapse to get there. The verdict is not close to the line: Reduce starts when the Base is 30% or more below the price, and it is 51% below.

Two inputs deserve a note. The return on acquisitions moves the Base by 24% down and 24% up, and a two point change in growth moves it about 13%. Those are the real swing factors, and no single reasonable change brings the Base near $45.

What the options market implies

I did not obtain a current options chain for Bending Spoons, so I draw no conclusion about implied volatility or tail risk, and the options check is not supported. The price history shows how much the stock moves: $29.00 at the IPO, $53.46 on October 14, $40.50 on November 11, and a gain of 6.4% in the current session. A third-party 52-week range runs from $30.11 to $59.50.

Dividend and buybacks

Bending Spoons pays no dividend, and management did not discuss dividends or buybacks on the third-quarter call. Capital goes to acquisitions and to financing them. The share count has risen about 18% in a year, so the question for shareholders is not payout but whether each new share issued at $33 to $42 buys more value than it costs. At my Base value of $22.29, issuing shares at $40.00 is good for existing holders; the problem is that every investor who buys now at $45.25 pays more than that.

Reasons to own BSP

  • The cash generation is real. Operating cash flow was $515.6 million over nine months against capital spending of $9.2 million, and management points to a run rate above $1 billion.
  • Acquired businesses keep growing after the deal. The 2023 cohort grows 10%, the 2024 cohort 8% and the 2025 and 2026 cohorts 6%, and WeTransfer and Komoot grew more than 30%.
  • Deal economics look attractive. Mailchimp at 2.9 times revenue could earn about 11% after tax at a 40% margin, above my 9.5% cost of money.
  • Funding is open and leverage is moderate. Net leverage fell from 2.4x to 2.1x in the quarter, liquidity is $3.36 billion, a debut bond issue was upsized, and Berkshire Hathaway invested $330 million.
  • A long runway. Management says $5 billion a year is a reasonable assumption for deployment, and it has bought more than 50 businesses.

What could go wrong?

  • Growth is bought. Organic growth is 3%, and the reported 125% revenue growth cannot continue without new deals of rising size.
  • Funding costs. The new bonds pay 9.625% and 8.625%, the credit ratings are below investment grade, and floating-rate debt is only partly hedged. Interest expense rose 163% in a year.
  • Dilution. Four share issues since early October (Berkshire, Miro, the $1.3 billion placement and Intuit) add 57.5 million shares, about 9% of the September count.
  • AI and retention. Management expects no AI effect so far on the consumer subscriptions, which are about a third of revenue, but allows that some deterioration is possible over time. Remini has low retention. Brightcove is declining.
  • Pricing and cost cuts. The model depends on raising prices and cutting costs. Management expects a modest one-time churn spike from a new AOL pricing setup, and most of Airtable's pre-acquisition team is largely leaving under separation packages.
  • Accounting. Amortization of acquired intangibles was $189.0 million in a quarter, including a $65 million catch-up charge, and goodwill and intangibles are 78% of assets. A big deal that disappoints leads to impairment.
  • Execution capacity. Management calls its people the scarcest resource, and integrating Airtable, Miro and Mailchimp in the same six months is a heavy load.
  • Supply of shares and sentiment. The stock fell about 24% from its October 14 close to November 11 and then rose again, and lock-up expiries for existing holders are a data gap.

Management execution

Luca Ferrari, a co-founder, is chief executive. The record is strong on the thing they say they do: revenue rose from $387 million to $1.31 billion in two years, and adjusted operating margin rose from 36% in 2023 to 47% in 2025. Guidance has been raised, but I note that the raise from $2.78 billion to $3.07 billion of revenue came from deals that closed after the August guidance, not from existing businesses. Against that, the pace has changed: first-quarter 2026 acquisitions had an aggregate enterprise value of $2.01 billion, and since then it has added Tractive ($759 million), Airtable ($1.29 billion), Miro ($1.36 billion) and Mailchimp ($3.68 billion). The size of the deals and the dependence on outside capital are rising, a character change that I count against the score. Management credibility is 13/20: excellent on operations and deal sourcing, short on a public record, and unproven when credit gets tight.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Deal returns fall to the cost of funding because sellers learn what the buyer will pay, and the company keeps buying for growth.
  2. Credit markets close or tighten while leverage is above 3x, and equity has to be issued below intrinsic value.
  3. Price increases and AI substitutes cause consumer churn, and organic growth turns negative across the acquired portfolio.

The Base of $22.29 sits 33% of the way up that range, and the price sits at the top of it. Choices that lean conservative are the full 22% tax charge, a flat debt balance, reorganization costs that never go to zero and the 11% discount rate. Choices that lean generous are net interest of $500 million that already nets a large cash balance, no deduction for placement fees, and an assumption that Mailchimp and other deals close on time.

The strongest counter-argument is that the machine is better than my arithmetic: the company turns purchases at 2.5 to 2.9 times revenue into 50% margins, the cohorts grow, and deployment could be much larger than $3 billion a year. It is the Bull case, and in the grid above it is worth $45 only at about $10 billion a year of deals at a 14% return. The company has no record of deploying that much through a credit cycle, and its own leverage guide of 2x to 3x limits the debt part of it.
