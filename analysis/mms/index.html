Maximus guides to fiscal 2027 free cash flow of $700 to $800 million, which looks like a 23% yield on its $3.2 billion market value. It is not. Fiscal 2026 free cash flow was $69 million, against a guide of $425 to $475 million given in August, because a major federal customer paid late, and the 2027 guide includes the catch-up. Take it out and a normal year of owner cash flow is about $417 million, or $7.87 a share, a 12.9% yield. That is still cheap. But the company is also changing: it closed the $660 million Agile Defense acquisition on November 18, loses about $75 million of one-time VA incentive profit, and carries roughly $1.74 billion of net debt by my estimate. At $61.17 the shares trade 22.6% below my Base value of $79.00.

The Bear value is $49.04 and the Bull value is $119.38, so the price sits 24.7% above Bear and 48.8% below Bull. The required margin of safety (MOS), the discount to fair value I need before buying, is 42.5%, so the maximum buy price is $45.43. The shares are 34.7% above that and would need to fall 25.7% to reach it.

Valuation route: a government services contractor, treated as an established non-financial business and valued on the free cash flow that belongs to shareholders after interest, a full tax rate and stock compensation. The primary method is a ten-year discounted cash flow. The cross-check is five years of cash flow plus a discounted exit at a multiple of adjusted EBITDA. Economic classification: Standard. Values are present fair-value estimates, not 12-month price targets. The price is the November 25 close from a third-party quote page, which a broker screen showed within 0.6%.

What Maximus is

Maximus runs government programs for public agencies: it handles enrollment and call centers for Medicaid and other benefit programs, performs medical disability exams for the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA), and supplies technology and modernization work to federal agencies. Fiscal 2026 revenue was $5,231 million, split between U.S. Federal Services ($2,992 million, 18.1% segment operating margin), U.S. Services ($1,682 million, 9.1%) and Outside the U.S. ($557 million, a 1.0% operating margin loss).

The stock has been through a hard year. Adjusted earnings per share of $8.24 for fiscal 2026 look like 12% growth, but they include an estimated $1.05 a share from performance incentives on the VA medical exam contracts, worth about $75 million of revenue and profit in the first three quarters. A customer-directed change to the program removed those incentives in the fourth quarter, and the fiscal 2027 guide assumes none. Without the incentive, fiscal 2026 adjusted earnings were about $7.19 a share, 2% below the $7.36 of fiscal 2025 (my arithmetic). The shares fell 5.9% intraday on the day of the results, to about $61.44.

Then there is Agile Defense, a defense and national security technology firm bought for a $660 million base price in cash, with further payments tied to results. The release does not give the terms or Agile's earnings. A third-party summary of the related filing reports up to $50 million of payments tied to first-year revenue and $295 million of new secured term loans. I treat both as estimates.

Why I use $417 million of cash flow and not the $700 to $800 million guide

The guide midpoint of $750 million is $333 million above my normalized figure. The company says the outlook includes collections that slipped past year end and a return of days sales outstanding from 93 to its 60 to 70 range. Both are a one-time catch-up of cash that belonged to fiscal 2026. Fiscal 2026's $69 million plus the more than $330 million collected after year end comes to about $400 million, close to the $425 to $475 million the company had guided for that year. The cash was late, not extra.

So I rebuild a normal year from the guide's own profit and interest figures. Adjusted EBITDA of $707.5 million (the guide midpoint, which is already after about $35 million of stock compensation) less $135.6 million of tax, $45 million of capital spending and $110 million of interest gives owner free cash flow of $417 million, or $7.87 a share on 53.0 million diluted shares. The tax is a full 25% of EBITDA less $55 million of depreciation and $110 million of interest. I do not deduct the $96 million of acquired-intangible amortization for tax, which is conservative if part of it is deductible. I used the full rate because, as I read the fiscal 2025 annual report, cash taxes of about $150 million ran well above the $100 million tax charge, so the book tax line flatters cash. Cash conversion then comes out near 103% of the $404 million of adjusted net income implied by the guide, which is plausible for a business with depreciation near capital spending.

Two flags remain. Management has described a program that sells receivables, and unbilled receivables have grown, so reported cash flow can be pulled forward. Neither is quantified in the release, and I did not add a haircut. I did include the working-capital catch-up in the valuation as a one-time cash credit of $300 million in Base ($200 million in Bear and $330 million in Bull), because the company has already collected more than $330 million of it.

Balance sheet and shares

At September 30 Maximus held $280 million of cash against $1,610 million of gross debt, a net debt of $1,330 million and leverage of 1.8x on the credit agreement's definition. Since then it paid $660 million for Agile Defense, with $295 million of new term loans (a third-party figure) and cash, and it collected the delayed receivables. My pro forma net debt is about $1,740 million: $1,905 million of gross debt, about $215 million of cash after the deal, and $50 million set aside for the milestone payments. That is 2.5 times the guided fiscal 2027 adjusted EBITDA. The company says pro forma leverage is about 2.3x on the credit agreement's definition, which differs from my simple net debt over guided EBITDA, and inside its 2x to 3x target.

I did not find the fiscal 2026 annual report, so the debt maturity schedule and interest hedges below come from the credit agreement filings and the prior year's report as I read them earlier. The term loans and the revolver mature in May 2029, about 30 months away, and about $375 million of floating-rate debt was hedged into 2027 and 2028. These should be confirmed in the annual report when it is filed.

The share count tells a quieter story. Maximus bought back 5.8 million shares for $457 million in fiscal 2025 and about 2.6 million shares for roughly $192 million (or $205 million, the two figures in the release disagree) in fiscal 2026, with $400 million of authorization left. The fiscal 2026 diluted count was 54.0 million, the fourth quarter was 53.1 million, and the fiscal 2027 guide assumes 52.1 million on average. I use 53.0 million, today's approximate diluted count, and show 52.1 million in the stress test.

Primary valuation: ten years of owner free cash flow

The primary method discounts ten years of owner free cash flow to shareholders at 10%, the framework default, and I test 9% and 11%. The first cash flow is a full year away, which is slightly conservative.

Base starts from $417 million in fiscal 2027. The legacy business loses 3% of EBITDA in fiscal 2028, because the main VA medical exam contract is due to be re-bid from July 2027 (as I understand management's comments earlier in the year) and the incentive is gone, then is flat in 2029 and grows 1% a year after that. Agile Defense is $53 million of EBITDA for its first ten months, then grows 12%, 8%, 6% and 4% in the next four years and 2% after. Owner free cash flow stays between $415 million and $433 million through 2031 and reaches $462 million in 2036. Terminal growth is 1%, so the terminal value is 11.2 times the 2036 cash flow, an 8.9% yield. The present value of the ten years is $50.09 a share, the terminal value adds $37.70 and the excess cash after the deal adds $0.28. The primary value is $88.07.

Terminal value share: 43% of the primary value, at a ten-year horizon, below the 75% level where I would check that the terminal value is supported by sustainable flows. Buybacks are not added to the cash flows, because repurchases at fair value do not change value per share.

Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 7.0 times EBITDA

The cross-check takes the first five years of the same cash flow and values the business at the end of fiscal 2031 at a multiple of adjusted EBITDA. Fiscal 2031 EBITDA is $732 million. At 7.0 times, the business is worth $5,127 million, less $1,755 million of net debt (gross debt of $1,905 million less $150 million of minimum cash) leaves $3,372 million for shareholders. Discounted at 10% that is $39.51 a share, the five years of cash flow add $30.14 and excess cash adds $0.28. The secondary value is $69.93.

Why 7.0 times. Today's enterprise value is about 7.0 times fiscal 2027 adjusted EBITDA ($3,242 million of market value plus $1,740 million of net debt is $4,982 million). After tax and capital spending, 7.0 times EBITDA implies essentially no growth in after-tax operating cash flow forever at a 10% return. That fits a business whose largest contracts are re-bid, with little reinvestment need and a thin organic growth record. Bear uses 6.0 times, which implies a decline of about 1.4% a year, and Bull uses 8.5 times, which implies growth of about 1.5%. The leverage is moderate and held flat in all three cases.

Exit value share: 56% of the secondary value comes from the exit, at a horizon of five years, below the 85% level where I check that the exit is supported by sustainable flows.

The two methods differ by 23% of their midpoint, which is in the 20% to 30% warning band, so I took the gap apart before weighing anything. Both use the same cash flows, the same net debt and the same share count. The whole difference is the end multiple: the ten-year method is worth 9.1 times EBITDA at the end of fiscal 2031, and the exit method uses 7.0 times. Setting the exit to 9.1 times gives $88.04, the primary value, so nothing else differs. Both answers are defensible. 9.1 times implies growth of about 2% a year in after-tax operating cash flow forever at a 10% return, which is generous for a contractor that must re-win its big contracts. 7.0 times is today's market multiple and implies no growth. The choice does not change the decision: at the top of the range the maximum buy price would be $50.64 (42.5% below $88.07), and at the bottom $40.21 (42.5% below $69.93), and the price of $61.17 is above both.

What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $61.17 needs one of two things: owner free cash flow about 30% lower than my Base in every year (about $291 million in the first year, $5.49 a share, instead of $417 million), or an exit multiple of 6.0 times with the other inputs fixed (cross-check method only). No terminal growth rate gets the primary method down to the price, since even a cash flow that falls 8% a year after 2036 still gives $67.55. The market is pricing something between my Bear and Base: a hard VA re-bid, or a catch-up that is smaller than management says.

As a return, buying at $61.17 and holding the Base case for five years, with the cash flows added up and an exit at 7.0 times EBITDA, gives about 11.1% a year. The same math gives 0.1% a year in Bear and 22.6% in Bull. Because the exit multiple of 7.0 times equals today's multiple, the Base return is mostly the cash flow, not a re-rating.

On earnings the shares trade at 7.9 times the fiscal 2027 adjusted EPS guidance midpoint of $7.75, 8.5 times fiscal 2026 adjusted earnings excluding the incentive. The growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) is not meaningful on guided EPS, which falls 6%, and about 1.0 on the roughly 8% growth excluding the incentive. It is a diagnostic only, and acquisition-affected. The free cash flow growth check points the same way as the cash analysis above: free cash flow was $366 million, then $69 million, then a guided $700 to $800 million, so the swings are working-capital timing and I use no multi-year growth rate from them.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C starts at a 42.5% margin of safety. I added nothing on top, because each risk is already charged once: the lost incentive and the VA re-bid in the Bear scenario, refinancing in the Bear interest cost, the working-capital catch-up in a haircut credit, the method disagreement in the Confidence score for model construction, and the acquisition in the scorecard. A 42.5% discount to the $79.00 Base gives a maximum buy price of $45.43. The framework's valuation gap alone (Base 29.2% above the price) would classify the stock as undervalued, but a Hold is right at a price above the maximum buy, and an acquisition-led change in the company's character argues for a step down from a Buy in any case. A single input moving by 15% of its own size, such as the 10% discount rate moving to 11.5%, changes the Base by 10.3%, and cutting every cash flow by 20% changes it by 14.9%, so the buy price is not hostage to one assumption. I could not confirm the 52-week low, so I do not say whether the stock has traded at that price in the past year.

What the options market implies

I did not obtain a current options chain for Maximus, so I draw no conclusion about implied volatility or tail risk, and the options check is not supported. The stock moved 5.9% on the day of the results, and its range this year, from about $98 in January to the mid-$50s in the summer, shows it moves a lot in either direction.

Dividend and buybacks

Maximus pays $0.33 a share a quarter, $1.32 a year, a yield of 2.2% at $61.17. At 52.1 million shares that is about $69 million, or 17% of my owner free cash flow, which is safe. Buybacks are the swing item: $457 million in fiscal 2025 and about $192 million in fiscal 2026, with $400 million of authorization left. With leverage at about 2.3x to 2.5x after the deal and the 2029 refinancing ahead, I expect repurchases to stay below the pace of fiscal 2025 until cash catches up.

Reasons to own MMS

  • A cash yield the price does not reflect. Normalized owner free cash flow of about $417 million is 12.9% of the market value, and the shares trade at 7.9 times guided earnings.
  • High-margin federal work. U.S. Federal Services earned an 18.1% segment margin for the year and 19.8% in the fourth quarter.
  • The cash is coming back. More than $330 million of delayed receivables was collected by November 13, which supports the working-capital catch-up in the guide.
  • A new growth leg. Agile Defense gives Maximus a defense and national security business to grow beside its health and benefits work.
  • Leverage inside the target. The company puts pro forma leverage near 2.3x against a 2x to 3x target, with an interest cover of about 6 times EBITDA in my Base case.

What could go wrong?

  • The VA contracts. The incentive that added about $1.05 a share is gone, and the main contract is due for re-bid from July 2027. This is the single biggest swing in the Bear case.
  • A different company, with more debt. Net debt rises from $1,330 million to about $1,740 million for an acquisition whose earnings are not disclosed, at about 10 times my estimate of its EBITDA.
  • Cash quality. Free cash flow was 18% of net income in fiscal 2026, days sales outstanding reached 93, a receivables sale program exists and cash taxes have run above the tax charge.
  • Government dependence. Most of the business is one customer type. A shutdown, a funding delay or a program change can move the cash by hundreds of millions in a quarter, as this year showed.
  • Refinancing in 2029. The term loans and the revolver come due about 30 months from now, and part of the debt floats.
  • Outside the U.S. The segment lost $5.6 million for the year on $557 million of revenue.

Management execution

Maximus beat the top of its adjusted EPS guidance for fiscal 2026 ($8.24 against a range that had been cut to $7.90 to $8.20 in August), expanded operating margin from 9.7% to 11.7%, and closed a defense acquisition it announced in October within about a month. Against that, free cash flow came in at $69 million against a $425 million to $475 million guide, the adjusted result relied on an incentive that management then had to remove from the outlook, and the 2027 guide shows earnings per share falling 6% at the midpoint. The release also gives two different buyback totals. Management credibility is 10/20: good on margin and deal execution, weak on cash forecasts and on the durability of earnings.

What I would watch from here

Green

  • First-half fiscal 2027 free cash flow of $250 million or more, with days sales outstanding at 80 or below and heading to the 60 to 70 range
  • The VA medical exam contracts extended or won again on terms that include performance incentives
  • Agile Defense revenue running at or above the plan implied by the guide, with the milestone payments earned
  • Net debt to EBITDA under 2.2x by the end of fiscal 2027

Yellow

  • First-half free cash flow of $100 to $250 million, or days sales outstanding stuck between 80 and 93
  • The VA re-bid delayed without a clear outcome
  • Buybacks resumed at a large scale before leverage is below 2.2x
  • A second acquisition of more than $300 million

Red

  • First-half free cash flow below $100 million, or the collected receivables not showing up in operating cash flow
  • The main VA contract lost, or re-priced at a lower margin
  • Adjusted EBITDA guidance cut below $690 million, or net debt to EBITDA above 3x
  • A refinancing at a much higher spread, or a dividend cut

The next review point is the fiscal 2027 first-quarter report, expected in early February 2027, with the first test of whether the delayed cash has arrived. Better results raise the Base only if they lift owner free cash flow, since the exit multiple stays at 7.0 times.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. The VA medical exam work is lost or re-priced sharply and Agile Defense is too small to replace it, which is the Bear path.
  2. The delayed cash does not arrive, so free cash flow stays far below the $700 million guide while leverage is near 2.5 times.
