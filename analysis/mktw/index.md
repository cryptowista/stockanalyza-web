MarketWise sold $91.2 million of new subscriptions in the second quarter, up 57% from a year earlier. It reported $75.8 million of GAAP revenue, down 5%. The gap is deferred revenue: cash comes in when someone subscribes, but accounting revenue trickles out over the life of the subscription. Billings lead; revenue lags. If you only read the revenue line, you'd think the business was shrinking. It isn't.

MarketWise publishes financial research, analysis and software for self-directed investors under a stable of consumer-facing brands, sold as digital subscriptions. It's organized as an "Up-C": the public Class A shares are only a small slice of the company's economics, with most of the ownership held as Class B shares tied to units of the operating LLC. Understanding that structure isn't optional here; it's the difference between a reasonable valuation and a wildly wrong one.

The shares would need to fall about 20.7% to get there. Until then this is a hold: the operating recovery is real and the balance sheet is clean, but the stock has already re-rated a good part of the way there.

## What MarketWise is, and how it's owned

MarketWise sells subscriptions to independent financial research, market commentary, courses and software across a portfolio of brands aimed at self-directed retail investors. It had 399,866 paid subscribers and 2.1 million active free subscribers at June 30, 2026. Since roughly mid-2024, the company has deliberately shifted toward fewer, higher-value products and customers: paid subscriber count is still below its pandemic-era peak, but average revenue per user (ARPU) reached $822 in the second quarter, up 73% year over year and the fifth straight quarterly increase.

The capital structure matters as much as the operating story. At June 30, 2026, MarketWise had 2,666,908 Class A shares (the publicly traded stock) and 12,986,774 Class B shares, tied one-for-one to common units of the operating subsidiary, MarketWise, LLC, for **15,653,682 total economic shares**. Management itself uses the combined count for market capitalization, and so do I: valuing this company on the 2.7 million Class A shares alone would overstate per-share value by roughly six times and be simply wrong.

Total economic shares fell from about 16.15 million at the end of the first quarter, mostly because of a settlement, not a buyback in the usual sense. On April 21, 2026, MarketWise resolved an arbitration with former CEO Mark Arnold: it paid him $12.2 million in cash, and in exchange MarketWise, LLC redeemed and cancelled 520,867 common units (and the corresponding Class B shares) held by Arnold and an affiliated entity. As part of the same deal, the Arnold parties waived their rights under the company's Tax Receivables Agreement (TRA), a long-dated obligation tied to tax benefits from the original 2021 Up-C conversion. That's a real, one-time use of cash, and it also permanently retired a claim on future TRA payments, both of which are more consequential to a minority shareholder than the label "share repurchase" alone suggests.

## Which numbers matter for MarketWise

The primary metric is normalized owner free cash flow, using the total economic share count of 15.654 million, not GAAP earnings and not billings taken at face value. My estimates for a normalized year are Bear **$38 million**, Base **$52 million** and Bull **$65 million**, or Bear **$2.43**, Base **$3.32** and Bull **$4.15** per economic share. The Base sits close to management's own FY2026 CFFO target of approximately $50 million, which itself represents about 10% growth over 2025.

## Shares and the balance sheet

MarketWise carries $32.9 million of cash and zero debt, a genuinely clean balance sheet for a company that also pays a large dividend. The structure means Class A shareholders receive their return through the quarterly dividend and any buybacks (which work by MarketWise redeeming a corresponding LLC unit, so buybacks and dividends both flow through the same Up-C mechanics), while Class B/LLC unit holders receive distributions directly from the operating company, including tax distributions tied to their share of taxable income. FY2026 tax distributions to unit holders are expected to run near $40 million. None of this changes total economic value per share, but it does mean the $1.80 headline "dividend" isn't simply $1.80 times 15.65 million shares in cash leaving the business; it's one piece of a broader capital-return mechanism across two classes of owners.

## What is the market already pricing in?

At $20.08 and 7.5x the Base multiple, the price implies sustainable normalized free cash flow of about **$42 million**, below both management's roughly $50 million FY2026 CFFO target and my own $52 million Base estimate. So the market isn't pricing collapse, it's applying a real discount to marketing-driven billings growth, churn risk, the structural cash distributions the Up-C requires, and the governance profile of a controlled company. Some of that discount is earned; the question for a prospective buyer is whether all of it still is once the FY2026 numbers are actually delivered.

## How the buy price is set

For a Confidence B score, the framework's base margin of safety is 25% to 35%. Insider and LLC-member concentrated voting control, Up-C tax-distribution obligations, and a management track record that includes both a severe post-pandemic contraction and a recent leadership-related legal settlement push this to the top of that range: **35%**. Applied to the $24.50 Base, the **maximum buy price is $15.93**. The preferred accumulation zone runs from the $14.00 Bear value to $15.93. ## Dividend

The FY2026 Class A dividend target is $1.80 per share, a 9.0% yield at $20.08. It can't be mechanically extended across all 15.65 million economic shares, since Class B/LLC holders receive their return through separate distributions within the Up-C structure rather than the same per-share dividend. Part of the quarterly payment (management has cited roughly $0.20 of the current $0.45) reflects mechanics tied to the company's structure and its tax-distribution obligations, rather than being a pure discretionary dividend in the ordinary sense. The dividend is real and currently well covered by cash flow, but it is Board-discretionary and structurally more complex than a typical corporate payout, which is why I mark it attractive but not simple.

## Reasons to own MKTW

- **Billings acceleration.** Q2 billings up 57% year over year to $91.2 million, the highest quarterly level since 2023.
- **ARPU expansion.** Up 73% year over year to $822, reflecting the deliberate shift toward higher-value products and customers.
- **A debt-free balance sheet** with $32.9 million of cash even after a one-time $12.2 million legal settlement.
- **Strong retention.** Roughly 91% revenue retention in 2025 on the existing subscriber base.
- **A large funnel.** 2.1 million active free subscribers give the company a pool to convert without paying for new customer acquisition from scratch.
- **Aggressive Class A cash returns**, including the $1.80 per-share dividend target and an active buyback authorization.

## What could go wrong?

- **Thesis breaker: marketing spend doesn't convert to durable cash flow.** Billings bought with elevated marketing spend that don't translate into renewing subscribers would leave FCF well below the Base case.
- **Subscriber demand is cycle-sensitive.** Interest in financial research and trading tools tends to track market conditions and volatility.
- **Concentrated control.** Insiders and LLC members hold outsized voting power relative to public Class A shareholders.
- **Up-C complexity.** Tax distributions, the TRA, and the mechanics linking Class A dividends to LLC-level cash flows add a layer most investors don't have to think about in an ordinary corporation.
- **Key-person and content risk.** The brands depend on specific analysts and personalities; the April 2026 settlement with a former CEO is a reminder that leadership disputes carry real cash and governance costs.
- **Dividend policy can change.** The $1.80 target is a Board decision each period, not a contractual commitment.

## Management execution

Management exceeded FY2025 guidance, raised FY2026 billings guidance by 10% to $330 million on the strength of Q2, kept the balance sheet debt-free, and resolved a multi-year arbitration with a former CEO in a way that also permanently retired a TRA claim. Offsetting that: concentrated control remains a structural feature rather than a temporary condition, the company is still recovering from a severe prior contraction, and the settlement itself, while resolved, is a reminder of governance friction in the recent past. **Assessment: 13/20, fair. The next test is converting elevated marketing spend into subscribers who renew at the retention rates already seen elsewhere in the book.**

## Adversarial review

The first draft's core arithmetic reconciled cleanly, and its share-count and cash figures matched the company's own 10-Q filings closely. Checking it against the second-quarter 10-Q and 8-K found one significant fact missing entirely, not an error in the numbers already there.

| What the first draft left out | What the filings add |
| --- | --- |
| The decline in economic share count from ~16.15M to 15.65M was noted but not explained | It was driven by the April 21, 2026 settlement with former CEO Mark Arnold: a $12.2 million cash payment, in exchange for redeeming and cancelling 520,867 common units (and the matching Class B shares), plus the Arnold parties' waiver of their rights under the company's Tax Receivables Agreement |
| "$32.9m cash" was stated without explaining what consumed part of it during the quarter | Confirmed correct, and tied directly to the same settlement: the $12.2 million payment is explicitly called out in the company's own cash commentary for the quarter |
| Dividend mechanics described only as "structurally complex" | Added the specific detail management has given: roughly $0.20 of the current $0.45 quarterly payment reflects mechanics tied to the corporate structure and tax-distribution obligations, not a simple discretionary dividend |

None of this changes the Base value, which stays at $24.50, or the Hold/Watchlist verdict. It does sharpen the governance picture: what looked like an unexplained decline in share count and an unexplained cash outflow both trace back to the same event, a leadership dispute that got resolved on terms favorable enough to also retire a long-dated TRA claim, which is a genuinely positive detail the first draft didn't surface.

The strongest counter-thesis, for the bulls: a debt-free company converting billings growth into real cash flow, with a large free-subscriber funnel and improving retention, trading at 6x Base free cash flow, is priced for far less success than it's currently delivering, and a clean resolution of a legacy legal dispute removes one more overhang. The strongest counter-thesis, for the bears: marketing-funded billings growth is the easiest kind of growth to manufacture and the hardest to sustain, insiders control the votes regardless of what public shareholders think, and a company that just settled a former-CEO arbitration has shown governance friction that a 97.7%-clean earnings release doesn't fully capture. Both are reasonable; the 35% margin of safety exists because this is exactly the kind of setup where the truth could go either way.

## Final verdict: hold at $20.08, watchlist for new money below $15.93

MarketWise's operating recovery, measured the right way through billings and cash flow rather than GAAP revenue, is genuine. So is the discount the market applies for a controlled Up-C structure with a history of severe contraction and a recently settled leadership dispute. The stock has already closed part of that gap; what's left is real but not deep enough to satisfy this framework's margin of safety.
