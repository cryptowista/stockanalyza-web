## What Hornbeck Offshore is

Hornbeck Offshore Services is the company created when Hornbeck and Helix combined. The deal closed on September 1, 2026 and the shares began trading as HOS on September 2. The old Hornbeck equity that traded before its 2020 bankruptcy is a different claim, so there is no clean trading history for this business: 37 sessions at the time of writing.

The company runs three kinds of work that all need ships and crews: marine transportation and specialty services, subsea services and well intervention. Trailing revenue is about 72% oilfield, 20% renewables and other, and 8% defense. Geographically, about 49% is the United States, 24% Brazil and 17% the North Sea. Backlog was about $2.0 billion at the second quarter. In September it won a two-year firm decommissioning contract in the North Sea, due to start in the second quarter of 2027, with extension options. The price and margin are not disclosed, so I do not add a separate value for it.

Management's synergy ambition is $75 million a year within three years. That figure mixes extra revenue with cost savings and is not a promise of $75 million of cash. It also comes with a chief executive award that pays for gross synergies, which is a reason to watch what reaches the cash flow statement.

## What the $550 million EBITDA is made of

Management reports adjusted EBITDA of $550.6 million for the twelve months to June 30. I use $516.4 million. The gap of $34.2 million is not a haircut for caution. It comes from two definitions: I keep stock-based compensation as a cost (SBC, shares paid to employees, which is a real cost to owners), and I leave out interest income, which is not operating profit.

The same discipline matters for cash. Summing the two legacy companies, operating cash flow was $387.0 million and all capital spending $120.8 million, which leaves $266.2 million. That number is flattering for three reasons. Drydocking, the periodic dry-dock overhaul every vessel needs, cost $102.4 million and is already inside operating cash flow. Lease principal ($3.5 million) and a stock compensation cost of about $14.5 million (my estimate) still have to come out, which leaves $248.1 million. And Helix's receivables moved from a $71.3 million outflow to a $38.2 million inflow in the first half, so part of the recent jump is timing.

Management's adjusted free cash flow of $289.7 million is a maintenance-focused measure and leaves out some investment. On my static diagnostic, with a 26% cash tax rate on EBITDA after a tax depreciation proxy, free cash flow to the firm (FCFF, the cash left after operating costs, tax and investment but before interest and debt) is $226.1 million. After interest it is $171.6 million and after lease principal $168.1 million. These are my estimates. They show why I treat $267 million as a ceiling, not as the earnings power.

## Primary valuation: three years of cash flow and an exit multiple

The method values the whole business first and then deducts what is owed. I forecast free cash flow for three years ending October 26, 2027, 2028 and 2029, discount at 11% and add a value at the end of year three equal to 5.5 times that year's EBITDA. Base case inputs, all estimates unless stated:

- Cost of capital of 11%: a 4.96% ten-year Treasury yield (Federal Reserve, October 22), an estimated 5% equity premium and a risk loading of 1.5 give a 12.46% cost of equity. A 9.5% debt cost, 26% tax and roughly 75% equity weight give about 11.1%, rounded to 11%. I test 10% to 12%.
- Existing-business EBITDA of $490 million, then $505 million twice: below the $516 million trailing level, because near-term earnings normalize before the new ships arrive.
- Synergies of $10, $25 and $45 million, against the company's $75 million ambition, plus newbuild contributions of $0, $15 and $25 million.
- Drydocking of $105 to $110 million a year and replacement capital spending of $100 to $115 million, about $205 to $225 million of annual upkeep. This is above management's maintenance-only classification and is my estimate of what keeps a fleet continuing.
- Remaining newbuild spending of $45 million and $24.1 million, a further $15 million a year of growth spending, $5 million a year of working capital and $15, $10 and $5 million of integration cash.
- Cash tax at 26% of EBITDA less a tax depreciation proxy of $240 to $250 million. I could not check this against a tax asset register.

This gives **$7.78** a share. The exit value is 83.1% of the total, which is high for a three-year horizon, so the multiple has to be defended. I chose 5.5 times as a capital-heavy service fleet with a moderate, funded recovery, modest returns above the cost of capital and unproven merger delivery. It sits between about 4.4 times paid for the Tidewater WSUT acquisition and about 9 times for Oceaneering, and neither is a formula for HOS. A 5.5 times multiple on $575 million assumes that the business can produce about $285 million of free cash flow in year four. My own cash model produces $226 million. That gap is the heart of the debate, and I keep it visible instead of averaging it away.

## Secondary valuation: free cash flow with fleet renewal built in

The cross-check uses the same three years and the same claims, but replaces the multiple with a year-four cash flow. Terminal growth is 2% and incremental returns on new capital are 12%. Economic depreciation is the cost of renewing ships (drydocking and replacement), kept apart from the tax depreciation proxy. The year-four cash flow is $225.7 million, after $86.2 million of tax, $112.2 million of drydocking and $117.3 million of replacement capital and $45.1 million of growth investment. Divided by 9% (the 11% cost of capital less 2% growth), it gives a year-3 value of $2,507.5 million, or 4.36 times EBITDA, and an equity value of $2,071.5 million. That is **$6.32** a share.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exit multiple method | 2,781.7 | 231.2 | $7.78 |
| Cash flow method | 2,302.7 | 231.2 | $6.32 |

The two methods are 20.7% apart, just above the 20% mark. The whole $1.46 difference is the terminal value: $3,162.5 million against $2,507.5 million, because interim cash, shares and claims are identical. I do not lower the discount rate to close the gap. The two methods share operating drivers, so their gap is not independent evidence and I did not raise confidence for it. I also did not lower confidence for it, since the whole gap is explained.

## Valuation triangulation

I keep the exit multiple value of $7.78 as the Base because the asset-level replacement and tax schedules are incomplete, and a calibrated multiple is the usual way to value an operating fleet. The cash flow value of $6.32 stays next to it because it tests exactly the missing detail. I do not average the two. Three ranges answer three different questions and none of them is a probability interval:

- **Operating cases:** $3.42 to $11.00 on the exit multiple method.
- **Method range at Base inputs:** $6.32 to $7.78.
- **Selected input and method set:** $4.69 to $8.66, from six combinations of discount rate, shares, cash, replacement spending and multiples (table below).

The comparisons that differ from this report are easy to explain. A quote page shows the market value on issued shares and misses about $751 million of warrants and awards. Management's EBITDA differs by $34.2 million in definition. And the cash presentation differs from the later filing by an unexplained $121.0 million that I could not trace to a transaction, so I did not invent one. I could not find usable analyst target prices for the new company, so none enter this analysis.

## Dividend

HOS has no declared recurring dividend and I found none in the primary filings. The model assumes no dividends and no buybacks. It is not an income stock, and the cash after interest and before debt repayment, $209 million in year three of the Base, is not dividend capacity: year three has to repay about $370 million of principal, mostly the 2029 notes.

## Reasons to own HOS

- **A specialized fleet and service line.** Combining vessels, subsea systems and well intervention makes the company more relevant to offshore customers, and Jones Act status and technical experience are real barriers, though not yet a proven high-return moat.
- **Funded growth.** The remaining $69.1 million of newbuild outfitting is in my numbers, and the contribution starts in late 2027 or early 2028 instead of being treated as free.
- **Cash and manageable maturities.** $638.6 million of cash against $769.1 million of debt, with positive cash through a moderate stress case.
- **A testable synergy plan.** The $75 million ambition and the new North Sea decommissioning award give the next few reports something concrete to confirm or deny.

## What could go wrong?

- **Cash economics below the sustainable case.** Lower utilization with the same crew, charter, drydock and renewal costs reduces EBITDA and cash together, and adjusted free cash flow can overstate what owners keep. Normalized earnings below $400 million on a lasting basis would mean starting over.
- **Concentration and jurisdiction.** Offshore budgets, Brazil and North Sea execution, marine casualties and the ownership rules affect where ships work and who can hold the shares.
- **An unclear balance sheet.** The post-closing cash, the restricted-group cash, the covenant certificates and the decommissioning reimbursement are not yet verified. I could not determine which financial covenant would bind first in the Bear case, and I will not guess.
- **Allocation and dilution.** Integration can eat cash, savings can stay gross, newbuild budgets can rise and the lock-up on shares generally runs to February 28, 2027, so selling pressure can appear without any change in value.

## Management execution

The combined team has no reported quarter yet, so the record is mixed and mostly borrowed from the two predecessors. Management credibility scores **7 of the 14 points I can assess, within a possible range of 7 to 13 out of 20**. Three dimensions, including returns on investment and per-share outcomes, cannot be scored yet and are not counted as zero.

- The cash bridge changed materially between the September presentation and the later filing, by $121.0 million, and nobody has explained it.
- The newbuild enhancement budget is $101.8 million with service starting late 2027 or early 2028. $69.1 million is still to be spent.
- Hornbeck's 2024 refinancing partly funded an equity and award repurchase, and whether that was accretive cannot be established.
- Helix's FY2025 adjusted EBITDA was $238.1 million in the August presentation and $252.1 million under the new definition, a $14.1 million change for the same year.

- An unfunded cash shortfall or cash that cannot be moved
- More than 340 million shares without matching consideration

- An actual covenant breach without a funded cure

Nothing is red today and several items cannot be rated until results arrive. The next review points are the Q3 results and call on November 5 and the lock-up expiry on February 28, 2027. Four things must be shown: closing and opening cash with the $45.9 million fee accrual, shares and warrants on one date, recurring cash separate from drydocking and working capital, and a measurable synergy and newbuild timetable.

## The three most important thesis breakers

1. Sustainable cash after necessary renewal falls below the economic case, instead of merely reflecting working capital timing.
2. Debt maturities, collateral, cash access or decommissioning obligations create an unfunded equity requirement.
3. Integration, allocation or dilution stops operating earnings from reaching value per economic share.
