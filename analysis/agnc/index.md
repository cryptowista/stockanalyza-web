# AGNC lost 17% of its book value in one quarter and kept paying 12 cents a month

AGNC, Nasdaq. Published November 22, 2026. Price is a broker quote of $7.73 on November 22, after the Friday, November 20, 2026 session. I could not confirm the Friday close from a second source: the 52-week low was $7.74 on November 19 and one recent close was $7.89. The fiscal year is the calendar year, so "FY26" is 2026. AGNC is a mortgage real estate investment trust that buys US government-backed (Agency) mortgage bonds with borrowed money. Tangible net book value, or TBV, is its equity per share excluding goodwill. NSDRI is net spread and dollar roll income, the company's own measure of recurring income.

## What AGNC is

AGNC owned an investment portfolio of $94.4 billion at September 30, 2026. $93.8 billion was Agency mortgage bonds and forward contracts to buy them, of which $86.6 billion were 30 year mortgage bonds, plus $0.6 billion of credit-risk transfer and other credit investments. It funds the portfolio mostly with short-term repurchase agreements and hedges the interest rate risk with swaps and other derivatives, so its profit is the spread between what the bonds pay and what funding and hedges cost.

Tangible net book value leverage "at risk" was 7.9 times at quarter end and 7.6 times on average for the quarter. Unencumbered cash and Agency bonds were $6.3 billion. The average yield on the portfolio was 5.21% for the quarter against 4.87% in the prior quarter, and the net interest spread was 1.86%, down from 2.00%.

## Primary valuation: dividends and exit book value

An agency mortgage REIT returns what it earns, so I value the stream of dividends and the book value that remains. I start from the reported book of $7.13 and model 10 years. Income on book is 19.5% in year 1, which is about $1.39 a share, a little below four times the third-quarter NSDRI of $0.37, and fades to 14% by year 4 as the wide spreads on new purchases normalize. Management describes returns of 18% to 20% on newly purchased assets, according to coverage of the call, and I treat that as a starting point and not a permanent level. Dividends equal income, so Base assumes the $1.44 a year dividend settles near $1.39 in year 1 and $1.17 in year 2. Book value then drifts down by 3.5% a year from hedge costs, prepayment premium losses and rate moves that hedges do not fully offset. I discount at 12.0%, my estimate for a lender with 7.9 times leverage and a beta of about 1.4, and I assume the shares trade at 0.95 times book at the end of year 10.

The model gives **$7.08**. Dividends over 10 years contribute $5.55 and the exit value $1.53, which is 22% of the total and below my 75% attention level for a 6 to 10 year horizon. The income path, the drift and the discount rate are my estimates.

## Secondary valuation: justified price to book

The second method asks what price to book a 12% required return justifies. Income on book of 14% less 3.5% of drift gives an economic return of 10.5%. A company that pays out all of its income and does not grow is worth its book value times the economic return divided by the cost of equity, which is 10.5 divided by 12.0, or 0.88 times. On $7.13 that is $6.24. This applies the same inputs as the primary model in closed form, so it is a check on the arithmetic and on the exit multiple, not independent evidence.

## Valuation triangulation

The two methods give $7.08 and $6.24, a divergence of 12.6%, below the 20% warning level. They share the same income, drift and discount assumptions. I weight the dividend model 60% and the closed form 40%, because the first captures the early years of high income. The weighted value is $6.74.

## What is the market already pricing in?

Holding everything else at Base, the price of $7.73 requires a cost of equity of 10.2% instead of 12.0%, or a mark-to-market drift in book value of 1.7% a year instead of 3.5%. The market is therefore paying for either a much lower required return or for book value that stops eroding, which is not what the last five years show. It is also paying 1.08 times a book value that is from September 30.

On a one year view, if income is $1.39 a share, book value drifts down 3.5% and the price stays at 1.08 times book, the shares return about 14.5%, mostly the dividend. This is a model return for information, not a forecast.

## Reasons to own

- The yield is 18.6% and income still covered the dividend by 103% in the weakest quarter of the year.
- Spreads on new purchases are wide: management cites returns of 18% to 20% on new assets, and only a small share of mortgages now has an incentive to refinance, which lowers prepayment risk.
- Liquidity of $6.3 billion and 97.6 million shares issued above book value in the quarter helped the balance sheet through the shock.
- The Bull case, with a drift of 1.5% a year and a 11% cost of equity, is worth $9.06, which is 17% above the price.

## What could go wrong?

- Rates. The Fed raised rates 25 basis points in September, per coverage of the results, and markets were pricing about four more rises by mid-2027. An 82 basis point rise in the 10 year yield, as in the third quarter, took 16.9% off book value.
- Book value erosion. A drift of 5.0% a year instead of 3.5% takes the Base value to $6.10.
- Dividend. If income falls below $0.36 a quarter the company has to choose between a cut and paying out of book value.
- Dilution. New shares at a price near book add little, and new shares below book would reduce it.
- Stale book value. My book value is from September 30, and the November move in rates and the share price suggest it is lower now.

## Management execution

Management added hedges, kept leverage near 7.6 times on average and raised $992 million at an average of $10.17, which is a good price in hindsight. The record on book value is poor over a long period: $15.75 in 2021, $8.88 at the end of 2025 and $7.13 now. The company earned a 22.7% economic return in 2025, which shows how large the swings are in both directions. I would want to see book value, NSDRI and leverage for the fourth quarter.
