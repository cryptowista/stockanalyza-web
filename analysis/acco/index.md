## What ACCO is

ACCO Brands is a Lake Zurich, Illinois maker of school, office and technology accessories. The names you may know: Swingline, Five Star, Mead, Leitz, GBC, Quartet, AT-A-GLANCE and Kensington, plus PowerA gaming accessories. About 75% of 2025 sales came from brands ranked first or second in their categories, and the twelve biggest brands produced about $1.1 billion. The Americas make up 59% of sales and International 41%. About 40% of products come from ACCO's own plants and 60% from suppliers, mostly in Asia, which is why tariffs matter: the 10-K says U.S. tariffs, especially on goods from China and Vietnam, have had a material adverse effect.

The business shrinks in a steady way. Net sales fell from $1,947.6 million in 2022 to $1,524.7 million in 2025, down 21.7%, and comparable sales dropped 9.3% in 2025 alone. Management says enterprise buyers are postponing technology accessory deals because AI budgets come first. What keeps profit alive is cost: a $100 million multi-year savings program that management expects to deliver in full this year.

In January 2026 ACCO bought EPOS, a business audio and video products company, from Demant A/S for a net $1.1 million after cash acquired. Management expects about $80 million of EPOS sales this year and $15 million of cost savings within 18 months. It also says EPOS will be neutral to adjusted earnings per share in 2026, which is a polite way of saying the $36.5 million accounting gain from buying it so cheaply is not the same as profit.

## Cash flow, debt and the covenant

ACCO uses cash in the first half and makes it back in the second. In 2025 free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital spending) was $50.8 million, down from $132.3 million in 2024. For 2026 management guides $75 million to $85 million, after about $24 million of restructuring payments and $15 million of capital spending. That guidance is the most important number here: it is the cash the business keeps after interest, and it decides how fast the debt falls.

Nothing is due until 2029, and $204.8 million is available under the revolver. The pressure points are different. The credit agreement allows a leverage ratio (net debt divided by trailing twelve month adjusted EBITDA) of at most 4.75 times for the first two quarters of 2026 and 4.25 times for the third and fourth. The ratio was 4.3 times at June 30, the seasonal peak. Management guides to 3.7 to 3.9 times at year end, and the third quarter normally brings in cash from back-to-school, so I treat this as a watch item rather than a forecast of trouble. The 2025 amendment also caps 2026 dividends plus buybacks at the greater of $40 million or 1% of total assets.

The larger test is later. The $575 million of 4.25% notes come due in March 2029 and, according to the 10-Q, must be refinanced by September 2028. At an assumed 8.5% (my estimate, not a quote) the extra interest is about $24 million a year, or $0.19 a share after tax. That is 21% of 2026 adjusted earnings per share. I do not put it in the Base, because the date is two years away and the multiples already carry a risk discount, but it is in the stress tests below.

## Primary valuation: EV to EBITDA

For a levered manufacturer the cleanest question is what the whole business is worth, then how much of that belongs to the owners after the lenders. I use net debt of **$802.0 million**, the average of $776.5 million at December 31, 2025 and $827.4 million at June 30, 2026, because ACCO borrows in the first half and repays in the second. Using either end alone moves the answer by about 3%.

The multiple is 7.0 times in the Base. My framework puts struggling or commodity-like businesses with falling sales at 5 to 8 times and stable, branded ones at 8 to 12 times. ACCO has leading brands and a working cost program, but sales are down 22% in three years, debt is 4.3 times EBITDA and the moat does not look wide. That puts it in the upper half of the lower band. I could not verify a peer table or ACCO's own multiple history on the same basis, so the multiple is an assumption and is tested at 6 and 8 times.

## Secondary valuation: free cash flow

The second method asks what the shareholders keep after interest, restructuring and capital spending. I normalize free cash flow at **$75 million**, the low end of 2026 guidance. The five year average from 2022 to 2026 is $87.8 million, but it mixes a $132.3 million peak with a $50.8 million trough, so I do not use it. That is $0.785 per diluted share. My framework gives cyclical, indebted businesses 8 to 10 times and takes off 1 to 2 turns for rising debt, falling returns and dilution above 2%. ACCO shows falling returns and dilution, and net debt ended 2025 above the 2024 level: shares outstanding rose from 90.17 million on March 2 to 92.31 million on July 24, 2026, up 2.4% in under five months. ## How the buy price is set

A Confidence C score carries a base margin of safety of 35%. I add 5 points for leverage (4.3 times EBITDA at June 30 and a covenant that tightens) and 5 points for a structurally shrinking top line. No negative modifier applies. The required MOS is **45%**, and the maximum buy price is $4.94 times 0.55, or **$2.72**. That is 40% below the current price and above the Bear value of $1.93. At $2.72 the equity would be worth about $260 million, and the debt would be 3.1 times the equity, which is still a lot.

## Dividend

ACCO pays $0.075 a quarter, or $0.30 a year, a 6.6% yield at $4.54. The annual rate has been $0.30 in every year from 2022 to 2025. Dividends cost $27.0 million in 2025 and $13.8 million in the first half of 2026, about a third of the $80 million free cash flow midpoint. The 10-K says debt reduction comes first in capital allocation, and there were no buybacks in the first half of 2026 after $15.1 million a year earlier. A dividend at that cost is covered, but the covenant cap on dividends and buybacks (the greater of $40 million or 1% of total assets in 2026) tells you where the lenders would draw the line. Dividends paid to non-U.S. holders face U.S. withholding tax, and the rate depends on your tax treaty and your broker.

## Reasons to own ACCO

- **The price already assumes stable earnings.** Seven times my Base EBITDA, and 5.8 times free cash flow, leaves little room for hope to be priced in.
- **Cash arrives in the second half.** Guidance of $75 million to $85 million of free cash flow is 17% to 20% of the equity value, and every $80 million of debt paid down adds about 18% to the equity.
- **The cost program is working where it matters.** Americas adjusted operating margin reached 21.2% in the second quarter, and management raised sales and earnings guidance in July.
- **EPOS was bought for almost nothing.** A net $1.1 million, about $80 million of sales and a $15 million synergy target, paid in cash.
- **Tariff refunds are not in the numbers.** The company filed $20 million of claims expected in 2026 and $5 million in 2027, and it books them only when receipt is assured. In my test they add about $0.12 a share.

## What could go wrong?

- **Thesis breaker: leverage does not come down.** The ratio was 4.3 times at June 30 against a 4.25 times limit for the second half. If the second half cash flow disappoints, ACCO would need lender consent or equity support, and the shareholders pay for both.
- **Refinancing.** $575 million of 4.25% notes must be refinanced by September 2028. At 8.5% the extra cost is about $0.19 a share after tax, 21% of 2026 adjusted earnings per share (my estimate).
- **Shrinking sales.** Net sales are down 21.7% since 2022 and International comparable sales fell about 9% in the second quarter. Technology accessories are softening as enterprise buyers shift budget to AI.
- **Margin squeeze.** Management expects second half gross and operating margins below last year, because inflation and tariffs arrive before price increases.
- **Earnings quality.** The $36.5 million bargain purchase gain is larger than first half GAAP net income of $33.5 million, and restructuring is added back every year. Counting it as a recurring cost lowers my Base to $3.97.
- **Pension and dilution.** Noncurrent pension obligations were $108.4 million at June 30, ACCO also sits in a union multi-employer plan that is in critical and declining status (contributions are described as not significant), and the share count rose 2.4% between March and July.

## Management execution

Thomas Tedford has been chief executive since 2023, and the record since then is mixed in a specific way. The cost program is delivering, guidance was raised in July, and EPOS was a smart small deal. Against that, sales are still shrinking, debt peaked above 4 times, International leadership changed in 2026 and the pipeline of acquisitions is described as attractive while the leverage covenant tightens. Dividends were kept at $0.30 and buybacks stopped. **Assessment: 11/20, executing on cost, still searching for sales.**

## Final verdict: hold at $4.54

ACCO is a cash-generative owner of real brands that the market has stopped writing off, and the price already reflects that. What the price does not reflect is how little room $802 million of net debt leaves. A small miss in EBITDA or a turn lost in the multiple removes a large part of the equity, while a small beat adds as much.
