## What NerdWallet is

NerdWallet runs a free personal finance site and app where people compare credit cards, loans, insurance and savings products. It is paid when a reader applies for, or is approved for, a partner's product. About 650 employees support it, the gross margin is 93.5%, and the founders hold 53.8% of the votes through a dual-class structure.

The weak point is how readers arrive. Management has pointed to organic search as the reason credit card revenue fell $8.6 million year on year and small-business revenue fell 11%, and it describes its largest auto insurance partner relationship as stabilized, not recovered. I could not find Google's share of traffic, so it stays an unknown that I price through the Bear case and the margin of safety.

## Which cash to value

I value owner cash: operating cash flow minus all capital spending, including capitalized software and intangibles, minus stock pay. The last twelve months look like this.

I do not use it as the base, for four reasons. First, NerdWallet is not a federal cash taxpayer in 2026: it paid $1.8 million of cash tax in the first half against a $10.6 million charge and received $9.5 million of refunds, and management expects normal payments in the second or third quarter of 2027. Second, earnings are falling, and the guide of $65 million to $80 million of GAAP operating income compares with $90.8 million over the last twelve months. Third, the marketing step-up. Fourth, reinvestment: amortization runs about $40 million a year against $17 million of capitalized spending, and the gap is filled by small acquisitions ($24.2 million of cash acquisitions in the last twelve months), so I charge $12 million a year for them.

## Where the value sits

The value after year five is 57% of the Bear, 67% of the Base and 74% of the Bull primary value, all below the 85% level at which I would stop and check a five-year model. Most of the Base value therefore comes from the first five years of cash, and the final value assumes 2% growth, which is no more than inflation.

## The secondary valuation: a multiple of cash earnings

The second method takes adjusted EBITDA minus stock pay and capitalized spending, which is $93.5 million in the Base case, and applies a multiple. I use 5.0 times in the Bear, 6.5 times in the Base and 8.0 times in the Bull, then add net cash of $56.9 million and divide by 64.88 million shares. Base is **$10.24**, Bear $5.46 and Bull $15.61.

A multiple needs a reason and not just a range. These multiples are before tax. After 26% tax, 6.5 times is 8.8 times earnings, which at an 11% required return means a business whose earnings stay flat, about -0.3% a year forever. That is the view I hold for the Base: demand for the product is real, but the search channel and the falling latest quarter argue against paying for growth in this method. The Bear multiple of 5.0 times is 6.8 times after tax, or earnings shrinking about 2.4% a year at 12%. The Bull multiple of 8.0 times is 10.8 times after tax, only +0.7% a year at 10%, and is deliberately modest because the Bull case already carries a much larger earnings base of $165 million. The multiples are my assumptions, and I have no peer table to check them against.

## What the price already assumes

At $10.91 the market value is $708 million and the enterprise value is $651 million: 4.7 times adjusted EBITDA and 10.5 times my normalized owner cash. Put differently, owner cash of $53.3 million, 14% below my $62.0 million, justifies today's price at 5% growth and an 11% discount rate. The market has already priced in the tax normalization and some of the earnings decline. It has not priced in a failure of the search channel, which is what the Bear case describes.

The stock is up 33% in the last month, but it is 19% lower this year and well below its 52-week high of $16.24, so the recent bounce has not erased the decline.

## How the buy price is set

Confidence C calls for a margin of safety of 35% to 50%. I use 45%: 40% as the base for this grade, plus 5 points for concentration in the search channel and a few card and insurance partners. The search risk also sits in the Bear scenario and in the 11% discount rate, and the tax normalization sits in the cash flow, so I do not charge them again here. The maximum buy price is $11.63 times (1 minus 45%) = **$6.40**. At a 35% margin it would be $7.56. It is a decision reference, not an order to place.

## Reasons to own

- **Net cash and a 93.5% gross margin.** The balance sheet carries no real debt, and each extra dollar of revenue is highly profitable if the marketing pays back.
- **Return on capital near 20%.** A marketplace that needs little equipment.
- **Buybacks at modest prices.** The diluted share count fell 14% in a year.
- **Growth in the pipeline.** The third-quarter revenue guide is about +17%, helped by personal loans, deposits and the College Finance acquisition.

These reasons justify research. They do not close the gap between $10.91 and the $6.40 I would want to pay.

## What could go wrong?

- **Organic search.** The channel management blames for the card and small-business declines. Google's share of traffic is not disclosed.
- **Marketing that does not pay back.** The $15 million to $20 million hit from return-based spending is a bet, and the payback comes after 2026.
- **Tax normalization.** My owner cash assumes full tax from 2027. A larger cash tax bill would lower it further.
- **Partner concentration.** The largest auto insurance relationship is stabilized, not recovered, and a few card issuers matter a lot.
- **Control and selling.** Founders hold 53.8% of the votes, and a large holder, Topline, sold about 2.0 million shares in June at $8.95 to $9.27. Short interest is 17.7% of the float.

## Management execution

Management is candid about the weak spots, which I count in its favor, and it has bought back stock well below today's price. The change to return-based marketing lowers near-term profit on purpose, and the same kind of acquisition spending keeps showing up in cash flow. I give management credibility 6 of 10 and capital allocation 6 of 10.

## What I would watch from here

### Supports the thesis

- Card and small-business revenue stabilizing year on year.
- Adjusted EBITDA at or above the top of the guide, $147 million.
- Cash taxes staying low beyond the second half of 2027.

### Watch closely

- Adjusted EBITDA between $131 million and $147 million, as guided.
- Buybacks above $50 million a quarter while the price is above $10.
- Revenue growth below 10% in the quarters after the third.

### Breaks the thesis

- Adjusted EBITDA below $131 million, or marketing spend rising with no revenue response.
- A further step down in organic search traffic or loss of a top partner.
- Cash taxes returning above 20% of operating income earlier than 2027.

The thresholds are my monitoring triggers, not company guidance. The next report is the third-quarter release on November 5, 2026.

## The three most important thesis breakers

- **Search traffic falls again.** Both methods assume the base of revenue survives.
- **Marketing spend does not pay back.** Revenue grows but profit does not, and owner cash falls toward my Bear $42 million.
- **Taxes normalize sooner or higher.** This removes the part of the cash flow that is already priced in my Base.

## Final verdict: hold at $10.91

NerdWallet is a profitable, net-cash marketplace whose headline cash flow overstates what it will earn once taxes return and marketing spending pays for itself. The shares are fairly priced against my Base value, but at 70.5% above the maximum buy price they do not offer the protection I require.

**Verdict: HOLD at $10.91. Triangulated Base value $11.63 (range of the two methods $10.24 to $12.55), Confidence C (14/25), required margin of safety 45%, maximum buy price $6.40. Main thesis breaker: a further decline in organic search traffic.**

For holders this is a review, not an instruction to sell. For new money it means waiting for a price near $6.40 to $7.56, or for evidence that the search channel is stable.
