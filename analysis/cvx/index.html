In the quarter Brent averaged $97, Chevron earned $5.28 a share adjusted and cut net debt by $7.8 billion to $20.7 billion. The company plans its capital on $70 oil, and Brent has since slid to about $92. At $208.37 the shares are 35% above my Base value of $154.40, which assumes oil near $72, and the price needs operating cash flow of about $54 billion a year, against my $44 billion.

My Base value is $154.40, which is 35.0% below the price. The Bear value of $69.72, with oil near $60, is 67% below the price, and the Bull value of $234.87, with oil near $82, is 13% above it. Economic classification: Standard (provisional). Values are present fair value estimates, not 12 month price targets.

What Chevron is

Chevron produces oil and natural gas and refines and sells fuels and chemicals. In the third quarter it produced 3,980 thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day, with U.S. production above 2 million for the fifth quarter in a row, and its U.S. refineries ran at 98% of crude capacity. The 2025 purchase of Hess added production in Guyana and the Bakken. Of that, 2,043 thousand barrels a day came from the U.S. and 1,937 thousand from abroad.

Third-quarter adjusted EPS of $5.28 was below the second quarter's $6.06 as Brent slipped from $104 to $97, and timing effects from derivatives and inventory cut earnings by $1.58 billion in the quarter. International output fell because higher prices reduce Chevron's share of production under foreign contracts and because of Middle East disruption. Reported EPS was $0.22 below adjusted, from a $160 million legal reserve, a $110 million impairment and $24 million of pension settlement costs, plus a $143 million currency loss.

What the oil price is doing

Brent averaged $104 in the second quarter and $97 in the third. By November 18 it traded near $92 and the benchmark was on track for a fall of about 20% for the month, as Gulf exports recovered and U.S. crude stocks rose. The conflict around the Strait of Hormuz is not resolved, so both directions remain possible. Chevron itself plans its capital and shareholder returns on $70 Brent and says it expects to cover its capital spending and dividend below $50 Brent through 2030.

I use my own link between oil and cash flow. In the third quarter of 2025, with Brent near $69, cash flow from operations excluding working capital was $9.9 billion. In the third quarter of 2026, with Brent at $97, it was $16.6 billion. That is about $6.7 billion a quarter for $28 a barrel, or roughly $1 billion a year for each $1 of Brent. Part of that gain is refining margins and higher output, not crude alone, so it is a rough guide.

Cash flow, capital and share count

Cash from operations was $41.2 billion for the nine months against $23.2 billion a year earlier, and capital spending was $12.6 billion, so free cash flow was $28.5 billion. Adjusted free cash flow was $33.8 billion, and it includes a $1 billion loan repayment from Tengizchevroil and asset sale proceeds. Chevron guides to $18 to $19 billion of organic capital spending for 2026.

Total debt was $33.5 billion and cash $12.8 billion at September 30, so net debt was $20.7 billion, down from $34.5 billion at the end of 2025. Minority interests were $5.65 billion. Chevron paid $10.5 billion of dividends and bought back $8.2 billion of stock in the nine months, together $18.7 billion against $28.5 billion of free cash flow. The surplus went to the balance sheet. Shares outstanding were 1,951 million, down from 1,980 million at the end of 2025, and diluted shares averaged 1,962.9 million in the quarter, which is the figure I use.

The release did not give asset retirement obligations, pensions or leases, so the bridge from enterprise value to equity uses net debt and minority interests only, and I test $5 billion of other claims in the robustness table.

Primary valuation: normalized owner cash flow

I assume operating cash flow of $44 billion a year, which I link to Brent near $72. That is a little above the $40 billion that the 2025 relationship gives at $70, because Hess is now in for a full year and Chevron targets $3 billion to $4 billion of structural cost savings by the end of 2026, but it is my estimate and not company guidance. I subtract $18.5 billion of capital spending, the midpoint of the 2026 range, and $0.9 billion for minorities and stock compensation (my estimate). Owner cash flow is $12.53 a share.

I grow it 3% a year for five years, which reflects production growth of 2% to 3% a year and a falling share count, then 2% for years 6 to 10 and 1.5% in the terminal period. I discount at 10% because the cash flow follows a commodity price. The company's own target of more than 10% a year at $70 oil is much higher than my 3%, so my Base is conservative against management's plan.

The model gives $161.68. Terminal value is 46% of it over a ten year horizon, below my 75% attention level.

Secondary valuation: multiple of operating cash flow

The second method applies 7.0 times normalized operating cash flow of $44 billion, an EV of $308 billion, less net debt of $20.7 billion and minority interests of $5.65 billion, divided by 1,962.9 million shares. I use 7.0 times because the balance sheet is strong, production is growing 2% to 3% a year and the dividend has a low breakeven, but 7.0 times is also the upper end of what I consider reasonable for a business whose earnings swing with oil. It implies an owner cash flow yield on equity of 8.7%.

The model gives $143.47.

Valuation triangulation

The two methods give $161.68 and $143.47, a divergence of 11.9%, below the 20% warning level. They share the same normalized cash flow and capital spending, so they are not independent, and an error in the $44 billion affects both. I weight the cash flow model 60% and the multiple 40%, because the multiple is a market convention and the model shows its assumptions. The weighted value is $154.40.

What is the market already pricing in?

The price equals an EV of $435 billion, or 9.9 times my normalized operating cash flow. For both methods to give $208.37, normalized operating cash flow would have to be about $54 billion, which on my rough link is Brent near $82 for years, not for a quarter. The price is 7.5 times the annualized nine-month cash flow excluding working capital of $43.4 billion, which looks cheaper but includes the war-time price. Trailing earnings per share are $13.62 and the price to earnings ratio is 15.3, according to StockAnalysis. The dividend yield is 3.4%.

Reasons to own

  • Net debt fell $7.8 billion in the quarter to $20.7 billion, about 0.4 times annual operating cash flow.
  • The company targets production growth of 2% to 3% a year, capital spending of $18 to $21 billion and a dividend covered below $50 Brent.
  • Return on capital employed was 17.7% in the quarter and 14.5% for the nine months.
  • Shares outstanding fell from 1,980 million to 1,951 million in nine months.

What could go wrong?

  • Oil price. Normalized operating cash flow of $40 billion gives a Base value of $132.92, and Bear-case oil gives $69.72.
  • Quality of the quarter. Nine-month earnings include a period of Brent near $100, and the price has fallen about 20% in November.
  • Middle East. International output fell 5.3% and refined product sales fell 8.5% on supply disruption, per the quarter's summary.
  • Other claims. Asset retirement obligations, pensions and leases were not in the release, and $5 billion of them would lower the secondary value to $140.92.
  • Capital spending. Spending at $21 billion instead of $18.5 billion lowers the Base value to $144.54.

Management execution

Management cut net debt by $7.8 billion in one quarter, held the dividend at $1.78 and kept buybacks at $2.5 billion, the bottom of its range, so it chose to keep cash and not to spend the oil windfall on larger buybacks. It also signed agreements to cut Bakken midstream costs by about 50% and plans $7 billion of Venezuelan joint venture investment over five years. The Venezuelan plan adds a country risk that my model does not value.

What I would watch from here

Green: Brent holding above $85 for several quarters, fourth-quarter operating cash flow above $14 billion with no working capital help, and buybacks raised above $2.5 billion a quarter.

Yellow: Brent below $75, capital spending above $19 billion, or international output below 1.9 million barrels a day.

Red: Brent below $65, net debt rising again, or a dividend that exceeds free cash flow.

The three most important thesis breakers

  1. Oil holding near $82 or higher, which would move normalized cash flow to the $54 billion of my Bull case.
  2. Costs and growth beating plan, so that cash flow grows 10% a year as management targets and not 3%.
  3. A sharp fall below $65 Brent, which would test the dividend and move the model toward the Bear case of $69.72.
