What CGI is

CGI is a Montreal-based IT and business consulting firm with about 94,000 consultants. It builds, integrates and runs software systems, with a "proximity" model that places teams close to clients in nine geographic segments. Managed services, where CGI runs a client's systems under a multi-year contract, produced nearly C$9.1 billion of fiscal 2026 revenue, a little over half of the total. Contracted backlog is C$32.1 billion, 1.9 times annual revenue, and just over C$12 billion of it converts to revenue in the next twelve months, about 72% of fiscal 2026 revenue. Government work was just over 50% of fourth-quarter bookings, up from 40% a year earlier.

The market's concern is the same as for the rest of the sector: AI may cut the hours needed for coding and testing, and AI model makers may sell directly to CGI's clients. Management's reply is that AI is now part of all delivery work, that coding productivity gains run up to 40% to 50% and design and requirements work 15% to 20%, and that clients are asking for faster delivery and adjacent work, not smaller budgets. It also reported a 122% fourth-quarter book-to-bill for managed services that embed AI. These are management claims, and fiscal 2026 organic growth does not yet prove them.

The leadership changed in the middle of the sell-off. On May 12, 2026 the board named Tim Hurlebaus, previously president and chief operating officer, as chief executive, after François Boulanger retired with less than two years in the role. The share price had fallen about 42% over the prior twelve months. The 52-week range is $59.63 to $95.20, so the stock is now about 40% above its low and 12% below its high. Founder Serge Godin is co-chair and Julie Godin is executive chair, and the company has two share classes, with multiple-voting Class B shares alongside the listed Class A shares.

I subtract everything the business has to spend to stand still: property and equipment, intangible assets (mostly software), contract costs, and lease payments, which I treat as an operating cost and so leave lease liabilities out of debt in the primary method. Balance sheet and shares

At September 30, CGI had C$494 million of cash and C$3,324 million of long-term debt including the current portion, plus C$631 million of lease liabilities. Reported net debt, which includes leases and subtracts cash and a small amount of other liquid items, is C$3,423 million, against C$3,451 million a year earlier, and net debt to capitalization is 24.7%. Net debt is about 1.0 times EBITDA (my arithmetic: adjusted EBIT of C$2,706 million plus C$623 million of amortization and depreciation), which is in the comfortable range, and net finance costs of C$123 million are covered about 22 times by adjusted operating profit.

Debt matures gradually: C$300 million within a year, C$1,050 million in one to three years and C$1,993 million in three to five years, none after five. In September CGI repaid US$600 million (C$835 million) of senior notes and issued C$500 million of new notes at 4.195% (due December 2029) and 4.484% (due June 2031). A C$2.5 billion revolving credit line is available, with C$2,496 million unused and tranches maturing in 2029 and 2031. In the fourth quarter CGI repaid C$336 million of net debt, so a leverage trigger for a three-tier cash flow analysis is not met: leverage is about 1.0 times, interest takes under 5% of operating cash flow and maturities are well under 25%.

The shares are where the capital goes. CGI bought back 14.16 million Class A shares for C$1,553 million in fiscal 2026 (about C$110 a share including related tax), after C$1,275 million in fiscal 2025, and paid C$144 million in dividends. Buybacks, dividends and acquisitions together came to C$1,895 million, 99% of free cash flow after leases, and net debt did not rise, so the returns were not debt-funded. The current repurchase program allows up to 18.98 million shares until February 5, 2027, with 11.11 million still available at September 30. Weighted diluted shares fell from 225.5 million to 211.3 million for the year and were 205.5 million in the fourth quarter. After the quarter, 182.6 million Class A and 24.1 million Class B shares were outstanding on November 6, 206.7 million in total, higher than the earnings-per-share count. I could not reconcile that gap of about 1 to 2 million shares, which may be shares held for employee plans, so I test 207 million below.

I use 204.8 million diluted shares, the fourth-quarter weighted count less half of the fourth-quarter repurchases (about 1.5 million shares), which makes the market value C${mcap/1000:.1f} billion. That is an estimate, not a reported number. Buybacks are not added to the cash flows, because repurchases at fair value do not change value per share, and I divide by today's count.

Primary valuation: ten years of owner free cash flow

The primary method discounts ten years of owner free cash flow at 10%, the framework default for an established company, and I test 9% and 11%. Owner free cash flow starts at C$1,766 million, which I treat as the 2026 level, so the first discounted cash flow is a full year away. That is a slight conservatism of about two months.

Base grows owner free cash flow 3.5% a year for five years and 2.5% for the next five, which reaches C$2,098 million in 2031 and C$2,373 million in 2036. Terminal growth is 2.0%. Organic revenue growth is close to zero today and management expects improvement, so 3.5% assumes organic growth of 2% to 3% by 2028, a stable margin near 16%, and no help from acquisitions or buybacks. The terminal value is 12.8 times 2036 owner free cash flow. The present value of the ten years of cash flow is C$61.91 a share and the terminal value adds C$56.97.

I do not subtract net debt in this method. Owner free cash flow is after interest and lease payments, so the debt is already serviced inside the cash flow, and I assume it stays at today's level. Subtracting it again would count the debt twice. The primary value is $83.71 (C$118.87).

Terminal value share: 48% of the primary value, at a ten-year horizon, below the 75% level at which I would check that the terminal value is supported by sustainable flows.

Secondary valuation: five years of cash flow and an exit at 9.5 times operating profit

The cross-check takes the first five years of the same cash flow and values the business at the end of 2031 at a multiple of normalized adjusted operating profit. That profit is C$2,616 million for 2026 and grows 3% a year to C$3,033 million in 2031. At the Base exit of 9.5 times, the enterprise is worth C$28,815 million then. From that I subtract today's net debt including leases of C$3,423 million, held flat, because an operating profit multiple values the business before lease interest. The secondary value is $79.60 (C$113.04).

Why 9.5 times. Today's enterprise value, including leases, is C$27.7 billion, or 10.6 times normalized operating profit. Owner free cash flow plus after-tax interest is about 71% of operating profit, and at a 10% required return a multiple of 9.5 times implies growth of about 2.4% a year forever. That fits a firm with low-single-digit organic growth, a return on invested capital near 14% and some AI risk. Bear uses 6.5 times, which implies about -0.8% growth, in effect a shrinking business, and Bull uses 11.0 times, which implies about 3.3%.

Exit value share: 68% of the secondary value comes from the exit, at a horizon of five years, below the 85% level at which I check that the exit is supported by sustainable flows.

What is the market already pricing in?

Run backward on the Base case, $83.36 needs one of four things: owner free cash flow and operating profit growing 4.0% a year for five years instead of 3.5%, an exit multiple of 10.1 times instead of 9.5 in the cross-check, a discount rate of 9.8% instead of 10% on both methods, or a starting owner free cash flow of C$1,821 million, 3.1% higher than mine. That last figure is close to the C$1,851 million I would use if I did not normalize the deferred tax gain. All four are small changes, so the market is pricing my Base almost exactly, and that is why the verdict is Hold: the price is fair, not cheap.

As a return, buying at $83.36 and holding the Base case for five years, with the cash flows and an exit at 9.5 times operating profit, gives about 8.8% a year in US dollars at a constant exchange rate. That is a little under the 10% discount rate, as expected for a price 2% above Base. The same math gives -6% a year in Bear and 15% in Bull.

On earnings the shares trade at 13.2 times fiscal 2026 adjusted EPS. The growth-adjusted ratio (PEG) is about 1.6 on 8.2% EPS growth, a diagnostic only. It overstates quality, because 6.3 of those 8.2 points come from a shrinking share count and not from profit. The free cash flow growth check points the same way: the 20% per-share jump in fiscal 2026 rests on a year without restructuring and on a deferred tax gain. As a stress test, taking 20% off both the terminal value and the exit value lowers the Base to $71.50, 12.4% below the Base.

How the buy price is set

Confidence C starts at a 42.5% margin of safety. I added nothing for AI disruption, the new chief executive or the dual-class structure, because each is charged elsewhere: AI in the Bear scenario and in the Confidence score for weak forward evidence, stock compensation, deferred tax and recurring integration costs in the owner cash flow, and acquisitions by modeling only organic growth. A 42.5% discount to the $81.66 Base gives a maximum buy price of $46.95. That is 21% below the 52-week low of $59.63, so the stock has not traded there in the past year, and I would be a buyer there only if the thesis were intact. The exchange rate matters for a US-dollar buyer: a 5% stronger Canadian dollar (USD/CAD 1.35) lifts the Base to $85.89 and a 5% weaker one (1.49) lowers it to $77.82.

Dividend and buybacks

CGI raised its quarterly dividend 8.8% to C$0.185 a share on November 10, C$0.74 a year, or about US$0.52, a yield of 0.6%. The record date is November 27 and the payment date December 18. At about 205 million shares the dividend costs roughly C$150 million, 9% of owner free cash flow, so it is safe and small. The real payout is buybacks, which were C$1,553 million in fiscal 2026 at an average price of about C$110, below today's C$118. Management has not said how much it will buy in fiscal 2027, and 11.1 million shares remain under the current authorization.

Reasons to own GIB

  • Visible revenue. Backlog of C$32.1 billion is 1.9 times annual revenue, and just over C$12 billion converts in the next twelve months. Managed services is a little over half of revenue and booked 110% of revenue for the year.
  • A stable margin. Operating margin has stayed between 16.1% and 16.5% in every year from 2021 to 2026 on StockAnalysis's operating income series, which matches CGI's adjusted EBIT in fiscal 2025, including a 16.3% result in a year with weak Germany and U.S. Federal.
  • Cash and per-share returns. Owner free cash flow yields 7.3%, debt is about 1.0 times EBITDA, and buybacks cut the diluted share count 6.3% in a year without adding debt.
  • Government demand. Just over half of fourth-quarter bookings were government work, and U.S. federal bookings were 185% of revenue in the quarter.
  • A fair price. At 13.2 times adjusted earnings and a 7.3% owner free cash flow yield, the market is paying for the cash CGI makes and not for a rebound.

What could go wrong?

  • AI deflation. If clients buy fewer hours and renegotiate rates, revenue and margin fall together. The fiscal 2026 numbers show no proof either way, because organic growth was close to zero.
  • Growth is bought. Acquisitions supplied 2.4 of the fourth quarter's 2.5 points of constant-currency growth. Buying growth costs integration money that adjusted profit leaves out (C$104 million in fiscal 2026).
  • Germany and U.S. Federal. Germany's adjusted EBIT fell 20.6% and U.S. Federal's fell 10.8%. Together they are about 18% of revenue, and management expects the federal business to keep rebounding, but its margin fell in fiscal 2026.
  • Profit growth is thin. Adjusted net earnings grew 1.4%. Earnings per share growth of 8.2% depends on buying back about 6% of the shares every year.
  • Cash flow flattered once. Cash taxes ran about C$85 million below the tax charge in fiscal 2026, and the prior year carried restructuring, so the 12% jump in free cash flow is not a trend.
  • Control and a new chief executive. Multiple-voting Class B shares sit alongside the listed Class A shares, and the chief executive changed in May after less than two years of the previous one.
  • Exchange rate. I value the business in Canadian dollars, and a US-dollar buyer carries the currency. A 5% move in USD/CAD moves my values by about 5%.

Management execution

CGI does not issue formal guidance, so there are no targets to grade against, and that is not a penalty. What the numbers show is a company that has held its operating margin between 16.1% and 16.5% for six years, lifted return on invested capital from 13.6% to 13.9% in fiscal 2026 and shrunk its diluted share count 6.3% in a year while holding net debt flat. In September it repaid US$600 million of notes and issued C$500 million of new ones. Against that, organic growth has been near zero, fiscal 2026 margin slipped 10 basis points, Germany and U.S. Federal weighed on profit, and the board replaced the chief executive in May. Management called fiscal 2027 expectations high and the path a slog, which is a candid description. Management credibility is 14/20: reliable on margin, capital allocation and balance sheet, weaker on organic growth and on forecasting the two weak segments.
