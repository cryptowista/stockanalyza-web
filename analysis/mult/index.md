# Multitude: priced for an 11% return on equity

MULT, Xetra. Published October 13, 2026. Price as of the 4:10 pm CEST Xetra quote on October 13, 2026. Values in euros.

My Base path is 13% to 14%, so the shares look 17% cheap. They do not look cheap enough for a high-yield lender with a controlling founder, a shrinking core revenue line and 11% capital costs. ## What Multitude is

Multitude is the company that used to be called Ferratum. Jorma Jokela founded it in Helsinki in 2005 to make small online loans, it listed in Frankfurt in February 2015, moved its seat to Malta in June 2024 and to Switzerland in December 2024. The listing stayed on Xetra, in the Prime Standard. Revenue was €257 million in 2025, it has more than 700 employees and it lends in 17 European countries. Jokela still owns about 55.4% of the shares.

The group has three units, all funded through Multitude Bank, which holds a Maltese banking licence. Consumer Banking (the Ferratum brand) makes unsecured consumer loans and credit lines and earns most of the profit. SME Banking (CapitalBox) lends to small businesses and is losing money. Wholesale Banking lends secured money to other lenders and payment firms, and it is the part that is growing: revenue up 64.5% in the first half of 2026. In May 2026 Multitude also bought the 80% of Sortter, a Finnish loan comparison platform, that it did not already own, for €7.0 million in cash.

The business model is simple to describe and hard to run. Multitude takes deposits at low rates across Europe, lends them at high rates to borrowers that mainstream banks often decline, and accepts that a large share of those loans will not be repaid. In the first half of 2026 it booked €34.9 million of impairments on €979.5 million of loans and investments. The profit is what is left between those two lines.

## How much of the profit is repeatable

The €8.7 million Q2 net profit beat every forecast I could find, but not all of it will come back. Other income included about €2.5 million from earn-outs on the 2025 divestments and the revaluation of the old 20% Sortter stake when Multitude took control. One broker puts organic adjusted earnings before tax at €6.6 million, against €10.0 million reported. After tax, I treat about **€3.0 million** of the quarter as one-off (my estimate).

Then the guidance arithmetic. The company needs €16.9 million of net profit in the second half to reach €30 million, 29% more than in the first half. It is plausible: Sortter will contribute a full six months (management expects about €5 million of annual net profit from it), and there may be more year-end earn-outs. On my count, the clean Q2 run rate plus the rest of Sortter gives about €25.8 million a year of net profit before any growth. So €30 million is reachable, but some of it will be the same kind of income I just took out.

One more deduction matters for shareholders. The perpetual notes count as equity under IFRS, so their interest never touches the income statement, but it comes out of earnings per share. In 2026 that is about €7.6 million a year (my estimate, at a 2% Euribor), up from about €3.5 million in 2025 on the older €45 million of notes. That is why 2026 earnings per share will barely grow even if net profit rises 13%.

## Capital, funding and shares

Total equity of €243.1 million is not all owned by shareholders. About €87.5 million of it is perpetual capital (my figure, the difference between reported total equity and the €155.6 million of common equity in the data provider's balance sheet), which includes the March notes. The notes pay 3-month Euribor plus 8.90%, the issuer may defer interest, the first call is March 23, 2031, and if they are not called the rate steps up to 13.40%. Multitude Bank also announced €25 million of Tier 2 notes in March 2025 at 3-month Euribor plus 11.0%, and the group has €100 million of senior bonds due in 2028.

Common equity of €155.6 million includes €51.0 million of goodwill and intangible assets, up from €35.7 million in March because of Sortter. Tangible common equity is therefore €104.5 million, or €4.88 per outstanding share. The price is 0.78 times common book value on outstanding shares, 0.82 times on the 22.49 million diluted shares the company used for earnings per share in Q2, and 1.16 times tangible book value.

Funding is not the weak spot. Deposits of €1.2 billion exceed loans and investments of €979.5 million, and cash plus short-term investments were €525.7 million on June 30. Multitude Bank reported a common equity tier 1 (CET1) ratio of 17.96% and a total capital ratio of 23.45% at the end of June (company figures, bank level, not the group). The bond covenant requires a group net equity ratio of at least 18%, against 24.0% now, and caps dividends at 50% of net profit while that ratio is below 25%.

There are 21,723,960 shares in issue, 311,820 of them held in treasury, so 21,412,140 are outstanding. The diluted count was about 1.1 million higher in the first half of 2026, which is why I value the shares on 22.49 million. The founder's 55.4% means the free float is small and the average daily volume is about 8,500 shares, roughly €48,000.

## Primary valuation: residual income

For a bank, cash flow is the raw material, not the output, so I value the equity directly. Residual income says the shares are worth today's common book value plus the present value of every euro earned above the cost of equity. If the bank earns exactly its cost of equity, it is worth its book value and nothing more.

Base case inputs, all my estimates:

- Return on beginning common equity of 13.0% in year 1, 13.5% in year 2 and 14.0% in year 3, fading back to 13.0% by year 5 and 12.5% after that. Year 1 is about €20.2 million of profit to shareholders, between the clean run rate (11.6%) and the company's plan.
- Cost of equity of 13%. That is high for a bank on purpose: unsecured high-yield lending, a controlling shareholder, €48,000 of daily trading and capital that already costs 11% to 13% further up the balance sheet.
- Half of earnings paid out, half retained.
- Terminal growth of 2%.
- Starting common equity of €155.6 million at June 30, 2026, after perpetual capital, on 22.49 million diluted shares.

## Secondary valuation: forward earnings

The cross-check is a plain earnings multiple on the same next-twelve-month profit: €20.2 million to shareholders, or €0.90 per diluted share. At 7 times that is **€6.29**. The 7 times is my estimate. A bank that earns about its cost of equity is worth about book value, and book value divided by earnings at a 13% return gives 7.7 times. I take a little off for the controlling shareholder and the illiquidity. I could not verify a peer table for Nordic and Baltic high-yield lenders on the same period and accounting basis, so the multiple is tested at 5 and 9 times below.

For the scenarios the same method uses €0.69 at 5 times in the Bear (a 10% return on equity) and €1.04 at 9 times in the Bull (15%). That gives €3.46 and €9.34.

## Price to book history and growth diagnostics

Multitude's own history is short on reliable data, so I use it only as a cross-check. On the data provider's common equity, the shares traded at about 0.74 times book at the end of September 2024, 0.67 times at the end of 2024 and 0.82 times at the end of 2025 (my arithmetic, using approximate month-end prices read from the price chart). Today's 0.78 times on outstanding shares is in the middle of that range. History says the market has never paid book value for this business, and today is no exception. Applied to the €6.92 diluted book value, that range gives €4.64 to €5.67. I do not weight it.

The growth-adjusted check (PEG) is a diagnostic only. The price is 6.3 times my next-year earnings per share and 5.2 times 2025 earnings per share of €1.08. Earnings per share are set to fall in 2026 because of the higher perpetual note interest, so a PEG ratio is not meaningful. Growth durability: normalizing, with shrinking consumer revenue offset by lower credit losses, Wholesale Banking and Sortter fees. Cyclical distortion: medium, for a real reason. Unsecured consumer credit losses move with unemployment, and Multitude's are at a multi-year low partly because the riskiest micro loans were sold. A free cash flow growth check does not apply to a bank.

## What is the market already pricing in?

At €5.66 and 22.49 million diluted shares, the market value is **€127.3 million**, or 0.82 times common book value of €155.6 million. In a bank, price to book equals (return on equity minus growth) divided by (cost of equity minus growth). With 2% growth and a 13% cost of equity, 0.82 times means a permanent return on equity of **11.0%**. On outstanding shares it is 10.6%. At a 12% cost of equity it is 10.2%, at 14% it is 11.8%.

Multitude earned 14.8% on average common equity in 2025 and about 12% annualized in the first half of 2026 including the one-offs. Without them, the first half was closer to 8%, because Q1 was weak and the new note interest started in March. So the market is not betting on a collapse. It prices in the first half of 2026 being closer to the truth than 2025. My Base says the second half will look better than the first. The company says much better.

## How the buy price is set

A Confidence C score carries a base margin of safety of 35% to 50%, and I start at 35%. I add 5 points for credit cyclicality (unsecured consumer loans with losses near 7% of the book in a good year), 5 points for financial leverage (common equity is about 10% of total assets, and the next layer of capital costs 11% to 13%) and 5 points for opacity: part of the 2026 profit comes from earn-outs and revaluations, and the split between common and perpetual equity is my reconstruction, not a reported figure. No positive modifier applies. The required MOS is **50%**, and the maximum buy price is €6.81 times 0.50, or **€3.41**.

That is below the Bear value of €4.04. The margin of safety is meant to cover the case where my scenarios are wrong, not only the case where the Bear arrives, and for a lender with this profile the scenarios can be wrong in a hurry. At €3.41 the stock would trade at 0.49 times common book value and a little over 0.7 times tangible book.

## Dividend

Multitude paid €0.55 a share on May 4, 2026 for 2025, €11.8 million in total, 44% of net profit and about 51% of earnings per share. At €5.66 that is a 9.7% yield. It came out of retained profit, not out of capital contribution reserves, so Switzerland withholds 35% at source. How much of that you can reclaim depends on your country's tax treaty with Switzerland and on your broker. Before any reclaim, the cash yield is 6.3%.

The 2025 payout was at the top of the company's 25% to 50% policy range, and one broker that had expected €0.31 called it generous for a company that had just raised expensive capital. My Base pays out half of profit to shareholders, which gives €0.45 for year 1, so I do not assume €0.55 repeats for 2026 automatically. The covenant cap of 50% of net profit would allow about €0.70 on €30 million.

## Reasons to own MULT

- **The market prices a lower return than the bank has earned.** 0.82 times book implies about 11% on equity. The return was 14.8% in 2025, and my Base is 13% to 14%.
- **Wholesale Banking is working.** Revenue up 64.5% in the first half, earnings before tax up from €0.4 million to €3.5 million, and loans up 37.0% to €271.3 million. It is secured lending, a better kind of growth than more consumer loans.
- **Sortter was cheap.** €7.0 million for 80% of a platform that management says runs at about €25 million of revenue and €5 million of profit. It also adds fee income that does not depend on Multitude's own balance sheet.
- **Liquidity is ample.** Deposits exceed loans by about €220 million, and Multitude Bank reports a 17.96% CET1 ratio.
- **Management hits its numbers.** 2025 net profit of €26.6 million beat the €24 million to €26 million guidance, and the finance chief says the group has met or beaten its capital market guidance in each of the last five years.
- **A stake that is easy to miss.** Multitude Bank bought a stake in the Norwegian Lea Bank in 2024, 9.9% at first with a further 8.7% agreed subject to approval. One broker values it at about a quarter of Multitude's market value. I could not verify its carrying value, so it is not in my Base.

## What could go wrong?

- **Thesis breaker: regulation cuts consumer yields.** The revised EU Consumer Credit Directive has to be applied from November 2026, and Sweden has already capped consumer loan rates. Consumer Banking revenue is down 12.2% this year. If rate caps spread, the most profitable unit shrinks faster than Wholesale Banking grows.
- **Credit losses.** Impairments were 6.8% of the book on an annualized basis in Q2, the lowest in years. In a recession unsecured consumer losses can rise by several points within a few quarters, and each point on the €537.4 million consumer book is roughly €5.4 million of profit before tax.
- **Expensive capital.** The perpetual notes cost about 11% and step up to 13.40% if not called in 2031. The Tier 2 notes cost about 13%. If the return on common equity falls toward 10%, the layers above shareholders earn more than the shareholders do.
- **Earnings quality.** Q2 profit included about €3.0 million after tax of earn-outs and revaluation gains, and the €30 million target may again rely on similar items at year end.
- **Control and liquidity.** The founder's 55.4% carries any ordinary shareholder vote on its own, and trading is about €48,000 a day. The company has moved its registered seat twice in six months, which also moved minority shareholders into Swiss law and Swiss withholding tax.
- **SME Banking.** The loss widened to €3.5 million in the first half, and the target for profitability has slipped to 2027 at the latest.
- **Conduct history.** Ferratum settled responsible lending claims in New Zealand in 2020, and in 2021 Australia's regulator said Ferratum Australia had charged illegal fees. Those units are gone, but this is a business that regulators watch closely.

## Management execution

Antti Kumpulainen is chief executive and Jorma Jokela, the founder and majority owner, sits on the board, chaired by Ari Tiukkanen. The delivery record is good: 2025 profit rose 31.7% and beat guidance, the micro loan exit was carried through, and Sortter was bought at a low price under an option agreed in 2023. The capital record is mixed. The group refinanced €45 million of perpetual notes into €70 million at Euribor plus 8.90% and then paid out a dividend at the top of its policy range, and the SME Banking turnaround is running late. **Assessment: 12/20, reliable on its own targets, expensive in how it funds them.**

## Final verdict: hold at €5.66

Multitude is a profitable, liquid, well-capitalized specialist lender that the market prices at a discount to book, and the discount is not irrational. It reflects returns that barely clear the cost of capital, a founder who controls the company, a core revenue line that is shrinking under regulation, and a balance sheet that pays 11% to 13% for the capital sitting just above the shareholders.

**Verdict: HOLD at €5.66, watchlist for new money. Triangulated Base value €6.81, upside 20.3%, Confidence C (13/25), required margin of safety 50%, maximum buy price €3.41.**

The main risk to this verdict is a second half that delivers the €16.9 million management needs without help from one-offs, which would put the return on equity near 14% and move the value toward €7.71. Until that shows up, the shareholders are being priced like the bondholders, without the bondholders' place in line.
